\begin{quote}
\begin{flushright}
\emph{The map is not the territory.} \\
Alfred Korzybski
\end{flushright}
\end{quote}
\bigskip

Scientific inquiry is typically organized around research entities that serve as the objects of investigation. In the theory of nescience, these entities are collected into a set $\mathcal{E}$ whose elements may be physical, abstract, or even hypothetical. Within this framework, representations provide a means to reconstruct, compare, and combine such entities. However, this task is inherently challenging: entities may possess infinite structure, be empirically inaccessible, or remain only partially understood.

To address this difficulty, we decompose the representation problem into two complementary components. The first concerns the representation of entities. In particular, we study the conditions under which a finite string $r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ represents an entity $e \in \mathcal{E}$, the circumstances under which the entity can be reconstructed from such a string, and the relations that may exist between distinct representations of the same entity. These issues constitute the subject of the present chapter. The second component concerns the description of representations. In this case, the objective is to extract from a given representation a concise model capable of supporting human reasoning and prediction. This complementary problem will be examined in Chapter \ref{chap:Descriptions}.

The analysis developed here relies on the notion of a \emph{representation oracle}. The oracle establishes the semantic relation between strings and entities by specifying, through an internal reconstruction function, which strings encode elements of $\mathcal{E}$. This abstraction separates the syntactic domain $\mathcal{B}^\ast$ from the ontological domain $\mathcal{E}$ while making explicit the assumptions required for reconstruction.

Within this framework we study structural properties of representation oracles, including minimality and comparability, and examine the extent to which background knowledge may be internalized by the oracle. These properties help clarify the distinction between information carried explicitly by representations and information supplied implicitly by the oracle.

Finally, we classify representations according to their formal properties. In particular, we distinguish decodable, minimal, admissible, extended and effective representations, and analyze how these categories arise in scientific practice. These categories distinguish theoretical properties of representations from their actual use in scientific practice. They provide a framework for analyzing how representations may be refined, corrected, or replaced as scientific understanding develops. In this way, the theory of nescience places representation at the center of scientific development while clarifying its formal constraints and epistemic limits.

\section{Representation Oracle}

The representation of the entities composing the set $\mathcal{E}$, which may not be effectively definable or enumerable, raises significant challenges (see Section~\ref{sec:scientific_representation}\index{Scientific representation} for an overview of proposed solutions in the scientific literature, and their limitations). This book presents a particular approach to this problem by dividing scientific representation into two closely related subproblems: the encoding of entities by means of representations, and the modeling of these representations by means of descriptions. From this perspective, scientific models do not explain entities directly, but rather explain them through their representations (see Figure~\ref{fig:representationProblem} in the Introduction reproduced here for ease of reference). In this section we focus on encoding; the role of descriptions is addressed in Chapter \ref{chap:Descriptions}.

In Kolmogorov complexity\index{Kolmogorov complexity}, the representation problem is usually approached by assuming that the set $\mathcal{E}$ is well defined and countable, and that a total encoding function $f : \mathcal{E} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ exists, analogous to a Gödel numbering\index{Gödel numbering}. The theory of nescience similarly adopts the idea that entities can be encoded as numbers, or equivalently as finite strings. Our approach, however, departs from this tradition by dropping the requirement that $\mathcal{E}$ be explicitly definable or enumerable.

Rather than postulating an encoding function with domain $\mathcal{E}$, we take as primitive the well-defined set of finite strings and assume the existence of an abstract oracle that internally associates certain strings with entities. More precisely, we introduce an oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ that acts as an abstract decision device, determining whether a string $r$ contains sufficient information to reconstruct an entity.

For the purposes of this book, and without loss of generality, we restrict attention to binary strings as the medium of representation.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:descriptions_topic}
Given a set of entities \(\mathcal{E}\), a \emph{representation oracle}\index{Representation oracle}
is a total decision oracle
\[
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}} : \mathcal{B}^\ast \longrightarrow \{\mathrm{true},\mathrm{false}\}
\]
with the following semantics: there exists an oracle-internal partial \emph{reconstruction function}\index{Reconstruction function}
\[
\delta_{\mathcal{O}} : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}
\]
whose domain coincides with the set of strings accepted by the oracle, that is,
\[
\operatorname{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal{O}})
=
\{\, r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast : \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(r)=\mathrm{true} \,\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\noindent
Equivalently, for every \(r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast\),
\[
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(r)=\mathrm{true} \, \Longleftrightarrow \, \delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r)\ \text{is defined}.
\]
When defined, $\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r)$ yields a unique entity of $\mathcal{E}$ reconstructed from $r$ without any additional information. The function $\delta_{\mathcal{O}}$ is not assumed to be computable or explicitly characterizable, and serves only to fix the semantic interpretation of the oracle.

The oracle $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ is an idealized construct. For most collections of entities, it cannot be realized in practice, and no explicit decoding procedure associated with it can be provided. The role of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ is purely formal: it serves as a reference device for delimiting what representations of entities are in principle obtainable within the framework. Accordingly, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ is treated as a black box and is defined only through its input-output behavior. In this respect, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ is analogous to the oracle in an oracle Turing machine\index{Oracle Turing machine} (see Definition~\ref{def:Oracle-Turing-Machine}).

Within this framework, only elements of $\mathcal{B}^\ast$, that is, finite binary strings, are eligible to serve as representations of entities (see the discussion of the ontology problem\index{Problem of ontology} in Section~\ref{sec:scientific_representation}). Other forms of representation (such as physical models, diagrams, or verbal descriptions) are admissible only insofar as they can be transcribed into binary strings. No distinction is drawn between scientific representations and other types of representations (see the discussion of representational demarcation\index{Representational demarcation} in Section~\ref{sec:scientific_representation}). Finally, it is essential to note that $\delta_{\mathcal O}$ is not required to be total: some binary strings may fail to represent any entity.

In general, the internal oracle need not be unique for a given collection of entities, reflecting the plurality and ambiguity inherent in real-world scientific representation.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:reconstruction_bijection}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a set of entities, let $\delta_\mathcal{O}$ be a reconstruction function for an oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$, and let $\pi : \mathcal{B}^\ast \to \mathcal{B}^\ast$ be a bijection. Define the partial function $\delta_\pi : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}$ by $\delta_\pi(x)=\delta_\mathcal{O}(\pi(x))$ for every $x \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ such that $\delta_\mathcal{O}(\pi(x))$ is defined. Then $\delta_\pi$ is also a reconstruction function for $\mathcal{E}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\pi : \mathcal{B}^\ast \to \mathcal{B}^\ast$ is a total bijection and $\delta_\mathcal{O} : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}$ is a partial function, the composition $\delta_\pi = \delta_\mathcal{O} \circ \pi$ is again a partial function from $\mathcal{B}^\ast$ to $\mathcal{E}$.

More explicitly, for each $x \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$, the value $\delta_\pi(x)$ is defined if and only if $\pi(x)$ lies in the domain of $\delta_\mathcal{O}$. Hence
\[
\operatorname{dom}(\delta_\pi) = \{x \in \mathcal{B}^\ast : \pi(x)\in\operatorname{dom}(\delta_\mathcal{O})\} = \pi^{-1}(\operatorname{dom}(\delta_\mathcal{O})).
\]
Whenever $\delta_\pi(x)$ is defined, we have
\[
\delta_\pi(x)=\delta_\mathcal{O}(\pi(x))\in\mathcal{E},
\]
so \(\delta_\pi\) associates binary strings with entities of \(\mathcal{E}\). Therefore \(\delta_\pi\) is also a valid reconstruction function for \(\mathcal{E}\).
\end{proof}

If $\pi$ is not the identity, then in general $\delta_\pi$ differs from $\delta_\mathcal{O}$, because it decodes strings after systematically transforming them by $\pi$. Thus a given set of entities may admit multiple reconstruction functions.

Rather than deploying individual oracles for each set $\mathcal{E}$, we could have employed a universal decision device. This is a machine $\mathcal{U}_\mathcal{O}$ that, given the encoding of an oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ and a string $r$ as input, computes $\mathcal{U}_\mathcal{O} \left( \langle \mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}, r \rangle \right) = \mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E} \left( r \right)$. Universal machines are particularly applicable to the universal set $\xi$, encompassing all entities (see Section \ref{sec:descriptions_entities}). However, the theory of nescience is explicitly domain-relative: the choice of $\mathcal{E}$ determines which entities are under investigation and the oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ fixes the admissible style in which the entities are encoded. We work with entity sets $\mathcal{E}$ corresponding to different areas of knowledge, each associated with a single oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ that is most suitable to our current knowledge and practical needs.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:description_dna}
Consider the case when the entities under study are animals. Initially, one might use detailed physical descriptions of animals as encodings. In this scenario, the internal reconstruction function would be a hypothetical machine capable of reconstructing the original animal from its description. As our understanding of biology advances, we might instead adopt an alternative encoding based on the animals' DNA\index{DNA}. Both of these encodings serve as representations of the entities.
\end{example}

As illustrated in Proposition \ref{prop:reconstruction_bijection} and Example \ref{ex:description_dna}, the entities in $\mathcal{E}$ can be encoded in multiple ways (see the problem of style\index{Problem of style} in Section \ref{sec:scientific_representation}). Different oracles accommodate different encoding schemes. In practical terms, some strings provide more adequate representations of an entity than others (see the standard of accuracy\index{Standard of accuracy} in Section \ref{sec:scientific_representation}). The optimal representation style depends on the type of questions we aim to answer.

\begin{notation}
We denote by $\mathfrak{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ the class of all representation oracles over the set of entities $\mathcal{E}$.
\end{notation}

Representations must not only correspond to entities but must also be relevant to them (see the requirement of directionality\index{Requirement of directionality} below in this section, in Chapter \ref{chap:Miscoding}, and in Section \ref{sec:scientific_representation}).

We employ an oracle function rather than an oracle relation $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{O} \subseteq \mathcal{B}^\ast \times \mathcal{E}$, not merely to associate strings with their corresponding entities, but to reconstruct an entity from its representation. The internal oracle's ability to recover original entities underpins our capacity to make hypotheses about entities based on their representations (see surrogative reasoning\index{Surrogative reasoning} in Section \ref{sec:scientific_representation}). According to the theory of nescience, scientific inquiry involves not only learning how to encode entities properly but also understanding the mechanisms by which internal reconstruction functions decode them. In this sense, the purpose of encoding entities in the theory of nescience differs fundamentally from that in Shannon's information theory, as illustrated in Example \ref{ex:shannon_encoding}.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:shannon_encoding}
Take, for instance, a set $\mathcal{E}$ consisting of two books: "The Ingenious Gentleman Don Quixote of La Mancha" and "The Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet". We might encode the first book with the string "0" and the second with the string "1". While these strings allow us to uniquely identify each book within the set, they do not qualify as valid encodings within the theory of nescience. In information theory, the goal is to uniquely identify an object based on a reference, assuming mutual agreement between the sender and the receiver about the mapping from references to objects. In contrast, the theory of nescience seeks representations that preserve the richness and detail of the original entities. For example, it would be impossible to hypothesize about Cervantes'\index{Cervantes} influence on Shakespeare\index{Shakespeare} using only the strings "0" and "1".
\end{example}

One possible response to the limitation discussed in Example \ref{ex:shannon_encoding}, where entities are encoded using overly simplistic strings that fail to capture their internal structure, would be to require the set $\mathcal{E}$ to be infinite, as is done in Kolmogorov complexity. However, while requiring $\mathcal{E}$ to be infinite prevents all entities from being encoded using uniformly short strings, it does not prevent the use of representations that, although longer, remain arbitrary and devoid of semantic content. That is, they do not allow us to make meaningful inferences about the original entities based solely on their representations. To enable such reasoning, the encoding must preserve essential structural and semantic features of the entities, not just their identity.

\section{Decodable Representations}
\label{sec:decodable_representations}

Given a finite string $r$, the oracle determines whether $r$ contains sufficient information to reconstruct a unique entity without additional input. If so, the oracle internally associates $r$ with that entity; otherwise, the string is deemed non-representational.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}\) be a representation oracle over the set of entities \(\mathcal{E}\), with  reconstruction function
\(\delta_{\mathcal O} : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}\).
We define the set of \emph{decodable representations}\index{Decodable representations} of the oracle as
\[
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{\top}
=
\{\, r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast \mid \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(r)=\mathrm{true} \,\}.
\]
We say the finite binary string $r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ is a \emph{decodable representation}\index{Decodable representation} if $r \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{\top}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Equivalently, we have that
\[
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{\top}
=
\operatorname{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal O}).
\]
Within the theory of nescience, decodable representations constitute the primary formal point of access to entities, since representations are the only mechanism at our disposal for accessing the entities under investigation.

\begin{notation}
When the set of entities $\mathcal{E}$ and the oracle $\mathcal{O}$ are clear from the context, we may write
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}$ or simply $\mathcal{R}^{\top}$.
\end{notation}

The subset $\mathcal{R}^{\top}$ of representations an oracle can decode depends on the reconstruction function implemented by the oracle, and therefore on the representation style associated with that function.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:formal_languages_oracles}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the set of formal languages over a fixed alphabet $\Sigma = \{a,b,c\}$. A representation of a language is a finite string encoding a rule or computational mechanism that generates or recognizes that language. Different representation styles correspond to different classes of computational models, each providing the oracle with distinct reconstruction capabilities.

Consider three representation styles:

A \emph{finite-automaton style}, in which representations encode finite automata\index{Finite automaton}. The oracle reconstructs the language recognized by each automaton. Under this style, the oracle-knowable entities are precisely the \emph{regular languages}\index{Regular languages}.

A \emph{pushdown-automaton style}, in which representations encode nondeterministic pushdown automata\index{Pushdown automaton} or context-free grammars\index{Context-free grammar}. The oracle reconstructs the language recognized by the automaton or generated by the grammar. This style makes all context-free languages knowable, including those that are not regular.

A \emph{Turing-machine style}, in which representations encode Turing machines\index{Turing machine}. The oracle reconstructs the language recognized by each machine. This style makes all recursively enumerable languages knowable, including those that are not context-free.

Each representation style corresponds to a different oracle and determines a different set of oracle-knowable entities. Since the regular languages form a proper subset of the context-free languages, which in turn form a proper subset of the recursively enumerable languages, these three oracles exhibit strictly increasing reconstructive power.
\end{example}

An entity may admit more than one decodable representation under a given oracle. It is therefore useful to isolate, for each entity \(e \in \mathcal{E}\), the subset of decodable representations that reconstruct exactly that entity.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}\) be a representation oracle with reconstruction function
\(\delta_{\mathcal O} : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}\). For any entity \(e \in \mathcal{E}\), we define the set of \emph{decodable representations} of \(e\)\index{Decodable representations} as
\[
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}, e}^{\top} = 
\{\, r \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top} \mid \delta_{\mathcal O}(r)=e \,\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The set \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}, e}^{\top}\) collects all the oracle-accepted strings that have the same semantic target. In this way, it makes explicit that the relation between entities and representations is generally one-to-many: a single entity may be reconstructed from several distinct strings, reflecting redundancy, variation in encoding, or differences in descriptive detail within a fixed representation style.

\begin{notation}
When the oracle is clear from the context, we may write \(\mathcal{R}_{e}^{\top}\) instead of \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}, e}^{\top}\).
\end{notation}

We distinguish between \emph{knowable} and \emph{unknowable} entities (see the gray areas in Figure~\ref{fig:entities_topics_1}; for example, the entity $e_2$ is not encoded by any string). An entity is said to be knowable if the oracle can reconstruct it from at least one decodable representation string. Knowability is therefore always relative to the particular oracle under consideration.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:knowable_entity}
We say that an entity $e \in \mathcal{E}$ is \emph{oracle knowable}\index{Oracle knowable} with respect to the oracle $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ if there exists at least one $r \in \mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{\top}$ such that $\delta_\mathcal{O}(r) = e$. An entity $e \in \mathcal{E}$ is \emph{oracle unknowable}\index{Oracle unknowable} if it is not oracle knowable with respect to $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$.
\end{definition}

An entity that is unknowable with respect to one oracle, for example because that oracle's internal reconstruction mechanism or representation style is too weak, may become knowable with respect to another oracle that adopts a different style or has greater reconstructive capacity.

\begin{example}
Consider Example~\ref{ex:formal_languages_oracles}. The language
\[
L_1 = \{\, a^n b^n \mid n \ge 0 \,\}
\]
cannot be recognized by any finite automaton, and therefore it is not decodable under the finite-automaton representation style. However, it can be generated by a context-free grammar, and thus becomes decodable under the pushdown-automaton style.

Similarly, the language
\[
L_2 = \{\, a^n b^n c^n \mid n \ge 0 \,\}
\]
is not context-free and therefore cannot be decoded under the pushdown-automaton style, but it can be recognized by a Turing machine. Consequently, it becomes decodable under the Turing-machine representation style.
\end{example}

We are interested in the set of all those entities that are knowable, that is, the entities for which there exists at least one representation that decodes the entity.

\begin{definition}
Given a representation oracle $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ over the set of entities $\mathcal{E}$, we define the set of \emph{oracle-knowable entities} by
\[
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top} = \left\{e\in\mathcal{E} \mid
\exists r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}
\text{ such that }
\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r)=e
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The fact that representations are finite binary strings imposes an immediate cardinality constraint on oracle-mediated knowledge. Even if the oracle is idealized and non-computable, it can only decode countably many representations, and therefore can make at most countably many entities knowable.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:countable_oracle_knowable}
For any oracle \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal E}\), the set of oracle-knowable entities \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\) is countable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}
=
\{\delta_{\mathcal O}(r)\mid r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\}.
\]
Since \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^\ast\) and \(\mathcal{B}^\ast\) is countable, the set \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\) is countable. The image of a countable set under a function is countable. Therefore \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\) is countable.
\end{proof}

This limitation has an immediate consequence for sufficiently large domains of inquiry. If the set of entities under consideration is uncountable, then no oracle based on finite representations can make all of them knowable.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:uncountable_entities_unknowable}
If the set of entities \(\mathcal{E}\) is uncountable, then for any oracle \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal E}\),
\[
\mathcal E\setminus \mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\neq\varnothing.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:countable_oracle_knowable}, the set \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\) is countable. Since \(\mathcal E\) is uncountable, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\) cannot exhaust \(\mathcal E\). Hence
\[
\mathcal E\setminus \mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\neq\varnothing.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
\label{ex:unknowability_real_numbers}
Consider the case in which the entities under study are real numbers, so that \(\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{R}\).
Since the set of finite binary strings \(\mathcal{B}^\ast\) is countable, any oracle \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}\) can decode at most countably many representations. Consequently, the set of oracle-knowable real numbers
\[
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}
=
\{\,\delta_{\mathcal O}(r)\mid r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}\,\}
\]
is countable. Because the set of real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable, there must exist real numbers that cannot be reconstructed from any finite binary representation under the oracle.
\end{example}

Example~\ref{ex:unknowability_real_numbers} illustrates a fundamental limitation of representation-based knowledge. In domains where the set of possible entities is uncountable, the space of entities necessarily exceeds the space of finite representations available to us. As a consequence, even an idealized oracle cannot make all entities knowable through finite descriptions.

\section{Minimal Representations}

Once a representation oracle $\mathcal{O}$ and the notion of decodable representation have been defined, it becomes possible to compare different representations of a given entity. In general, an entity $e \in \mathcal{E}$ may admit multiple distinct decodable representations, since different binary strings may contain sufficient information for the oracle to reconstruct the same entity. These representations may differ significantly in length, reflecting the presence of symbols that are not strictly required for reconstruction.

From a theoretical perspective, it is therefore natural to identify those representations that encode an entity in the most economical manner. Intuitively, such representations are those for which no shorter decodable representation of the same entity exists under the oracle: if a shorter representation existed that allowed the oracle to reconstruct the same entity, the longer one would not be minimal. We refer to these encodings as \emph{minimal representations}. They form one of the most stringent classes of representations in the theory of nescience, since they require shortest-length decodability for a given entity under a fixed oracle.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:nonredundant_description}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a set of entities, and $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ be an oracle with reconstruction function $\delta$. We say that the decodable representation $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\top}$ is a \emph{minimal}\index{Minimal representation} representation if there is no decodable representation $s\in\mathcal{R}^{\top}$ such that
\[
l(s) < l(r) \wedge\ \delta(s) = \delta(r)
\]
We denote by $\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}$ the set of minimal representations for $\mathcal{E}$.
\end{definition}

Because the internal reconstruction mechanism of the oracle is abstract and, in general, inaccessible, minimal representations must be understood as theoretical objects defined relative to the oracle's fixed reconstruction function. The set of minimal representations consists of the shortest decodable representations of each oracle-knowable entity. Together with the reconstruction function, this set identifies the entities that the oracle can reconstruct and the shortest strings from which they can be recovered.

\begin{notation}
When the set of entities $\mathcal{E}$ is clear from the context we write $\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}}$, and when the oracle is also clear we simply write $\mathcal{R}^{\star}$.
\end{notation}

An entity may admit multiple minimal representations, each of equal length and decodable by the oracle. It is therefore convenient to consider the collection of all minimal representations that reconstruct the same entity.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a representation oracle with reconstruction function $\delta : \mathcal{B}^\ast \rightharpoonup \mathcal{E}$, and let $e \in \mathcal{E}$ be an entity. We define the \emph{set of minimal representations of $e$} as
\[
\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e} = \bigl\{r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star} \;\big|\; \delta(r)=e \bigr\}.
\]
\end{definition}

In other words, $\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$ is the set of all minimal representations that decode the entity $e$ under the oracle $\mathcal{O}$.

\begin{example}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a set of entities consisting of chemical compounds, and let $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ be a representation oracle whose reconstruction function $\delta$ maps valid molecular encodings to their corresponding molecular structure (e.g., as a labeled graph of atoms and bonds). Assume that the oracle accepts strings written in a non-canonical linear encoding scheme in which any traversal-compatible ordering of atoms is permitted, provided that the resulting string encodes the correct connectivity. In particular, different linearizations of the same molecular graph may yield distinct valid strings. Consider the molecule ethanol, denoted by $e \in \mathcal{E}$. Under such a non-canonical encoding, the same molecule may admit multiple valid representations of equal length. For instance,
\[
r_1 = \texttt{CCO}, \qquad r_2 = \texttt{OCC}.
\]
Both strings are valid encodings under the chosen convention and satisfy
\[
\delta(r_1)=\delta(r_2)=e.
\]
Moreover, they have the same length, reflecting two different admissible linearizations of the same molecular structure. Assuming that no shorter valid encoding exists for this molecule within the given encoding scheme, both $r_1$ and $r_2$ are minimal representations of $e$, that is,
\[
r_1, r_2 \in \mathcal{R}^\star_e.
\]
\end{example} 

Before studying the properties of minimal representations, it is important to establish that such representations always exist for entities that the oracle can decode. Intuitively, if an entity admits at least one decodable representation, then among all such representations there must be one of minimal length. The following proposition formalizes this observation.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a representation oracle and let $e \in \mathcal{E}$ be a oracle-knowable entity. Then there exists at least one minimal representation $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $e$ is decodable, there exists at least one representation $r_0 \in \mathcal{R}^{\top}$ such that $\delta(r_0)=e$. Hence the set
\[
A_e = \{\, l(r) : r \in \mathcal{R}^{\top},\ \delta(r)=e \,\} \subseteq \mathbb{N}
\]
is non-empty. Because $\mathbb{N}$ is well ordered, the set $A_e$ admits a minimum element, say $m$. Choose a representation $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\top}$ such that $\delta(r)=e$ and $l(r)=m$. By definition of $m$, there exists no representation $s \in \mathcal{R}^{\top}$ such that $\delta(s)=e$ and $l(s) < l(r)$. Therefore, $r$ is a minimal representation of $e$, and thus $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$.
\end{proof}

This result shows that every decodable entity admits at least one minimal representation. In general, however, the minimal representation of an entity need not be unique: several distinct strings may achieve the same minimal length while still allowing the oracle to reconstruct the same entity.

\begin{example}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a set of entities consisting of finite undirected graphs with labeled vertices, and let $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ be a representation oracle whose reconstruction function $\delta$ interprets binary strings as adjacency matrices encoded row by row. A string $r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ is decodable if it encodes an integer $n$ (the number of vertices) followed by $n^2$ bits specifying the adjacency matrix. The oracle ignores any additional trailing bits beyond this structure.

Consider the graph $e \in \mathcal{E}$ consisting of two vertices connected by a single edge. The following strings are all decodable representations of $e$:
\[
r_1 = [n=2][0110][0000], \;
r_2 = [n=2][0110][\text{metadata}], \;
r_3 = [n=2][0110]
\]
They satisfy $\delta(r_1)=\delta(r_2)=\delta(r_3)=e$. However, $r_1$ and $r_2$ contain additional symbols that are ignored by the reconstruction function, and therefore play no role in determining the entity. Among these representations, $r_3$ has minimal length.
\end{example}

The sets $\mathcal{R}^\star_{e}$ introduced above collect the minimal representations associated with each individual decodable entity. The next proposition shows that these sets exhaust the whole set of minimal representations: every minimal representation belongs to exactly one such class, namely, the class determined by the entity reconstructed by the oracle.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a set of entities and $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ be a representation oracle with reconstruction function $\delta$. We have that
\[
\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}} = \bigcup_{e \in \mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}}\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First, let $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}}$. Since $r$ is a minimal representation, it is in particular decodable, so $\delta(r)$ is defined. Let $e=\delta(r)$. Then $e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}$ by definition of knowable entity. Moreover, since $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star}$ and $\delta(r)=e$, the definition of $\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$ gives $r \in \mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$. Hence
\[
r\in \bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}}\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e},
\]
and therefore
\[
\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}} \subseteq \bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}}\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}.
\]
Conversely, let
\[
r\in \bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}}\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}.
\]
Then there exists some $e \in \mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}$ such that $r\in\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$. By definition of $\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}$, this means that $r\in\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}}$ and $\delta(r)=e$. In particular, $r\in\mathcal{R}^{\star}$. Therefore
\[
\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}}\mathcal{R}^{\star}_{e}\subseteq \mathcal{R}^{\star}_{\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}}.
\]
\end{proof}

Minimal representations correspond to entities through the reconstruction function of the oracle. It is therefore natural to ask whether a single minimal representation could correspond to more than one entity. The following proposition shows that this cannot occur: the sets of minimal representations associated with different entities are disjoint.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e_1,e_2 \in \mathcal{E}$ with $e_1 \neq e_2$. Then
\[
\mathcal{R}^\star_{e_1} \cap \mathcal{R}^\star_{e_2} = \varnothing .
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a string $r \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{e_1} \cap \mathcal{R}^\star_{e_2}$. By definition of $\mathcal{R}^\star_{e_1}$ we have that $\delta(r)=e_1$, and by definition of $\mathcal{R}^\star_{e_2}$ we have $\delta(r)=e_2$. Since $\delta$ is a function on its domain, it assigns a unique value to each $r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ for which it is defined. Hence $e_1=e_2$, contradicting the assumption that $e_1 \neq e_2$.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that the sets $\mathcal{R}^\star_{e}$ form a collection of pairwise disjoint subsets of $\mathcal{R}^\star$. Consequently, each minimal representation identifies a unique entity under the oracle, and the reconstruction function $\delta$ induces a natural partition of the set of minimal representations according to the entities they encode.

A natural question is whether the notion of minimal representation depends on the particular coding scheme used to write representations as binary strings. The following proposition shows that minimality is invariant under any computable bijection that merely renames representations without altering their lengths. In this sense, minimal representations are stable under effective recodings of fixed size.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ be a representation oracle with reconstruction function $\delta_{\mathcal{O}}$, and let $f:\mathcal{B}^\ast \to \mathcal{B}^\ast$ be a total computable bijection such that $l(f(x)) = l(x)$ for all $x \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$. Define the transported oracle $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}$ by $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}(x) = \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(f^{-1}(x))$, with reconstruction function $\delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(x)
= \delta_{\mathcal{O}}(f^{-1}(x))$ whenever the right-hand side is defined. Then, for every $r \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$,
\[
r \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(r) \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First, observe that decodability is preserved by $f$. By construction $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}(f(r)) = \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}(r)$, and, whenever defined,
\[
\delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(f(r)) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}}(f^{-1}(f(r))) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r).
\]
Thus, $r$ is decodable under $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$ if and only if $f(r)$ is decodable under $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}$, and both reconstruct the same entity. Suppose that $r \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}$. Assume, for contradiction, that $f(r)$ is not minimal under $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}$. Then there exists a decodable representation $t \in \mathcal{B}^\ast$ such that $l(t) < l(f(r))$ and $\delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(t) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(f(r))$. Let $s=f^{-1}(t)$. Since $f$ is a length-preserving bijection,
\[
l(s)=l(t)<l(f(r))=l(r).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(s) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(t) =
\delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(f(r)) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r).
\]
Therefore, $s$ is a shorter decodable representation of the same entity as $r$, contradicting the minimality of $r$. Hence $f(r)$ is minimal under $\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}$.

Conversely, suppose that $f(r) \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}}$. If $r$ were not minimal under $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}$, there would exist a decodable representation $s$ such that $l(s)<l(r)$ and $\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(s)=\delta_{\mathcal{O}}(r)$. By the properties of $f$, the representation $f(s)$ would satisfy $l(f(s))<l(f(r))$ and $\delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(f(s)) = \delta_{\mathcal{O}'}(f(r))$, contradicting the minimality of $f(r)$. Therefore,
\[
r \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(r) \in \mathcal{R}^\star_{\mathcal{O}'_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that minimality is not tied to a particular binary coding, but only to the reconstruction structure induced by the oracle and to the relative lengths of equivalent encodings. In particular, any effective relabeling that preserves length leaves the class of minimal representations unchanged up to the induced bijection.

Finally, it is useful to isolate the smallest amount of algorithmic information that any decodable representation of $e$ must contain. This quantity provides a lower bound on the irreducible descriptive complexity of the entity relative to the decodability criterion.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:representational_complexity_floor}
Let $e \in \mathcal{E}_{\mathcal O}^{\top}$ be an entity and $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ be a representational oracle. We define the \emph{representational complexity floor} of $e$ given the oracle $\mathcal{O}_\mathcal{E}$ as
\[
\kappa_e = \min_{r \in \mathcal{R}_{e}^{\top}} K(r).
\]
\end{definition}

The representational complexity floor measures the least Kolmogorov complexity among all decodable representations of $e$. In this sense, it captures the minimum amount of algorithmic information required to represent $e$.

\section{Admissible Oracles and Knowability}
\label{sec:admissible_oracles}

A representation oracle reconstructs entities by interpreting their representations. In general, the information required for reconstruction may be distributed between the representation and the oracle itself. For example, an oracle containing a dictionary of entities and their identifiers may reconstruct an entity from a short identifier because the information describing that entity is stored internally. Although mathematically valid, such an oracle may not be an appropriate reference when the objective is to determine how much information is conveyed by the representation.

For this reason, we distinguish arbitrary representation oracles from \emph{admissible oracles}. Admissibility concerns the suitability of the oracle's interpretation rules, rather than their computational complexity.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:admissible_oracle}
A representation oracle is said to be \emph{admissible}\index{Admissible oracle} if its reconstruction mechanism satisfies two conceptual requirements: it recognizes a single, coherent encoding style, and it does not incorporate entity-specific information that substitutes for information the representations are intended to convey. An admissible oracle may employ general interpretation rules of arbitrary complexity and may recognize multiple representations of the same entity.
\end{definition}

A single encoding style need not assign a unique representation to each entity. For instance, a structural encoding may permit components to be described in different orders. Conversely, recognizing only one representation per entity does not establish admissibility, since an oracle could reconstruct entities through an internal lookup table.

\begin{example}
Consider representations of chemical compounds. An oracle that reconstructs molecular structures from arbitrary identifiers by consulting an internal dictionary relies on information about individual compounds that is not present in the identifiers. By contrast, an oracle interpreting a structural encoding can reconstruct a compound from a representation specifying its atoms, bonds, and other relevant structural properties. Different valid representations may describe the same compound, yet all can be interpreted using the same general rules. This illustrates the distinction between entity-specific information and general interpretation mechanisms.
\end{example}

Admissibility is a conceptual requirement rather than a mathematically characterized property of the reconstruction function. Its interpretation depends on the entities under consideration and the chosen encoding conventions. In particular, neither the lengths of representations nor the computational complexity of the oracle determine its admissibility.

\subsection*{Knowable and Unknowable Entities}
\label{sec:unknowable_entities}

In Section~\ref{sec:decodable_representations}, we introduced the notion of an \emph{oracle-knowable entity}. An entity is oracle knowable if it can be reconstructed from at least one finite representation by a specified oracle.

We now extend this notion beyond individual oracles. An entity is considered knowable if it can be reconstructed using at least one admissible representation oracle. This excludes arbitrary reconstruction mechanisms that merely retrieve information about particular entities from internal storage, without requiring that information to be conveyed by their representations.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:knowable_entity}
Let $\mathfrak{O}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{adm}}$ denote the collection of admissible representation oracles over $\mathcal{E}$. The set of \emph{knowable entities}\index{Knowable entity} is defined as
\[
\mathcal{E}^{\top} = \bigcup_{\mathcal{O}\in \mathfrak{O}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{adm}}}
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\top}.
\]
The set of \emph{unknowable entities}\index{Unknowable entity} is defined as
\[
\mathcal{E}^{\bot} = \mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{\top}.
\]
\end{definition}

Thus, an entity is knowable if at least one admissible oracle can reconstruct it from a finite representation. An entity is unknowable if no admissible oracle can do so. By construction, $\mathcal{E}^{\top}$ and $\mathcal{E}^{\bot}$ form a partition of $\mathcal{E}$.

Because every representation is a finite binary string, each individual oracle can reconstruct at most countably many distinct entities. Consequently, if the collection of admissible oracles is countable, the set $\mathcal{E}^{\top}$ is also countable. Under this additional assumption, an uncountable set of entities necessarily contains unknowable entities. However, admissibility alone does not imply that the collection of admissible oracles is countable.

The distinction between knowable and unknowable entities must be understood relative to the adopted criteria of admissibility. Because these criteria are conceptually specified, the theory does not claim that knowability is independent of all choices of representation conventions. The sets $\mathcal{E}^{\top}$ and $\mathcal{E}^{\bot}$ are mathematically well-defined once the collection of admissible oracles has been fixed.

Finally, knowability must be distinguished from the knowledge currently available. An entity may be knowable even though no representation of it has yet been discovered. Scientific inquiry may progressively identify new representations and thereby expand established knowledge, without changing the underlying set of knowable entities. Conversely, the failure to discover a representation does not establish that an entity is unknowable.

\section{Admissible Representations}
\label{sec:admissible_representations}

Minimal representations are the shortest strings from which a given representation oracle can reconstruct an entity. They are therefore useful in the theoretical study of representation length and optimality. In scientific practice, however, the shortest representation is seldom the most useful one. Empirical representations may contain repeated measurements, correlated variables, alternative views, or information introduced for error detection and verification. Such redundancy can improve robustness and support statistical estimation without necessarily changing the entity represented.

A different concern is the presence of information that can be removed without affecting reconstruction and that is not recoverable from what remains. Such information may introduce irrelevant patterns into subsequent analysis. The notion of \emph{admissible representation} introduced below provides a deletion-based criterion for excluding certain instances of this kind of extraneous information. It does not require representations to be minimal and does not claim to identify every form of semantic irrelevance.

Admissibility of a representation must be distinguished from admissibility of its oracle, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:admissible_oracles}. An \emph{admissible oracle} is selected according to conceptual requirements concerning its encoding style and the absence of inappropriate entity-specific knowledge. By contrast, the admissibility of a \emph{representation} is defined mathematically relative to a fixed oracle. All definitions and propositions in this section apply to arbitrary representation oracles. For scientific applications, the selected reconstruction oracle should also satisfy the conceptual requirements of oracle admissibility.

Fix a reference universal machine for Kolmogorov complexity and a nonnegative constant threshold $\tau$. The threshold is intended to distinguish informational contributions regarded as nontrivial from small, fixed amounts of information. The resulting classification depends on this choice, as well as on the selected reconstruction oracle.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:admissible_representation}
Let $\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}$ be a representation oracle with reconstruction function $\delta_{\mathcal O}$. A decodable representation $r\in\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O}$ is said to be \emph{admissible}\index{Admissible representation} (at threshold $\tau$) if
\[
\nexists s\sqsubset r\;\Bigl( K(s)>\tau \;\land\; K(s\mid r\setminus s)>\tau \;\land\;
\delta_{\mathcal O}(r\setminus s)=\delta_{\mathcal O}(r) \Bigr).
\]
Here $s\sqsubset r$ denotes a nonempty occurrence of a substring of $r$, and $r\setminus s$ is the string obtained by deleting that occurrence. If the same substring occurs at several positions, each occurrence is considered separately.
\end{definition}

Thus, a representation fails the criterion when some nonempty substring can be deleted while preserving the reconstructed entity, and the deleted substring has both nontrivial Kolmogorov complexity and nontrivial conditional complexity given the remainder. The latter condition means that the deleted information is not recoverable from the remaining string within the chosen threshold; it does not assert that the two strings are statistically or algorithmically independent.

The criterion permits some forms of redundancy. For example, if a representation contains a repeated component or a checksum computable from the remaining data, deleting that component may preserve reconstruction while leaving only a small conditional complexity. Such a deletion does not, by itself, establish inadmissibility. Other deletions must still be considered, and the result depends on the interpretation rules of the oracle.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:admissible_representation_sets}
For a fixed oracle $\mathcal O$, the \emph{set of admissible representations}\index{Admissible representations!set} is
\[
\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O} = \{r\in\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O}:r\text{ is admissible}\}.
\]
For an entity $e\in\mathcal E$, the \emph{set of admissible representations of $e$} is
\[
\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e} = \{r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O}:\delta_{\mathcal O}(r)=e\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The following relationship shows that the admissibility criterion does not exclude any minimal representation.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:minimal_representations_admissible}
For every oracle-knowable entity $e$,
\[
\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e} \subseteq \bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e} \subseteq
\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e}.
\]
In particular, $\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}$ is nonempty.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The second inclusion follows from the definition of admissibility. For the first, let $r\in\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e}$ and suppose that $r$ is not admissible. Then some nonempty occurrence $s\sqsubset r$ satisfies
\[
\delta_{\mathcal O}(r\setminus s)=\delta_{\mathcal O}(r)=e.
\]
Since $s$ is nonempty, $\ell(r\setminus s)<\ell(r)$. Hence $r\setminus s$ is a shorter decodable representation of $e$, contradicting the minimality of $r$. Therefore every minimal representation is admissible. Since every oracle-knowable entity has at least one minimal representation, the admissible set is nonempty.
\end{proof}

The converse does not hold in general. A representation may be longer than a shortest representation of the same entity and still satisfy the admissibility criterion. In particular, semantically removable components whose complexity or conditional complexity does not exceed $\tau$ do not witness inadmissibility. Admissibility therefore allows some redundancy, although it does not guarantee that every redundant encoding is admissible.

Admissibility is also oracle-dependent. To see this, choose a string $x$ satisfying $K(x)>\tau$ and $K(x\mid 1)>\tau$, and let $r=x1$. Consider two oracles over a common entity set containing $e$. Let $\mathcal O_1$ decode exactly $r$ and $1$ as $e$, while $\mathcal O_2$ decodes only $r$ as $e$. Under $\mathcal O_1$, deleting the occurrence $x$ from $r$ leaves $1$, which still decodes to $e$; thus $r$ is inadmissible. Under $\mathcal O_2$, deleting any nonempty substring of $r$ leaves a string outside the domain of its reconstruction function; thus $r$ is admissible. Consequently, admissibility is not a property of the binary string alone.

There is an important limitation to this deletion-based definition. A substring may be semantically irrelevant even though its deletion produces a string that the oracle cannot decode. For instance, suppose an encoding requires strings of exactly $2n$ bits and the oracle reconstructs an entity solely from the first $n$ bits, ignoring the last $n$ bits. The ignored bits may contain substantial information unrelated to the entity, yet deleting them produces a string of an invalid length. Indeed, every nonempty deletion is invalid under this encoding rule, so the representation satisfies the stated admissibility criterion. Accordingly, the absence of a deletion witness should not be interpreted as proof that a representation contains no irrelevant information whatsoever.

For applications to scientific data, admissibility is therefore best understood as an oracle-relative screening criterion for particular kinds of semantically removable information. It can exclude some extraneous content while permitting certain useful redundancies, but it is not a complete semantic test of relevance.

\subsection*{Admissible Representational Complexity Floor}

The representational complexity floor $\kappa_e$, introduced in Definition~\ref{def:representational_complexity_floor}, is the minimum Kolmogorov complexity among all decodable representations of an entity $e$, relative to a fixed oracle. We now define the analogous quantity when the minimization is restricted to admissible representations. This provides an additional reference for the study of deficiency and miscoding in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:admissible_complexity_floor}
Let $e\in\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$ be an oracle-knowable entity and fix a representation oracle $\mathcal O$. The \emph{admissible representational complexity floor}\index{Admissible representational complexity floor} of $e$ is
\[
\bar\kappa_e = \min_{r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}}K(r).
\]
The oracle and threshold $\tau$ are understood to be fixed.
\end{definition}

The minimum is well defined because $\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}$ is nonempty and Kolmogorov complexity takes nonnegative integer values. The following proposition combines the basic bounds relating the admissible floor to unrestricted decodability and minimum representation length.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:complexity_floors_order}
Let $e\in\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$, and let
\[
L_{\mathcal O}(e)
=
\min_{r\in\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e}}\ell(r).
\]
Then
\[
\kappa_e
\leq
\bar\kappa_e
\leq
\min_{r^\star\in\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e}}K(r^\star)
\leq
L_{\mathcal O}(e)+O(1),
\]
where the final bound uses the Kolmogorov-complexity convention adopted in this book.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:minimal_representations_admissible},
\[
\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e}
\subseteq
\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}
\subseteq
\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e}.
\]
Taking minima of $K$ over these nonempty sets, with the order of inclusion reversed, gives
\[
\min_{r\in\mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e}}K(r)
\leq
\min_{r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}}K(r)
\leq
\min_{r^\star\in\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e}}K(r^\star).
\]
The first two minima are $\kappa_e$ and $\bar\kappa_e$, respectively. Finally, every $r^\star\in\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e}$ has length $L_{\mathcal O}(e)$, and the bound $K(r^\star)\leq\ell(r^\star)+O(1)$ yields the final inequality.
\end{proof}

Thus, restricting the minimization to admissible representations cannot lower the representational complexity floor. The two floors coincide if and only if some admissible representation attains the minimum Kolmogorov complexity among all decodable representations of the entity. They need not coincide in general, because minimum string length and minimum Kolmogorov complexity are different optimization criteria.

Like $\kappa_e$, the admissible floor $\bar\kappa_e$ is relative to the selected oracle, and it additionally depends on the threshold $\tau$. It measures a property of the available encodings under those choices, not an oracle-independent complexity of the entity itself.

\section{Extended Representations}
\label{sec:extended_representations}

The sets of admissible representations introduced in Section~\ref{sec:admissible_representations} provide useful reference encodings of entities. Scientific inquiry, however, rarely begins with representations that permit complete reconstruction. Observations, measurements, and hypotheses may be incomplete or inaccurate, and they may contain information unrelated to the entity under investigation. To accommodate such cases, we extend the notion of representation beyond strings that are decodable by a given oracle.

An imperfect string may still share algorithmic information with an admissible representation of an entity. We use this relationship as a criterion for associating strings with entities, without requiring those strings to support reconstruction. As in the preceding sections, the construction is relative to a fixed representation oracle. For scientific applications, that oracle should satisfy the conceptual requirements of admissibility discussed in Section~\ref{sec:admissible_oracles}, but the following definition does not depend on those requirements.

Fix a representation oracle $\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}$, a reference universal machine for Kolmogorov complexity, and a nonnegative threshold $\lambda$. The threshold specifies the minimum reduction in description complexity considered significant for the purpose of this definition.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:representations_of_entity}
Let $e\in\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$ be an oracle-knowable entity. The set of \emph{extended representations}\index{Extended representation} of $e$, relative to $\mathcal O$, is
\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e} = \mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e} \;\cup\; \left\{r'\in\mathcal B^\ast\;\middle|\;
\exists r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e} \text{ such that }K(r)-K(r\mid r')>\lambda \right\}.
\]
For $e\notin\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$, we set $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}=\varnothing$. When the oracle and threshold are fixed and clear from the context, the subscripts may be omitted.
\end{definition}

The first term ensures that every string from which the oracle reconstructs $e$ is a representation of $e$, whether or not the string is admissible. The second term admits strings that may not be decodable, but that reduce the Kolmogorov complexity of at least one admissible representation of $e$ by more than $\lambda$. Such a string may be incomplete, inaccurate, or otherwise unsuitable for reconstruction.

The complexity condition measures information shared with a \emph{reference encoding}, not necessarily information about the entity itself. In particular, an admissible representation may still contain semantically irrelevant information that the deletion-based admissibility test fails to detect. A candidate string could share that information without conveying anything relevant about the entity. Consequently, the definition provides an oracle-relative algorithmic criterion for association, not a complete mathematical characterization of semantic relevance. Membership also depends on the selected universal machine and threshold.

The resulting hierarchy extends the relationships established in the preceding sections.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:extended_representation_inclusions}
For every $e\in\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$,
\[
\mathcal R^\star_{\mathcal O,e} \subseteq \bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e}
\subseteq \mathcal R^\top_{\mathcal O,e} \subseteq \mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two inclusions follow from the definition and basic properties of admissible representations. The last inclusion follows directly from the definition of extended representations, which explicitly includes every decodable representation of $e$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
Consider the orbital motion of Mars as an entity of scientific investigation. Historical astronomical tables, including those developed in the Ptolemaic tradition, and the more precise observations subsequently gathered by Tycho Brahe provide partial and potentially inaccurate information about planetary motion. Such records illustrate the intended role of extended representations: they may contribute to the study of an entity without permitting its complete reconstruction. Establishing their formal membership in $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}$ would nevertheless require a specified oracle, an admissible reference representation, and verification of the complexity condition.
\end{example}

Unlike decodable representations, extended representations need not identify their target uniquely. The same string may share information with admissible representations of several entities, so the sets $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}$ need not be disjoint.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:infectious_agents}
Before viruses and bacteria were clearly distinguished, a broad description such as ``a microscopic infectious agent capable of causing disease'' could convey information relevant to both categories without identifying either one. Depending on the chosen encoding style and reference representations, its binary encoding could qualify as an extended representation of both the category of viruses and the category of bacteria. The example illustrates possible overlap; the wording alone does not establish the required Kolmogorov-complexity inequalities.
\end{example}

It is convenient to collect the extended representations of all entities knowable by the selected oracle.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:set_extended_representations}
For a fixed oracle $\mathcal O_{\mathcal E}$, the \emph{set of extended representations}\index{Representation!extended set} is
\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,\mathcal E} = \bigcup_{e\in\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}} \mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}.
\]
When the oracle and entity set are clear from the context, we write $\mathcal R$.
\end{definition}

The set $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,\mathcal E}$ is a subset of $\mathcal B^\ast$ and is therefore countable. This does not imply that its membership is decidable or that it can be effectively enumerated. The definition involves an oracle's reconstruction function and Kolmogorov complexity, neither of which supplies a general effective membership test. Particular choices of oracle and encoding scheme may nevertheless yield computable or effectively enumerable sets.

Strings outside $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,\mathcal E}$ are not associated with any entity by the adopted criterion. They may be regarded as \emph{targetless candidate representations}\index{Targetless representations} in the sense discussed in Section~\ref{sec:scientific_representation}. Their exclusion does not establish that they contain no information about any entity: they may fail the threshold, or the selected oracle may lack a suitable decodable reference representation.

The extended-representation construction does not associate strings with entities that are not knowable under the selected oracle. This is a limitation of the formal reference-based definition, rather than a claim that no imperfect information about such entities can exist. Within its stated scope, the construction accommodates scientific representations that precede, or fall short of, complete reconstruction, providing a basis for the analysis of miscoding in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

\section{Effective Representations}
\label{sec:effective_representations}

The extended representations introduced in Section~\ref{sec:extended_representations} associate finite strings with entities according to a fixed representation oracle and an algorithmic-information criterion. This theoretical construction does not establish which strings have actually been proposed, accepted, or investigated in scientific practice. Moreover, satisfying the extended-representation criterion does not necessarily guarantee genuine semantic relevance to the intended entity.

Scientific inquiry instead proceeds with candidate representations, which may be incomplete, inaccurate, ambiguous, or entirely mistaken. We call these \emph{effective representations}. Here, \emph{effective} refers to their use or consideration in scientific practice, not to effective computability, successful reconstruction, or scientific validity.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:effective_representations}
Let $\mathcal E$ be a set of entities, and fix a scientific context, such as a research community, discipline, or collection of theories and observations under consideration. For each $e\in\mathcal E$, let
\[
\hat{\mathcal R}_e\subseteq\mathcal B^\ast
\]
denote the set of \emph{effective representations}\index{Effective representation} of $e$: the finite binary strings considered in that context to represent or approximate $e$, whether or not the attribution is correct. The set of effective representations for the domain $\mathcal E$ is
\[
\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}
=\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal E}\hat{\mathcal R}_e.
\]
\end{definition}

When the entity set and scientific context are clear, we write $\hat{\mathcal R}$ for $\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}$. The sets $\hat{\mathcal R}_e$ need not be disjoint: the same string may be attributed to different entities or competing explanatory targets.

Effective representations are not another level in the hierarchy of minimal, admissible, decodable, and extended representations. For a fixed oracle $\mathcal O$, neither
\[
\hat{\mathcal R}_e\subseteq\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}
\qquad\text{nor}\qquad
\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e}\subseteq\hat{\mathcal R}_e
\]
is guaranteed. The first set records candidate attributions made within scientific practice; the second is determined by a theoretical, oracle-relative criterion. An effective representation may fail that criterion, while a theoretical extended representation may never have been proposed or investigated.

These definitions do not introduce time as a mathematical variable. They describe a selected state of scientific inquiry, which may be studied independently of how that state arose or subsequently changed.

\subsection*{Evolution of Effective Representations}

To study the evolution of scientific theories, we may compare effective representations at different stages of inquiry. Let $T$ be a linearly ordered set of time indices and assume that the entity domain $\mathcal E$ and the scientific context being compared remain fixed. For each $t\in T$, let $\hat{\mathcal R}_e^t$ denote the candidate representations of $e$ considered at time $t$. The corresponding domain-wide set is
\[
\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}^t
=\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal E}\hat{\mathcal R}_e^t.
\]
These are time-indexed instances of the preceding timeless definitions, rather than a different notion of effective representation.

A time-indexed set describes the state of inquiry at a particular time; it need not contain every representation considered earlier. Consequently, neither $\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{t_1}\subseteq\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{t_2}$ nor its converse is generally required when $t_1<t_2$. Representations may be introduced, abandoned, or reinterpreted.

When the cumulative history of investigation is of interest, we may instead collect all representations considered up to a given time.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:cumulative_effective_representations}
For $t\in T$, the \emph{cumulative effective representations} of an entity $e$ and of the domain $\mathcal E$ are, respectively,
\[
\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{\leq t}
=\bigcup_{\substack{u\in T\\u\leq t}}\hat{\mathcal R}_e^u,
\qquad
\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}^{\leq t}
=\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal E}\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{\leq t}.
\]
\end{definition}

Unlike the representations considered at a particular time, cumulative effective representations retain previously investigated candidates even after their rejection. They record the historical breadth of inquiry, not the collection of currently accepted explanations.

\subsection*{Effectively Known and Unknown Entities}
\label{subsec:known_unknown_entities}

The availability of effective representations allows us to distinguish entities that have entered scientific consideration from those to which no candidate representation is attributed in the selected context.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:unknown_entity}
An entity $e\in\mathcal E$ is \emph{effectively known}\index{Effectively known} if $\hat{\mathcal R}_e\neq\varnothing$, and \emph{effectively unknown}\index{Effectively unknown} otherwise. The sets of \emph{effectively known entities}\index{Effectively known entities} and \emph{effectively unknown entities}\index{Effectively unknown entities} are
\[
\hat{\mathcal E}
=\{e\in\mathcal E:\hat{\mathcal R}_e\neq\varnothing\},
\qquad
\mathcal U=\mathcal E\setminus\hat{\mathcal E}.
\]
\end{definition}

By construction, $\hat{\mathcal E}$ and $\mathcal U$ partition $\mathcal E$. The expression \emph{effectively known} is used in a limited sense: it means that an entity has at least one candidate representation in the specified context, not that it has been correctly identified, reconstructed, or understood. Likewise, effective unknownness records the absence of a candidate attribution, not the impossibility of representing the entity.

Effective knowledge must not be confused with \emph{knowability}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:admissible_oracles}. Knowability concerns the existence, in principle, of a finite decodable representation under at least one admissible oracle. Effective knowledge concerns the representations attributed to entities in scientific practice. Thus, a knowable entity may be effectively unknown; conversely, the formal definitions permit candidate representations to be assigned to entities that are not knowable under the stipulated admissible schemes. Neither status implies the other.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:luminiferous_ether}
The luminiferous ether\index{Luminiferous ether} was postulated as a medium for the propagation of light. The hypothesis motivated mathematical models and experimental investigations, including the Michelson--Morley experiment\index{Michelson-Morley experiment}. In a scientific context in which this hypothesis is investigated, such models may be treated as effective representations of its intended subject, despite the later rejection of the proposed physical medium.

This history does not establish that the ether is an unknowable entity. An ether model may be representable as an abstract object, whereas the existence of the hypothesized physical medium is a different question. If the domain $\mathcal E$ is restricted to existing physical entities, a nonexistent medium is not thereby an unknowable member of that domain.
\end{example}

The same observational record may also be associated with competing scientific interpretations.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:phlogiston}
The phlogiston theory\index{Phlogiston} sought to explain combustion by postulating a substance released during burning. Observations once interpreted in this framework were later explained through oxygen chemistry; Joseph Priestley's \emph{dephlogisticated air}, for example, was subsequently identified as oxygen.

In a context comparing these interpretations, a recorded string $r$ may be attributed to both the phlogiston hypothesis and an oxygen-based explanation. If both attributions are included, the corresponding sets of effective representations overlap. This expresses competing interpretations of a record, not an identification of phlogiston with oxygen or a claim that $r$ satisfies the theoretical extended-representation criterion for both.
\end{example}

The timeless definitions above can also be applied separately to each stage of an evolving scientific context. In particular, for each $t\in T$, define
\[
\hat{\mathcal E}^{t}
=\{e\in\mathcal E:\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{t}\neq\varnothing\},
\qquad
\mathcal U^{t}=\mathcal E\setminus\hat{\mathcal E}^{t}.
\]
For the cumulative historical record, analogously define
\[
\hat{\mathcal E}^{\leq t}
=\{e\in\mathcal E:\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{\leq t}\neq\varnothing\},
\qquad
\mathcal U^{\leq t}=\mathcal E\setminus\hat{\mathcal E}^{\leq t}.
\]
The instantaneous sets need not evolve monotonically, since a community may cease to consider a candidate or its proposed target. The cumulative sets, by contrast, satisfy a simple monotonicity property.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:effective_representations_monotonicity}
For $t_1,t_2\in T$ with $t_1<t_2$ and every $e\in\mathcal E$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{\leq t_1}&\subseteq\hat{\mathcal R}_e^{\leq t_2},
&\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}^{\leq t_1}&\subseteq\hat{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal E}^{\leq t_2},\\
\hat{\mathcal E}^{\leq t_1}&\subseteq\hat{\mathcal E}^{\leq t_2},
&\mathcal U^{\leq t_2}&\subseteq\mathcal U^{\leq t_1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The set of indices $u\leq t_1$ is contained in the set of indices $u\leq t_2$. Hence the first inclusion follows from the definition of cumulative effective representations, and the second follows by taking unions over $e\in\mathcal E$. The first inclusion implies that an entity represented at some time up to $t_1$ remains historically represented up to $t_2$, giving the third inclusion. Taking complements in the fixed domain $\mathcal E$ yields the fourth.
\end{proof}

This monotonicity follows from retaining past associations, not from a law of scientific progress. The growth of the cumulative sets measures the scope of historical exploration; it does not imply that representations have become more accurate or that nescience has decreased. Comparing the quality of representations or scientific theories at different stages requires additional measures, including those introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

\section{Joint Representations}
\label{sec:joint_representations}

The set of extended representations $\mathcal{R}_e$ of an entity $e\in\mathcal{E}$ contains strings that carry non-trivial algorithmic information about the entity, without necessarily providing sufficient information for its complete reconstruction. Different extended representations may capture different aspects of the same entity, and transformations of these representations may preserve, eliminate, or introduce information relevant to it. This motivates the study of three elementary operations on representations: \emph{augmentation}, which adds information to an existing representation; \emph{reduction}, which removes part of a representation; and \emph{joint representation}, which combines representations of one or several entities. The purpose of this section is to establish how transformations of representations affect the algorithmic information available about the entities under investigation. Their effects on deficiency, surplus, and miscoding will be examined in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

% Subsection: Operation of Augmentation

\subsection*{Operation of Augmentation}

One natural way to enrich a representation is to append symbols that may encode information missing from the original representation. We call this operation \emph{augmentation}\index{Augmentation of representations}. It provides a simple syntactic mechanism for extending representations as additional observations or other information become available. Throughout this subsection, the representation oracle and the threshold used to define extended representations are fixed, and their subscripts are omitted when unambiguous.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be an extended representation of $e$, and let $s\in\mathcal B^\ast$ be an arbitrary binary string. We call the concatenated string $rs$ an \emph{augmentation} of $r$, and $s$ an \emph{augmentation string}\index{Augmentation string}.
\end{definition}

The augmentation string may contain additional information about the original entity, information about other entities, or information unrelated to the entity under investigation. Unlike the operation of joining extended representations, augmentation places no representational requirement on $s$. In particular, the augmented string $rs$ is not guaranteed to belong to $\mathcal R_e$.

Nevertheless, augmentation preserves access to much of the algorithmic information that $r$ provides about an admissible reference representation of $e$. Even when the boundary between $r$ and $s$ is not directly identifiable, the original prefix can be recovered from $rs$ by supplying its length. This yields the following bound.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, let $r\in\mathcal R_e$, and let $s\in\mathcal B^\ast$. Then, for every admissible representation $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)
\leq K(\bar r\mid r)+O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $rs$ and the length $\ell(r)$, a fixed procedure recovers $r$ by extracting the first $\ell(r)$ bits. A self-delimiting description of this length requires $O(\log(\ell(r)+2))$ bits. We can then use a shortest conditional description of $\bar r$ given $r$ to reconstruct $\bar r$. Combining these procedures gives
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)
\leq K(\bar r\mid r)+O(\log(\ell(r)+2)),
\]
as required.
\end{proof}

In particular, if an admissible reference representation $\bar r$ satisfies
\[
K(\bar r\mid r)\leq K(\bar r)-m
\]
for an information gain $m>0$, then
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)
\leq K(\bar r)-m+O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
Thus, the gain relative to the same reference encoding is preserved up to a logarithmic overhead. The overhead is a worst-case upper bound, not an unavoidable loss. This result does not imply that every augmentation remains an extended representation: if $r$ satisfies the algorithmic-information criterion with only a small margin above its threshold, the bound alone may not establish that $rs$ also satisfies it. A sufficiently large margin does establish membership through that criterion. Independently, $rs$ may belong to $\mathcal R_e$ if the oracle decodes it as $e$.

A stronger bound holds when the original representation can be recovered without supplying a description of its length.

\begin{proposition}
Let $g:\mathcal B^\ast\to\mathcal B^\ast$ be a fixed total computable function, chosen independently of the particular representations. Let $e\in\mathcal E$, $r\in\mathcal R_e$, and $s\in\mathcal B^\ast$ satisfy $g(rs)=r$. Then, for every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r\mid r)+O(1),
\]
where the constant may depend on $g$ and the reference universal machine, but not on $r$, $s$, or $\bar r$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Because $g$ is fixed and computable, a fixed procedure can first obtain $g(rs)$ from $rs$ and then execute a shortest conditional description of $\bar r$ given that output. Hence
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)
\leq K(\bar r\mid g(rs))+O(1)
=K(\bar r\mid r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

The hypothesis holds, for example, when a fixed encoding convention makes the boundary between the original representation and its augmentation computably recoverable. A self-delimiting original representation or an explicitly structured record can provide such a convention; ordinary concatenation alone does not.

Augmentation does not guarantee a strict improvement. The additional string may repeat information already available in $r$ or may be irrelevant to the entity. Conversely, when it supplies information missing from the original string, the augmentation may reduce the conditional complexity of an admissible reference encoding.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the motion of a physical system, and let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ contain measurements of its position and velocity over time. Suppose the augmentation string $s$ contains additional measurements of the forces acting on the system. The concatenated string $rs$ combines the original observations with the additional measurements. If the forces provide information relevant to reconstructing an admissible reference representation of the system's motion, the augmentation may reduce the remaining information required for that reconstruction. If the additional measurements are redundant or unrelated to the system, however, they need not improve the representation.
\end{example}

Augmentation may also add information associated with entities other than the original one. Since extended representations need not identify a unique entity, an augmented string may satisfy the representation criterion for several distinct entities.

Thus, augmentation provides a general syntactic mechanism for extending representations. It preserves the original string's algorithmic contribution relative to an admissible reference encoding up to a logarithmic overhead, or up to a constant when a fixed computable recovery procedure is available. Whether the augmentation improves the representation depends on the informational relevance of the appended string.

% Subsection: Operation of Reduction

\subsection*{Operation of Reduction}

An alternative way to transform a representation is to remove symbols that may be unnecessary, irrelevant, or misleading. We call this operation \emph{reduction}\index{Reduction of representations}. Reduction may also eliminate information essential to representing the entity, whereas augmentation retains all the original symbols. Consequently, although neither operation guarantees that the resulting string remains an extended representation of the original entity, augmentation preserves the information provided about an admissible reference encoding up to a logarithmic overhead, while reduction may cause a substantial information loss. Throughout this subsection, the representation oracle and the threshold used to define extended representations are fixed, and their subscripts are omitted when unambiguous.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $r,s\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$ be binary strings such that $rs\in\mathcal{R}_e$. We call the string $r$ a \emph{reduction}\index{Reduced representation} of the representation $rs$, obtained by removing the suffix $s$. The string $s$ is called the \emph{removed substring}.
\end{definition}

Reduction removes a suffix from an extended representation. The remaining string may still contain substantial information about an admissible representation of the original entity, or it may have lost information essential to establishing the representational relationship. The following proposition bounds the increase in conditional complexity caused by the removal.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $r,s\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$ satisfy $rs\in\mathcal{R}_e$. Then, for every admissible representation $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_e$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid r)
\leq K(\bar r\mid rs)+K(s\mid r)
+O\!\left(\log(K(s\mid r)+2)\right).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $r$, a shortest conditional description of $s$ given $r$ can be used to reconstruct $s$, after which a fixed procedure forms $rs$ by concatenation. A shortest conditional description of $\bar r$ given $rs$ then reconstructs $\bar r$. Under the two-part description convention adopted in this book, the first description can be made self-delimiting with an additional $O(\log(K(s\mid r)+2))$ bits. The remaining description can then be read and executed by a fixed procedure. Combining these steps yields
\[
K(\bar r\mid r)
\leq K(s\mid r)+K(\bar r\mid rs)
+O\!\left(\log(K(s\mid r)+2)\right),
\]
as required.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that the increase in the conditional complexity of an admissible reference representation is bounded by the information needed to recover the removed suffix from the remaining string, together with a logarithmic description overhead. This bound may be large when the removed suffix cannot be recovered from the retained string. It may be small when the removed information is predictable from what remains.

\begin{corollary}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $r,s\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$ satisfy $rs\in\mathcal{R}_e$. If $K(s\mid r)=O(1)$, then, for every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_e$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid r)\leq K(\bar r\mid rs)+O(1).
\]
Here the bounded-complexity condition is understood uniformly over the reductions under consideration.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $K(s\mid r)$ is bounded by a fixed constant, then both $K(s\mid r)$ and $\log(K(s\mid r)+2)$ contribute only a constant to the preceding bound.
\end{proof}

The corollary gives a sufficient condition for reduction to preserve information about an admissible reference encoding up to constant overhead. It is not a necessary condition: a removed suffix may be difficult to reconstruct from $r$ yet contain little information relevant to reconstructing $\bar r$. Conversely, reduction may remove substantial information while leaving a string that still qualifies as an extended representation.

\begin{example}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ admit an admissible representation $\bar r=xy$, where $x$ and $y$ are algorithmically independent, incompressible binary strings of length $n$. Consider the extended representation $xy$, and remove the suffix $y$ to obtain the reduced string $x$. Before reduction,
\[
K(xy\mid xy)=O(1).
\]
After reduction, the information needed to reconstruct the admissible representation from $x$ is
\[
K(xy\mid x)=n+O(\log n).
\]
Thus, reduction loses approximately $n$ bits of information about the admissible reference encoding. Nevertheless, under the stated incompressibility and independence assumptions,
\[
K(xy)-K(xy\mid x)=n+O(\log n).
\]
For sufficiently large $n$, this gain exceeds the fixed threshold $\lambda$ used to define extended representations. Consequently, $x$ remains an extended representation of $e$, even though the reduction has removed substantial information.
\end{example}

This example shows that reduction can significantly decrease the algorithmic information available about an admissible reference representation without necessarily destroying extendedness. In more extreme cases, removing the entire original representation may eliminate the information needed to satisfy the extended-representation criterion. The resulting empty string need not belong to $\mathcal{R}_e$.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to a chemical compound, and let $rs\in\mathcal{R}_e$ be a representation containing information about its molecular connectivity and stereochemical configuration. Suppose the suffix $s$ contains redundant annotations that can be reconstructed from the remaining representation $r$. Removing those annotations may preserve the information needed to reconstruct an admissible encoding of the compound. By contrast, if $s$ specifies the configuration of a chiral center, its removal may eliminate information required to distinguish the compound from another stereoisomer. In both cases, the transformation is a reduction, but its consequences for representing the original entity differ.
\end{example}

Reduction may also change which entities are represented by the resulting string. A reduction of an extended representation of $e$ may cease to represent $e$ while remaining an extended representation of another entity $e'\in\mathcal{E}$.

The distinction between augmentation and reduction is therefore important. Augmentation retains the original symbols while appending new ones; reduction removes symbols that may contain essential information. Neither operation guarantees that the transformed string remains an extended representation or that its adequacy improves. Augmentation may introduce irrelevant information, while reduction may discard relevant information. Their effects on deficiency, surplus, and miscoding will be examined in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

% Subsection: Operation of Concatenation

\subsection*{Operation of Concatenation}

A third operation consists of combining two existing extended representations into a single string. We call this operation \emph{joint representation}\index{Joint representation}. Joint representation differs from augmentation in that both component strings are already extended representations associated with one or more entities, whereas augmentation permits the appended string to be arbitrary. The components may be associated with the same entity or with two distinct entities. This distinction gives rise to two forms of joint representation, each with a different role in the theory of nescience. Throughout this subsection, the representation oracle and the threshold $\lambda$ used to define extended representations are fixed, and their subscripts are omitted when unambiguous.

\begin{definition}
Let $e_1,e_2\in\mathcal{E}$ be entities, and let $r\in\mathcal{R}_{e_1}$ and $s\in\mathcal{R}_{e_2}$ be extended representations. We define their \emph{joint representation}\index{Joint representation} as the concatenated string $rs$.

We distinguish two cases:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An \emph{intra-entity joint representation}\index{Intra-entity joint representation} occurs when $e_1=e_2$.
\item An \emph{inter-entity joint representation}\index{Inter-entity joint representation} occurs when $e_1\neq e_2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The distinction concerns the entities associated with the component representations rather than their syntactic form. Since the sets of extended representations of distinct entities may overlap, the same pair of strings may admit both interpretations under different choices of associated entities.

Intra-entity joining combines representations associated with one entity, potentially making complementary information available. Inter-entity joining combines representations associated with distinct entities and may reveal information about additional entities. Both are syntactic concatenations: neither guarantees that the resulting string belongs to the set of extended representations of any particular entity.

Before examining the two cases separately, we establish the general informational properties of joint representations. The first property concerns the preservation of information about admissible reference encodings of the component entities.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e_1,e_2\in\mathcal{E}$ be entities, and let $r\in\mathcal{R}_{e_1}$ and $s\in\mathcal{R}_{e_2}$. Then, for every admissible representation $\bar r_1\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{e_1}$,
\[
K(\bar r_1\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r_1\mid r)+O(\log(\ell(r)+2)),
\]
and, for every admissible representation $\bar r_2\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{e_2}$,
\[
K(\bar r_2\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r_2\mid s)+O(\log(\ell(s)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first inequality follows from the information-preservation property of augmentation, since $rs$ is an augmentation of $r$. For the second inequality, the string $s$ can be recovered from $rs$ by specifying its length $\ell(s)$ and extracting the corresponding suffix. A self-delimiting description of this length requires $O(\log(\ell(s)+2))$ bits. Once $s$ has been recovered, a shortest conditional description of $\bar r_2$ given $s$ can be executed. Therefore,
\[
K(\bar r_2\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r_2\mid s)+O(\log(\ell(s)+2)),
\]
as required.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that joint representation preserves the algorithmic information supplied by each component about its admissible reference encodings, up to logarithmic overheads for recovering the components. As in the preceding operations, this does not by itself establish semantic relevance to the associated entities.

The operation also inherits structural properties from ordinary string concatenation.

\begin{proposition}
Concatenation of extended representations is associative as a string operation. That is, for every $r,s,t\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}$,
\[
(rs)t=r(st).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The equality follows from the associativity of concatenation on $\mathcal{B}^\ast$.
\end{proof}

Associativity means that the grouping of successive concatenations does not affect the resulting binary string. It does not imply closure of joint representation on $\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}$: the intermediate strings $rs$ and $st$ need not themselves be extended representations. Thus, the equality is a statement about string concatenation, not necessarily about two successive applications of the formally defined joint-representation operation.

Joint representation is not commutative as a string operation: in general, $rs\neq sr$. Ordering may affect how the resulting string relates to the entities under investigation. Nevertheless, reversing the components changes the conditional algorithmic information available from the combined string by at most a logarithmic overhead.

\begin{proposition}
Let $r,s\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}$ and let $x\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$. Then
\[
K(x\mid rs)=K(x\mid sr)+O(\log(\ell(rs)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $rs$ and the length $\ell(r)$, one can separate the components and construct $sr$. A self-delimiting description of $\ell(r)$ requires $O(\log(\ell(rs)+2))$ bits. Consequently,
\[
K(x\mid rs)\leq K(x\mid sr)+O(\log(\ell(rs)+2)).
\]
The reverse inequality follows by specifying the corresponding boundary in $sr$ and reconstructing $rs$. Combining the two inequalities establishes the result.
\end{proof}

Hence the two concatenation orders are equivalent up to logarithmic overhead for the purpose of reconstructing a specified string, even though they need not have the same interpretation under a representation oracle.

A further property concerns duplication of a representation.

\begin{proposition}
Let $r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}$ and let $x\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$. Then
\[
K(x\mid rr)=K(x\mid r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The string $rr$ can be constructed from $r$ by a fixed program that duplicates its input. Therefore,
\[
K(x\mid r)\leq K(x\mid rr)+O(1).
\]
Conversely, since $rr$ consists of two identical halves, $r$ can be recovered from $rr$ by a fixed program that extracts its first half. Hence,
\[
K(x\mid rr)\leq K(x\mid r)+O(1).
\]
Combining these inequalities gives the result.
\end{proof}

This establishes the \emph{informational idempotence} of duplication: repeating a representation does not add independent algorithmic information beyond constant overhead. The string operation itself is not idempotent, since $rr$ is generally different from $r$. Nor must the representation oracle reconstruct the same entity from both strings.

An intra-entity joint representation combines two extended representations of the same entity. Its purpose is to make their potentially complementary informational contributions jointly available. The general preservation property takes a particularly useful form in this case.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $r,s\in\mathcal{R}_e$. Then, for every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_e$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)\leq
\min\{K(\bar r\mid r),K(\bar r\mid s)\}
+O(\log(\ell(rs)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The general information-preservation proposition gives
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r\mid r)+O(\log(\ell(r)+2))
\]
and
\[
K(\bar r\mid rs)\leq K(\bar r\mid s)+O(\log(\ell(s)+2)).
\]
Since $\ell(r),\ell(s)\leq\ell(rs)$, both overheads are bounded by $O(\log(\ell(rs)+2))$. Taking the smaller upper bound establishes the result.
\end{proof}

Thus, intra-entity joining preserves, up to logarithmic overhead, the information supplied by the more informative component relative to any fixed admissible reference encoding. A strict improvement is not guaranteed: if the two components convey essentially the same information, their concatenation may add little. If they convey complementary information, the joint string may require substantially fewer additional bits to reconstruct the reference encoding than either component alone.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:lung_cancer_joint}
Let $e$ be the entity corresponding to the causes of lung cancer. Suppose that $r$ is a representation consisting of observations collected exclusively from male subjects, while $s$ contains observations collected exclusively from female subjects. Both representations may contain nontrivial information about $e$ while omitting information about population variability.

The intra-entity joint representation $rs$ combines these collections of observations. If the datasets contain complementary information, their combination may provide a more informative representation of $e$ than either component separately. The combination does not, however, guarantee a less biased or more adequate representation; its contribution depends on the information contained in the datasets.
\end{example}

Intra-entity joining is therefore a mechanism for combining information associated with an existing entity. Its significance lies in the possible complementarity of the components, not merely in the increased length of the resulting string. Whether the combination reduces deficiency, increases surplus, or decreases miscoding depends on their informational content, as studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:Miscoding}.

An inter-entity joint representation combines extended representations of two distinct entities. Its purpose may be to investigate whether their combination carries information about entities beyond the two originally associated with the components.

Let $e_1,e_2\in\mathcal{E}$ be distinct, and let $r\in\mathcal{R}_{e_1}$ and $s\in\mathcal{R}_{e_2}$. The string $rs$ may remain informative about $e_1$ and $e_2$, and it may also satisfy the extended-representation criterion for another entity $e_3\in\mathcal{E}$. Since extended representations are not defined through unique identification, the same string may be associated with several entities.

The following example illustrates how inter-entity joining can make substantially more information about an additional entity available than either component supplies individually.

\begin{example}
Let $x,y\in\mathcal{B}^\ast$ be algorithmically independent, incompressible binary strings of equal length $n$, and let $z=x\oplus y$, where $\oplus$ denotes bitwise exclusive OR.

Consider three distinct entities $e_1,e_2,e_3\in\mathcal{E}$ whose admissible representations include $x$, $y$, and $z$, respectively. Since admissible representations are decodable, $x\in\mathcal{R}_{e_1}$ and $y\in\mathcal{R}_{e_2}$.

Under the stated incompressibility and independence assumptions, knowing either component alone leaves approximately $n$ bits of $z$ unspecified:
\[
K(z\mid x)=n+O(\log n),
\qquad
K(z\mid y)=n+O(\log n).
\]
By contrast, the joint string $xy$ allows $z$ to be reconstructed by a fixed program. Because the components have equal length, they can be separated by dividing $xy$ into two halves, so
\[
K(z\mid xy)=O(1).
\]
Since $K(z)=n+O(\log n)$, it follows that
\[
K(z)-K(z\mid xy)=n+O(\log n).
\]
For sufficiently large $n$, this information gain exceeds the fixed threshold $\lambda$ used to define extended representations. As $z\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{e_3}$, we conclude that
\[
xy\in\mathcal{R}_{e_3}.
\]
By comparison, the information gain relative to $z$ from either component alone is at most $O(\log n)$. Thus, the joint representation provides substantially more algorithmic information about this admissible reference encoding than either component individually. This does not establish that $x$ or $y$ lies outside $\mathcal{R}_{e_3}$: either string might satisfy the threshold criterion, possibly through another admissible reference encoding of $e_3$.
\end{example}

The example demonstrates that information available from a joint representation need not be substantially available from either component separately. A computable relationship between the components can make information about an additional entity accessible from their combination.

Inter-entity joining does not, however, guarantee that the joint string is an extended representation of an additional entity. It may contain only redundant information or remain informative solely about the original entities. It may also improve a representation of one of those entities by incorporating information associated with the other. Its potential role in the discovery of previously unrecognized entities will be examined in the later applications of the theory.

\subsubsection*{Joint Representations of Finite Collections}

The concept of joint representation extends from pairs to arbitrary finite collections.

\begin{definition}
Let $r_1,\ldots,r_n\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}$, where $n\geq 1$. We define their \emph{joint representation} as the concatenated string
\[
r_1r_2\cdots r_n.
\]
\end{definition}

By associativity of string concatenation, the result does not depend on how the components are grouped. If all components are associated with one entity, the construction generalizes intra-entity joining; if they are associated with different entities, it generalizes inter-entity joining. More generally, a finite collection may contain several representations of each of several entities. The resulting string can be formed by successive syntactic concatenations, although intermediate strings need not themselves be extended representations.

Let $w=r_1r_2\cdots r_n$. Any chosen component $r_i$ can be recovered from $w$ by specifying its starting position and length, requiring at most $O(\log(\ell(w)+2))$ additional bits. Consequently, for every admissible reference representation $\bar r$ of an entity associated with $r_i$,
\[
K(\bar r\mid w)
\leq K(\bar r\mid r_i)+O(\log(\ell(w)+2)).
\]
Thus, the joint string preserves access to the algorithmic information provided by each component, up to the overhead of identifying its boundaries. This does not guarantee that the joint string remains an extended representation of any specified entity, or that it improves representational adequacy.

\section{Representations of Research Areas}
\label{sec:representations_research_areas}

Research areas are subsets of the set of entities $\mathcal E$. Once individual entities admit representations, it is natural to ask how an entire research area can be represented. A representation of an area should collect representations of selected constituent entities while allowing each component to be identified and recovered independently. Unlike ordinary joint representation, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:joint_representations}, which uses string concatenation, an area representation uses a structured encoding that preserves component boundaries. The correspondence between components and entities must also be specified, although it need not be encoded in the resulting string.

Two limitations arise. A research area may be infinite, whereas every representation is a finite binary string. Moreover, an entity may not be knowable under the selected representation oracle and therefore may have no extended representations relative to that oracle. We accordingly construct area representations from finite subsets of oracle-knowable entities. Throughout the theoretical construction below, a representation oracle $\mathcal O$ and the threshold for extended representations are fixed; oracle subscripts may be omitted when unambiguous. For scientific applications, the selected oracle should also meet the conceptual requirements of admissibility discussed in Section~\ref{sec:admissible_oracles}.

\begin{definition}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a research area. Its \emph{knowable part}\index{Knowable part of an area} is
\[
A^\top=A\cap\mathcal E^\top.
\]
For a fixed representation oracle $\mathcal O$, its \emph{oracle-knowable part} is
\[
A^\top_{\mathcal O}=A\cap\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}.
\]
\end{definition}

The first set refers to knowability under at least one admissible oracle, whereas the second refers to the selected oracle. If $\mathcal O$ is admissible, then $A^\top_{\mathcal O}\subseteq A^\top$. Equality is not guaranteed: different entities may be knowable under different admissible oracles without all being knowable under one fixed oracle.

\begin{definition}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a research area, and let $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\subseteq A^\top_{\mathcal O}$ be a nonempty finite subset, with a fixed ordering $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. For each $i=1,\ldots,n$, choose an extended representation $r_i\in\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e_i}$. We define a \emph{representation of the research area}\index{Representation of a research area} $A$, associated with the finite subarea $F$, as the binary string
\[
\rho_F=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle,
\]
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes a fixed computable, self-delimiting encoding of finite sequences from which the number and values of the components can be recovered effectively.
\end{definition}

The encoding preserves the boundaries and order of the components. Their correspondence with the intended entities $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is determined by the selected finite subarea and its ordering, which are treated as part of the scientific context. The string $\rho_F$ alone need not identify those entities, since extended representations do not necessarily identify their targets uniquely. This construction does not require an effective enumeration of $\mathcal E$.

The string $\rho_F$ is not necessarily an extended representation of any individual entity. It is a structured string constructed from extended representations of the entities in $F$ and can be analyzed or described without introducing a separate oracle for research areas.

An area representation need not be complete. If $A^\top_{\mathcal O}$ is finite and nonempty, a representation covering all its entities can be constructed by choosing one extended representation per entity. A representation covering every entity in $A$ under the selected oracle requires $A$ itself to be finite and $A\subseteq\mathcal E^\top_{\mathcal O}$. Even when the globally knowable part $A^\top$ is finite, one fixed admissible oracle need not make every entity in it knowable. An entity outside $A^\top_{\mathcal O}$ cannot be included through the present component-wise construction using that oracle.

The fundamental structural property of an area representation is that its components remain recoverable.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\rho_F=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle$ be a representation of a finite subarea $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$. Each component $r_i$ can be recovered effectively from $\rho_F$ when its position $i$ is given.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The fixed sequence encoding has a computable parsing procedure. Given $\rho_F$, this procedure recovers the ordered sequence and returns the component at the specified position $i$.
\end{proof}

The proposition concerns recovery of the component string $r_i$, not identification or reconstruction of its intended entity $e_i$. It allows components to be analyzed, compared, or described independently.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\rho_F=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle$ be a representation of a finite subarea $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$. Then, for every $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and every admissible reference representation $\bar r_i\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{\mathcal O,e_i}$,
\[
K(\bar r_i\mid\rho_F)
\leq K(\bar r_i\mid r_i)+O(\log(n+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The number of components $n$ is computable from the structured string $\rho_F$. Since $1\leq i\leq n$, the index $i$ can be encoded in a fixed-length binary field of $O(\log(n+2))$ bits, with the field length determined by $n$. Given $\rho_F$ and this index, a fixed procedure recovers $r_i$. It can then execute a shortest conditional description of $\bar r_i$ given $r_i$. The index field has a known length, so combining it with that conditional description requires only a fixed additional decoding procedure. Hence
\[
K(\bar r_i\mid\rho_F)
\leq K(\bar r_i\mid r_i)+O(\log(n+2)),
\]
as claimed.
\end{proof}

Thus, an area representation preserves access to the algorithmic information that each component supplies about its admissible reference encodings, up to the cost of identifying that component. As in the preceding section, this does not itself establish the semantic relevance of that information to the entity.

Area representations are generally not unique. An entity may have several extended representations, and a finite subarea may be ordered in different ways. The same research area may therefore admit different structured representations. Moreover, a representation associated with $F\subseteq A$ can also serve as a partial representation of any larger area containing $F$, provided the component-to-entity correspondence is retained in the chosen context.

\subsection*{Effective Representations of Research Areas}

The preceding construction uses theoretical extended representations. Scientific practice, however, proceeds with effective representations that have been proposed or considered within a specified context. As in Section~\ref{sec:effective_representations}, we first define their area-level counterparts without introducing time.

\begin{definition}
For a research area $A\subseteq\mathcal E$, the \emph{effectively known part} of $A$ is
\[
\hat A=A\cap\hat{\mathcal E}.
\]
\end{definition}

The set $\hat A$ consists of the entities in $A$ to which the selected scientific context assigns at least one effective representation. Membership does not guarantee knowability or successful reconstruction: $\hat A$ may contain hypothetical, fictitious, incorrectly classified, or otherwise theoretically unknowable entities. Conversely, an entity may belong to $A^\top$ yet remain outside $\hat A$.

\begin{definition}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a research area, and let $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\subseteq\hat A$ be a nonempty finite subset with a fixed ordering. For each $i=1,\ldots,n$, choose an effective representation $\hat r_i\in\hat{\mathcal R}_{e_i}$. We define an \emph{effective representation of the research area}\index{Effective representation of a research area} $A$, associated with $F$, as the binary string
\[
\hat\rho_F=\langle\hat r_1,\ldots,\hat r_n\rangle.
\]
\end{definition}

An effective area representation collects the candidate representations assigned to the selected entities. Its components need not belong to the corresponding theoretical extended-representation sets $\mathcal R_{\mathcal O,e_i}$, and they may be inaccurate, misleading, or uninformative. As before, the correspondence between positions and intended entities is supplied by the ordered subarea, not necessarily by the encoded string.

\subsection*{Evolution of Area Representations}

To analyze scientific development, let $T$ be a linearly ordered set of time indices, with the entity domain and scientific context fixed for the comparison. Following Section~\ref{sec:effective_representations}, distinguish the effective representations considered at time $t$ from their cumulative historical record. Accordingly, define
\[
\hat A^{\,t}=A\cap\hat{\mathcal E}^{\,t},
\qquad
\hat A^{\,\leq t}=A\cap\hat{\mathcal E}^{\,\leq t}.
\]

An \emph{effective area representation at time $t$} is obtained by choosing a nonempty finite $F\subseteq\hat A^{\,t}$ and components $\hat r_i^{\,t}\in\hat{\mathcal R}_{e_i}^{\,t}$, and forming
\[
\hat\rho_F^{\,t}=\langle\hat r_1^{\,t},\ldots,\hat r_n^{\,t}\rangle.
\]
A \emph{cumulative effective area representation by time $t$} is defined analogously using $F\subseteq\hat A^{\,\leq t}$ and components $\hat r_i^{\,\leq t}\in\hat{\mathcal R}_{e_i}^{\,\leq t}$:
\[
\hat\rho_F^{\,\leq t}
=\langle\hat r_1^{\,\leq t},\ldots,\hat r_n^{\,\leq t}\rangle.
\]
The current-state representation need not include candidates considered earlier, whereas a cumulative representation may include candidates that were later rejected or superseded.

There is no general monotonicity requirement for $\hat A^{\,t}$. A community may cease to consider an entity or a particular representation. The cumulative effectively known part of a fixed area, however, is monotonic.

\begin{proposition}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a fixed research area, and let $t_1,t_2\in T$ satisfy $t_1<t_2$. Then
\[
\hat A^{\,\leq t_1}\subseteq\hat A^{\,\leq t_2}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the cumulative definition in Section~\ref{sec:effective_representations},
$\hat{\mathcal E}^{\,\leq t_1}\subseteq\hat{\mathcal E}^{\,\leq t_2}$. Intersecting both sides with the fixed set $A$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

The result records the historical expansion of the range of entities investigated within a fixed area. It does not imply improvement in scientific understanding, since new candidate representations may be incorrect, redundant, or associated with fictitious entities.

A further property concerns retaining previously considered components when cumulative representations are extended to larger finite subareas.

\begin{proposition}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be fixed, let $t_1<t_2$, and let $\hat\rho_{F_1}^{\,\leq t_1}$ be a cumulative effective representation of a nonempty finite subarea $F_1\subseteq\hat A^{\,\leq t_1}$. For every finite set $F_2$ such that
\[
F_1\subseteq F_2\subseteq\hat A^{\,\leq t_2},
\]
there exists a cumulative effective area representation $\hat\rho_{F_2}^{\,\leq t_2}$ that retains all component strings of $\hat\rho_{F_1}^{\,\leq t_1}$, with their original entity associations.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since cumulative effective-representation sets are monotonic, every component string associated with an entity in $F_1$ by time $t_1$ remains available in the cumulative set by time $t_2$. Retain these strings in their original order. For each entity in $F_2\setminus F_1$, choose an effective representation from its nonempty cumulative set at time $t_2$, and append these components in any fixed order. Encoding the resulting sequence yields the required representation.
\end{proof}

This extension preserves the old component strings and their contextual associations, not necessarily the earlier area representation as a literal binary prefix: the structured sequence encoding may change when new components are added.

These monotonicity results rely on keeping $A$ fixed. Scientific development may also change which entities are included in an area, as new candidates are introduced or earlier classifications are rejected.

\begin{example}
An astronomical investigation may initially include an object believed to be a planet. Later analysis may show that the observations arose from an instrumental artifact. The purported planet may then be excluded from the current classification of astronomical objects, even though its historical candidate representations remain recorded.
\end{example}

Thus, two changes should be distinguished: the cumulative expansion of entities investigated within a fixed area, and revisions to the membership of the area itself. For a time-varying area $A^t$, neither its successive memberships nor its effectively known parts need be monotonic. Moreover, the growth of the cumulative historical record, even when monotonic, does not by itself imply decreasing miscoding or improving scientific understanding.
