\chapter{Mismodel}
\label{chap:Mismodel}

\begin{quote}
\begin{flushright}
\emph{Everything should be made as simple as possible,\\
but not simpler.}\\
Albert Einstein 
\end{flushright}
\end{quote}
\bigskip

Even after a suitable representation of an entity has been chosen, the problem of understanding is far from solved. A representation is only the starting point: to reason about the entity, explain it, or make predictions, we still need a description, that is, a computable object intended to generate or reconstruct the representation. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, descriptions are the formal devices through which we attempt to capture the regularities present in a representation and separate them from what remains specific or accidental.

However, this second step introduces its own source of error. Because our understanding of an entity is usually incomplete, the descriptions we construct are rarely optimal. Some omit information needed to recover a minimal description of the target representation; others contain additional algorithmic information that cannot be recovered from any minimal description of that representation. In other words, even if the representation itself were admissible, our way of describing it may still be deficient, overly complicated, or both. Such imperfections can distort explanation, hinder prediction, and obscure the structure we are trying to uncover. It is therefore essential to identify this source of descriptive error and study it in a systematic way.

To address this issue, we introduce the concept of \emph{mismodel}, a measure used to quantify the error that arises from describing a representation poorly. At the descriptive level, there are two fundamental modes of failure. An extended description may be \emph{deficient}, in the sense that additional algorithmic information is needed to recover a minimal description of the target representation. Or it may exhibit \emph{surplus}, measured by the additional algorithmic information needed to reconstruct the current description from a minimal description of the target representation. The mismodel problem treats these two aspects as separate objectives to be minimized. A scalar measure of mismodel is then defined by taking the larger of the two, thereby measuring the dominant defect of a description relative to a fixed target representation.

As in the case of miscoding, an important complication arises immediately: minimal descriptions cannot generally be identified effectively. Because Kolmogorov complexity is not computable, we cannot in general determine the shortest description of a given representation, nor directly measure the exact gap between an available description and an ideal one. Nevertheless, the theory provides a rigorous conceptual framework for analyzing this gap in terms of algorithmic information. This allows us to define descriptive errors and study their mathematical properties even when the corresponding optimal descriptions remain unknown.

A deeper understanding of mismodel and of the mechanisms by which it can be reduced is essential for improving scientific modeling. Better descriptions do not merely reproduce representations more efficiently; they also make the underlying structure of those representations more accessible to reasoning, comparison, and refinement. In this chapter, we formally introduce the concepts of deficiency and surplus of a description, and from them derive the notion of \emph{mismodel}. We study the main properties of these quantities, introduce a normalized notion of relative mismodel, and examine mathematical conditions for decreasing mismodel through enrichment and simplification of descriptions. In this way, the chapter develops the descriptive counterpart of miscoding and clarifies the conditions under which imperfect descriptions can be brought closer to descriptive optimality.

\section{Deficiency of a Description}
\label{sec:deficiency_description}

An extended description of a representation may contain useful information about that representation without capturing its structure completely or optimally. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, an extended description need not reproduce its target representation exactly. Even when it does, it may fail to reveal a shortest description of that representation. In scientific practice, this situation arises when an available model captures some of the regularities of a phenomenon but additional information is still required to obtain an optimal computational account of it.

We formalize this notion by measuring the shortest directed algorithmic distance from an extended description to the set of minimal descriptions of the same representation. We call this quantity the \emph{deficiency of a description}.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:deficiency_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r$ be an extended description of $r$. We define the \emph{deficiency of $d$ as a description of $r$}\index{Deficiency of a description} as
\[
d_D(r,d) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d^\ast\mid d).
\]
\end{definition}

The set $\mathcal D_r^\ast$ is non-empty and finite, as established in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. Since conditional Kolmogorov complexity takes values in $\mathbb N$, the minimum is well defined.

The quantity $K(d^\ast\mid d)$ measures the length of the shortest program that, given the description $d$, outputs the minimal description $d^\ast$. Minimizing over $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ selects the minimal description that is easiest to recover from $d$. Thus, $d_D(r,d)$ measures the minimum amount of additional algorithmic information required to obtain some minimal description of $r$ from the current extended description.

It is important to distinguish this notion from ordinary descriptive length or reconstruction error. A description may generate $r$ exactly without being minimal, and recovering a minimal description from it may require additional information. Conversely, a non-exact extended description may already contain substantial information about a minimal description of $r$. Deficiency therefore concerns the algorithmic relationship between descriptions, rather than merely the difference between their outputs.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:deficiency_description}
Let $r$ be a binary representation encoding the complete adjacency list of a finite graph. An extended description $d\in\mathcal D_r$ may generate only a partial description of the graph, such as an encoding of a subset of its vertices and edges. Suppose that this partial description provides sufficient information
about $r$ to satisfy the informational condition defining extended descriptions, but does not determine the complete graph. In particular, we may have $\Gamma(d)\neq r$, while
\[
K(r\mid\Gamma(d))\le K(r)-\tau,
\]
where $\tau$ is the information threshold introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. The deficiency $d_D(r,d)$ measures how much additional algorithmic information must be supplied, beyond the current description, to recover some minimal description of the complete graph. If the partial description already captures most of the information needed to reconstruct the graph, the remaining deficiency may be relatively small. If substantial structural information is missing, the deficiency may be large. In either case, recovering a minimal description may require additional information beyond that needed merely to reconstruct the graph.
\end{example}

Before studying the structural properties of deficiency, we establish that it is stable under changes of the reference universal machine used to evaluate conditional Kolmogorov complexity.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_description_machine_invariance}
Let $U$ and $V$ be two reference universal prefix-free machines used to evaluate conditional Kolmogorov complexity. Assume that the description system $\Gamma$, the reference complexity used to define extended descriptions, and the information threshold remain fixed. Then, for every $r\in\mathcal R$ and every $d\in\mathcal D_r$,
\[
d_D^{\,U}(r,d) = d_D^{\,V}(r,d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the invariance theorem for conditional Kolmogorov complexity, there exist constants $c_{UV}$ and $c_{VU}$ such that, for every pair of binary strings $x,y$,
\[
K_U(x\mid y)\le K_V(x\mid y)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
K_V(x\mid y)\le K_U(x\mid y)+c_{VU}.
\]
Applying these inequalities to every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ and taking minima gives
\[
d_D^{\,U}(r,d)\le d_D^{\,V}(r,d)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
d_D^{\,V}(r,d)\le d_D^{\,U}(r,d)+c_{VU}.
\]
Combining both inequalities yields the result.
\end{proof}

Thus, deficiency is machine-invariant in the same sense as conditional Kolmogorov complexity. The proposition concerns changes in the machine used to measure conditional complexity while the underlying description system remains fixed. Changing the description system itself may change the set of minimal descriptions and therefore requires separate analysis.

The following proposition establishes the basic bounds on deficiency.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_description_bounds}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
0\le d_D(r,d)\le K(r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$, the conditional complexity $K(d^\ast\mid d)$ is the length of a program and is therefore non-negative. Taking the minimum gives $d_D(r,d)\ge 0$.

For the upper bound, choose any $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$. By definition,
\[
d_D(r,d)\le K(d^\ast\mid d).
\]
Since the valid description codes form a recursively enumerable prefix-free set, a fixed prefix-free machine can reproduce any valid description from its code. Therefore,
\[
K(d^\ast\mid d)\le K(d^\ast)+O(1) \le \ell(d^\ast)+O(1).
\]
Under the fixed optimal universal description system used throughout the theory, minimal descriptions satisfy $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r)$. Consequently,
\[
d_D(r,d)\le K(r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that deficiency is always finite and is bounded, up to an additive constant, by the Kolmogorov complexity of the target representation. In the worst case, the current description can be disregarded and a minimal description supplied from scratch.

% Subsection: Deficiency and Reconstruction Complexity

\subsection*{Deficiency and Reconstruction Complexity}

Deficiency measures the information required to recover a minimal description of $r$, rather than merely the information required to reconstruct $r$ itself. Nevertheless, these two quantities are closely related. The following result provides a characterization of deficiency in terms of the information required to recover both the target representation and its Kolmogorov complexity.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_description_characterization}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Under the fixed optimal universal description system, we have
\[
d_D(r,d) = K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1),
\]
where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denotes a fixed computable encoding of pairs.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Since $d^\ast$ is a minimal description of $r$, we have $\Gamma(d^\ast)=r$ and $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r)$. Given $d^\ast$, a fixed program can compute $r$ by executing $d^\ast$, and can determine $K(r)$ by reading the length of $d^\ast$. Therefore,
\[
K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr) \le K(d^\ast\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over all $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ gives
\[
K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)
\le d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
For the reverse inequality, suppose that both $r$ and $K(r)$ are given. Since the description system is effectively enumerable, one can enumerate all candidate descriptions of length $K(r)$ and simulate their computations by dovetailing. Because at least one minimal description of $r$ exists, this procedure eventually finds a description $d^\ast$ satisfying $\Gamma(d^\ast)=r$ and $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r)$. Such a description necessarily belongs to $\mathcal D_r^\ast$. Consequently, there exists a fixed program that, given $\langle r,K(r)\rangle$, outputs some minimal description of $r$. Therefore,
\[
d_D(r,d) \le K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
d_D(r,d) = K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition reveals an important property of descriptive deficiency. Recovering a minimal description of $r$ is algorithmically equivalent, up to a constant, to recovering both the representation $r$ and the length of one of its shortest descriptions. The distinction matters because knowing a representation does not, in general, imply knowing its Kolmogorov complexity. Although every finite representation admits a minimal description, finding one requires information that may not be available from the representation alone.

The next corollary establishes quantitative bounds relating deficiency to the information required to reconstruct the target representation.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:deficiency_description_reconstruction_bounds}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
K(r\mid d)-O(1) \le d_D(r,d) \le K(r\mid d) + K(K(r)\mid r) + O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_description_characterization},
\[
d_D(r,d) = K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1).
\]
Since $r$ is computable from the pair $\langle r,K(r)\rangle$,
\[
K(r\mid d) \le K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1),
\]
which establishes the lower bound.

For the upper bound, given $d$, one can first reconstruct $r$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(r\mid d)$. Once $r$ has been obtained, one can recover $K(r)$ using a shortest program of length $K(K(r)\mid r)$. Combining these procedures using the fixed universal prefix-free machine yields
\[
K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr) \le K(r\mid d)+K(K(r)\mid r)+O(1).
\]
Substituting the characterization of deficiency gives the desired upper bound.
\end{proof}

The lower bound shows that deficiency cannot be substantially smaller than the amount of algorithmic information required to reconstruct the target representation. The upper bound shows that the difference between these quantities is controlled by the information required to determine the Kolmogorov complexity of the target once the target is known. Since
\[
K(K(r)\mid r)=O(\log(K(r)+2)),
\]
we obtain the simpler bound
\[
K(r\mid d)-O(1) \le d_D(r,d) \le K(r\mid d) + O(\log(K(r)+2)).
\]
Thus, the difference between descriptive deficiency and ordinary reconstruction complexity is at most logarithmic in the complexity of the target representation.

This relationship shows that $K(r\mid d)$ approximates descriptive deficiency up to an additive term of order $O(\log(K(r)+2))$. Nevertheless, these quantities need not coincide, since reconstruction alone does not necessarily reveal a shortest description. A particularly important case occurs when the given description already generates the target representation exactly.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:deficiency_exact_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and let $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_r$ be an exact description of $r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,d) = K(K(r)\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
d_D(r,d) \le K(K(r)\mid r)+O(1) = O(\log(K(r)+2)).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Since $d$ is an exact description of $r$, we have $\Gamma(d)=r$, and therefore $K(r\mid d)=O(1)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_description_characterization},
\[
d_D(r,d) = K\bigl(\langle r,K(r)\rangle\mid d\bigr)+O(1).
\]
Since $r$ is computable from $d$ with constant overhead, recovering the pair $\langle r,K(r)\rangle$ from $d$ is equivalent, up to an additive constant, to recovering $K(r)$ from $d$. Hence
\[
d_D(r,d)=K(K(r)\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Furthermore, because $r$ is computable from $d$,
\[
K(K(r)\mid d)\le K(K(r)\mid r)+O(1).
\]
The stated bound follows.
\end{proof}

This corollary distinguishes exactness from descriptive optimality. An exact description already contains enough information to reconstruct the target representation, but it need not contain enough information to identify its shortest description. The additional deficiency of an exact description is therefore bounded by the information required to determine the shortest description length, given the current description.

For minimal descriptions, an even stronger result holds.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:minimal_description_has_small_deficiency}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be a
minimal description of $r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,d)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $d^\ast=d$ in Definition~\ref{def:deficiency_description}. Then $d_D(r,d)\le K(d\mid d)$. Since a constant-length program can output its conditional input, $K(d\mid d)=O(1)$. Therefore $d_D(r,d)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

This proposition confirms that minimal descriptions have negligible deficiency. However, the converse does not hold: a description may have negligible deficiency without itself being minimal.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:nonminimal_low_deficiency}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be a minimal description of a representation $r$. Construct another valid description $d$ that contains $d^\ast$ in a computably recoverable form, together with an additional block of information $z$. Suppose that the computation encoded by $d$ executes $d^\ast$ and ignores $z$, so that $\Gamma(d)=\Gamma(d^\ast)=r$. For sufficiently long $z$, the description $d$ is not minimal. Nevertheless, since $d^\ast$ can be recovered from $d$ by a fixed procedure, $K(d^\ast\mid d)=O(1)$. Consequently, $d_D(r,d)=O(1)$. Thus, a description may contain substantial unnecessary information while having negligible deficiency. The additional information does not prevent the recovery of a minimal description because the latter is already accessible within the current description.
\end{example}

This example clarifies why deficiency alone cannot characterize descriptive optimality. A description may be algorithmically close to a minimal description in the direction measured by deficiency while still containing substantial additional information. That complementary defect will be measured by the surplus of a description, introduced in the following section.

It is also important to distinguish conditioning on a description from conditioning on its output. Since $\Gamma(d)$ is computable from $d$, we have
\[
K(r\mid d)\le K(r\mid\Gamma(d))+O(1).
\]
However, the reverse inequality need not hold. A description may contain information that is not reflected in its output, including information useful for recovering a minimal description of $r$. Consequently, descriptive deficiency depends on the complete computational description, not merely on the representation it generates. 

% Subsection: Information-Loss Bound

\subsection*{Information-Loss Bound}

An extended description may be replaced by another description that preserves some of its information while discarding or modifying other parts. Such transformations may affect the amount of algorithmic information required to recover a minimal description of the target representation. It is therefore useful to establish a general bound on how much deficiency can increase when one extended description is replaced by another.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_description_information_loss}
Let $r \in \mathcal R$, and let $d,s \in \mathcal D_r$ be two extended descriptions of $r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,d) \le d_D(r,s)+K(s\mid d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast \in \mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Given $d$, one can first reconstruct $s$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(s\mid d)$, and then reconstruct $d^\ast$ from $s$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(d^\ast\mid s)$. Combining these procedures with constant overhead gives
\[
K(d^\ast\mid d) \le K(s\mid d)+K(d^\ast\mid s)+O(1).
\]
Taking minima over $d^\ast \in \mathcal D_r^\ast$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_D(r,d)
& = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d^\ast\mid d)\\
& \le K(s\mid d) + \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d^\ast\mid s) + O(1)\\
& = d_D(r,s)+K(s\mid d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that the increase in deficiency caused by replacing an extended description $s$ with another extended description $d$ is bounded by the algorithmic information required to reconstruct $s$ from $d$. In particular, when the original description can be recovered from the new one with constant overhead, the transformation cannot substantially increase deficiency.

An immediate consequence is that descriptions which are mutually recoverable with constant overhead have essentially the same deficiency.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:deficiency_description_equivalent}
Let $r \in \mathcal R$, and let $d,s \in \mathcal D_r$. If $K(d\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid d)=O(1)$,
then
\[
d_D(r,d)=d_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_description_information_loss},
\[
d_D(r,d) \le d_D(r,s)+K(s\mid d)+O(1) = d_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
Exchanging the roles of $d$ and $s$ gives
\[
d_D(r,s) \le d_D(r,d)+K(d\mid s)+O(1) = d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
d_D(r,d)=d_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This corollary shows that deficiency is stable under transformations that preserve the algorithmic information contained in a description, provided that the descriptions can be recovered from one another with uniformly bounded overhead. In particular, reversible computable recodings implemented by fixed procedures do not substantially change deficiency.

These results provide a general mathematical foundation for analyzing changes in deficiency without prescribing any particular transformation of descriptions. Their application to the augmentation and reduction operators introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, together with the conditions under which such operations decrease mismodel, will be examined in Section~\ref{sec:decreasing_mismodel}.

\section{Surplus of a Description}
\label{sec:surplus_description}

An extended description of a representation may contain more algorithmic information than is needed to describe that representation optimally. Some of this additional content may be redundant, in the sense that it can be recovered from a minimal description and therefore carries no substantial independent information. Other content, however, may not be recoverable from any minimal description without substantial additional algorithmic information. Such information may arise from unnecessary assumptions, arbitrary parameters, unrelated computational procedures, or auxiliary data that are not determined by the representation itself. We call the additional information required to recover the current description from a minimal one the \emph{surplus of a description}.

Intuitively, surplus measures the shortest directed algorithmic distance from the set of minimal descriptions of a representation to a given extended description of that same representation. It quantifies how much additional algorithmic information must be supplied, beyond a minimal description, in order to recover the current description. This distinguishes surplus from ordinary description length: a description may be considerably longer than a minimal one without containing substantial surplus, provided that its additional content is computably recoverable from a minimal description.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:surplus_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r$ be an extended description of $r$. We define the \emph{surplus of $d$ as a description of $r$}\index{Surplus of a description} as
\[
s_D(r,d) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d\mid d^\ast).
\]
\end{definition}

The set $\mathcal D_r^\ast$ is non-empty and finite, as established in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. Since conditional Kolmogorov complexity takes values in $\mathbb N$, the minimum is well defined.

The quantity $K(d\mid d^\ast)$ measures the length of the shortest program that, given a minimal description $d^\ast$ of $r$, outputs the extended description $d$. Minimizing over $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ selects the minimal description from which $d$ is easiest to recover. Thus, $s_D(r,d)$ quantifies the minimum amount of additional algorithmic information required to obtain the current description from some minimal description of the target representation.

It is important to distinguish surplus from unnecessary descriptive length. A long description need not have large surplus if its additional content can be reconstructed from a minimal description using a short program. Conversely, a description may contain substantial surplus when it incorporates independent information that cannot be recovered from any minimal description of the target representation.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:surplus_description}
Let $r$ be a binary representation encoding a finite time series, and let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be a minimal description of $r$. Consider another description $d$ constructed from $d^\ast$ together with an additional binary string $z$. Suppose that the computation encoded by $d$ executes $d^\ast$ and ignores $z$, so that $\Gamma(d) = \Gamma(d^\ast) = r$. Assume that $z$ is an incompressible string of length $n$ that is algorithmically independent of $d^\ast$. More precisely, suppose that
\[
K(z\mid d^\ast,K(d^\ast))\ge n-O(1).
\]
Assume also that $d$ is constructed using a fixed computable encoding from which both $d^\ast$ and $z$ can be recovered. Such a construction can be implemented by a fixed machine that parses the two components, executes $d^\ast$, and ignores the additional string. Since $z$ contributes approximately $n$ bits of independent information, we have
\[
K(d)\ge K(d^\ast)+n-O(1).
\]
Moreover, because $d^\ast$ is minimal,
\[
K(d^\ast)=K(r)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
K(d)\ge K(r)+n-O(1).
\]
As will be established below, this implies
\[
s_D(r,d)\ge n-O(1).
\]
Thus, the description $d$ reproduces the target representation exactly, but it contains substantial surplus. The additional information $z$ does not contribute to reconstructing $r$ and cannot be recovered from a minimal description without supplying essentially its own algorithmic content.
\end{example}

As in the case of deficiency, surplus depends on the choice of reference universal machine only up to an additive constant. Thus, surplus is stable under the usual machine-independence convention of algorithmic information theory.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_description_machine_invariance}
Let $U$ and $V$ be two reference universal prefix-free machines used to evaluate conditional Kolmogorov complexity. Assume that the description system $\Gamma$, the set of extended descriptions $\mathcal D_r$, and the set of minimal descriptions $\mathcal D_r^\ast$ remain fixed. Then, for every $r\in\mathcal R$ and every $d\in\mathcal D_r$,
\[
s_D^{\,U}(r,d) = s_D^{\,V}(r,d) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the invariance theorem for conditional Kolmogorov complexity, there exist constants $c_{UV}$ and $c_{VU}$ such that, for every pair of binary strings $x,y$,
\[
K_U(x\mid y)\le K_V(x\mid y)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
K_V(x\mid y)\le K_U(x\mid y)+c_{VU}.
\]
Applying these inequalities to every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ and taking minima gives
\[
s_D^{\,U}(r,d)\le s_D^{\,V}(r,d)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
s_D^{\,V}(r,d)\le s_D^{\,U}(r,d)+c_{VU}.
\]
Combining both inequalities yields the result.
\end{proof}

Thus, surplus is machine-invariant in the same sense as conditional Kolmogorov complexity. The proposition concerns changes in the machine used to measure conditional complexity while the underlying description system remains fixed. Changing the description system itself may change the set of minimal descriptions and therefore requires separate analysis.

The following proposition establishes the basic bounds on surplus.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_description_upper_bound}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
0\le s_D(r,d)\le K(d)+O(1)\le\ell(d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$, the conditional complexity $K(d\mid d^\ast)$ is the length of a program and is therefore non-negative. Taking the minimum gives $s_D(r,d) \ge 0$.

For the upper bound, choose any $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$. By definition,
\[
s_D(r,d)\le K(d\mid d^\ast).
\]
A program may ignore its conditional input $d^\ast$ and output $d$ directly. Therefore,
\[
K(d\mid d^\ast)\le K(d)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
s_D(r,d)\le K(d)+O(1).
\]
Finally, under the fixed optimal universal prefix-free description system used throughout the theory, the complete description encoding is prefix-free, so
\[
K(d)\le\ell(d)+O(1).
\]
Combining these inequalities yields the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that surplus is always finite and is bounded, up to an additive constant, by the Kolmogorov complexity of the description itself. In the worst case, the minimal description can be disregarded and the current description supplied from scratch.

Minimal descriptions themselves have negligible surplus.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_minimal_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be a
minimal description of $r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $d^\ast=d$ in Definition~\ref{def:surplus_description}. Then $s_D(r,d)\le K(d\mid d)$. Since a constant-length program can output its conditional input, we have $K(d\mid d)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

This proposition confirms that minimal descriptions have negligible surplus. Since a minimal description already belongs to the reference set against which surplus is measured, recovering it requires no substantial additional algorithmic information. However, the converse does not hold. A description may have negligible surplus without being minimal, because information that is computably recoverable from a minimal description does not contribute substantially to surplus.

% Subsection: Surplus and Description Complexity

\subsection*{Surplus and Description Complexity}

Surplus measures the algorithmic information required to reconstruct an extended description from a minimal description of the target representation. It is therefore natural to investigate how this quantity relates to the Kolmogorov complexity of the description itself.

The following proposition establishes a lower bound on surplus in terms of the difference between the complexities of the description and the target representation.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_description_lower_bound}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d)\ge K(d)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Given $d^\ast$, one can reconstruct $d$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(d\mid d^\ast)$. Therefore,
\[
K(d) \le K(d^\ast)+K(d\mid d^\ast) + O(1).
\]
Since $d^\ast$ is a minimal description of $r$, the properties of minimal descriptions established in
Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions} give
\[
K(d^\ast) = K(r) + O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
K(d\mid d^\ast) \ge K(d)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over all $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ yields
\[
s_D(r,d)\ge K(d)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that surplus cannot be substantially smaller than the additional Kolmogorov complexity contained in the current description relative to the target representation. In particular, if $K(d)\gg K(r)$ then the description necessarily has substantial surplus. However, the difference $K(d)-K(r)$ does not generally determine surplus for arbitrary extended descriptions. Since an extended description need not reconstruct $r$ exactly, its algorithmic information may be distributed differently from that of the target representation.

A stronger relationship holds when the description reconstructs the target exactly.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_exact_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_r$ be an exact description of $r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d) = K(d\mid r,K(r)) + O(1).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
s_D(r,d) = K(d) - K(r) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Since $d^\ast$ is a minimal description of $r$, we have $\Gamma(d^\ast)=r$ and $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r)$. Thus, given $d^\ast$, a fixed program can compute both $r$ and $K(r)$ by executing $d^\ast$ and reading its length. Consequently,
\[
K(d\mid d^\ast) \le K(d\mid r,K(r))+O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ gives
\[
s_D(r,d) \le K(d\mid r,K(r))+O(1).
\]
For the reverse inequality, Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_description_lower_bound} gives
\[
s_D(r,d)\ge K(d)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
Since $d$ is an exact description of $r$, we have $\Gamma(d)=r$, so $r$ is computable from $d$ with constant overhead. By the chain rule for prefix Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
K(d,r) = K(r)+K(d\mid r,K(r))+O(1).
\]
Moreover, because $r$ is computable from $d$,
\[
K(d,r)=K(d)+O(1).
\]
Combining these equalities yields
\[
K(d\mid r,K(r)) = K(d)-K(r)+O(1).
\]
Substituting into the lower bound gives
\[
s_D(r,d) \ge K(d\mid r,K(r))-O(1).
\] 
Combining the upper and lower bounds establishes
\[
s_D(r,d) = K(d\mid r,K(r))+O(1),
\]
and therefore
\[
s_D(r,d) = K(d)-K(r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition provides a direct characterization of surplus for exact descriptions. In this case, surplus is equal, up to an additive constant, to the difference between the Kolmogorov complexity of the description and the Kolmogorov complexity of its output. The result is significant because the difference concerns algorithmic complexity rather than literal description length. A description may be considerably longer than necessary without containing substantial surplus, provided that the additional symbols are computably recoverable from a
minimal description.

\begin{example}
Consider a minimal description $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ of $r$, and construct a new description $d$ by executing $d^\ast$ and then expressing its output through a verbose sequence of literal printing instructions. If this transformation is performed by a fixed computable procedure, then $K(d\mid d^\ast)=O(1)$, and consequently $s_D(r,d)=O(1)$, even when $\ell(d) \gg \ell(d^\ast)$.
\end{example}

As shown in the previous example, redundant descriptive length does not necessarily imply surplus. What matters is whether the additional information is algorithmically independent of the information available in a minimal description.

The characterization of surplus for exact descriptions also complements the corresponding result established for deficiency. Recall that, for an exact description $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_r$,
\[
d_D(r,d) = K(K(r)\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Together, the two results give
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_D(r,d)
& = K(K(r)\mid d) + O(1),\\
s_D(r,d)
& = K(d)-K(r)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]

These identities reveal two distinct sources of descriptive imperfection. Deficiency measures the information still needed to identify a shortest description of the target representation, whereas surplus measures the additional algorithmic complexity contained in the current description beyond that of the target.

A description can therefore have negligible deficiency while possessing substantial surplus. This occurs, for example, when a minimal description is already recoverable from the current description, but the latter also contains independent information unrelated to the reconstruction of the target.

% Subsection: Information-Growth Bound

\subsection*{Information-Growth Bound}

An extended description may be replaced by another description that introduces additional information or modifies its computational structure. Such transformations may increase or decrease the algorithmic information required to recover the new description from a minimal description of the target representation. It is therefore useful to establish a general bound on how much surplus can increase when one extended description is replaced by another.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_description_information_growth}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d,s\in\mathcal D_r$ be two extended descriptions of $r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d) \le s_D(r,s) + K(d\mid s) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Given $d^\ast$, one can first reconstruct $s$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(s\mid d^\ast)$, and then reconstruct $d$ from $s$ using a shortest conditional program of length $K(d\mid s)$. Combining these procedures with constant overhead gives
\[
K(d\mid d^\ast) \le K(s\mid d^\ast) + K(d\mid s) + O(1).
\]
Taking minima over $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_D(r,d)
& = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d\mid d^\ast) \\
& \le \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast} \bigl(K(s\mid d^\ast)+K(d\mid s)+O(1)\bigr) \\
& = s_D(r,s)+K(d\mid s)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that the increase in surplus caused by replacing an extended description $s$ with another extended description $d$ is bounded by the additional algorithmic information required to reconstruct $d$ from $s$. In particular, if the new description can be computed from the previous one with constant overhead, then its surplus cannot increase substantially.

This is the natural counterpart of the information-loss bound established for deficiency:
\[
d_D(r,d) \le d_D(r,s) + K(s\mid d) + O(1).
\]
The two inequalities differ in the direction of conditional complexity. Deficiency is controlled by the information needed to recover the previous description from the new one, whereas surplus is controlled by the information needed to construct the new description from the previous one.

An immediate consequence is that descriptions which are mutually recoverable with constant overhead have essentially the same surplus.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:surplus_description_equivalent}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d,s\in\mathcal D_r$. If $K(d\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid d)=O(1)$, then
\[
s_D(r,d)=s_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_description_information_growth},
\[
s_D(r,d) \le s_D(r,s)+K(d\mid s)+O(1) = s_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
Exchanging the roles of $d$ and $s$ gives
\[
s_D(r,s) \le s_D(r,d)+K(s\mid d)+O(1) = s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
s_D(r,d)=s_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This corollary shows that surplus is stable under transformations that preserve the algorithmic information contained in a description, provided that the descriptions can be recovered from one another with uniformly bounded overhead. In particular, reversible computable recodings implemented by fixed procedures do not substantially change surplus.

These results establish general mathematical properties of surplus without prescribing any particular transformation of descriptions. Their application to the augmentation and reduction operators introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, together with the conditions under which such operations decrease mismodel, will be examined in Section~\ref{sec:decreasing_mismodel}.

\section{Mismodel}
\label{sec:mismodel_description}

The concepts of deficiency and surplus capture two fundamentally different ways in which an extended description may fail with respect to a target representation $r$. Deficiency measures the algorithmic information required to recover a minimal description of $r$ from the current description, whereas surplus measures the information required to recover the current description from a minimal one. These quantities correspond to two opposite directions of algorithmic information transfer between an extended description and the set of minimal descriptions of the same representation.

Since deficiency and surplus need not decrease simultaneously, the analysis of mismodel is naturally formulated as a multiobjective optimization problem rather than as a single-objective one. An improvement in one component may be accompanied by a deterioration in the other. Consequently, the quality of a description cannot generally be characterized by considering either component in isolation.

% Section: Pareto Optimality

\subsection*{Pareto Optimality}

Before introducing a scalar measure of mismodel, it is useful to make explicit that descriptive refinement involves two competing objectives.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:mismodel_problem}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation. We define the \emph{mismodel problem}\index{Mismodel problem} for $r$ as the following multiobjective optimization problem:
\[
\min_{d\in\mathcal D_r} \bigl(d_D(r,d),\,s_D(r,d)\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

This formulation emphasizes that mismodel is not, at its core, a single number, but a trade-off between two distinct objectives: reducing the information required to recover a minimal description and reducing the additional information contained in the current description relative to a minimal one.

The feasible set $\mathcal D_r$ contains all extended descriptions of $r$, including exact and minimal descriptions. Thus, the optimization problem is formulated over descriptions that either reconstruct the target representation exactly or contain non-trivial algorithmic information about it.

The natural comparison relation for this problem is dominance, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:multiobjective_optimization}.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:dominance_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d,d'\in\mathcal D_r$ be two extended descriptions of $r$. We say that $d'$
\emph{dominates}\index{Description dominance} $d$, denoted by $d' \prec_r d$, if
\[
d_D(r,d')\le d_D(r,d), \qquad s_D(r,d')\le s_D(r,d),
\]
and at least one of these inequalities is strict.
\end{definition}

A dominating description is at least as good in both components and strictly better in one. A dominated description is therefore suboptimal in a strong sense: there exists another extended description that improves one component without worsening the other.

The appropriate notion of optimality for a multiobjective problem is Pareto optimality.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:pareto_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$. An extended description $d\in\mathcal D_r$ is said to be \emph{Pareto optimal}\index{Pareto optimal description} with respect to deficiency and surplus if there does not exist any $d'\in\mathcal D_r$ such that $d'\prec_r d$.

The set of all Pareto-optimal descriptions of $r$ is denoted by
\[
\mathcal P_r = \bigl\{ d\in\mathcal D_r: \nexists d'\in\mathcal D_r \text{ such that }d'\prec_r d \bigr\},
\]
and is called the \emph{Pareto-optimal set} of the mismodel problem. Its image under the mapping
\[
d\mapsto \bigl(d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\bigr)
\]
is called the \emph{Pareto frontier}\index{Mismodel Pareto frontier}.
\end{definition}

The Pareto-optimal set collects those extended descriptions for which no improvement in one component is possible without worsening the other. It therefore characterizes the non-dominated solutions of the theoretical mismodel problem. It is important to distinguish this theoretical frontier from the collection of descriptions currently available in scientific practice. Minimal descriptions exist mathematically for every representation, but they are generally unknown and cannot be identified effectively. Consequently, the theoretical Pareto frontier need not coincide with the frontier obtained by restricting the optimization problem to extended descriptions that have actually been discovered or investigated.


In practical applications, one may instead consider a non-empty restricted set $\mathcal C_r\subseteq\mathcal D_r$, for example the descriptions belonging to a particular model class. The resulting restricted optimization problem may have a different Pareto frontier, reflecting the limitations of the descriptions under consideration.

The following proposition establishes that Pareto-optimal descriptions always exist, without requiring the feasible set to be finite.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:pareto_nonempty_descriptions}
For every representation $r\in\mathcal R$, the Pareto-optimal set $\mathcal P_r$ is non-empty.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\mathcal D_r^\ast\subseteq\mathcal D_r$ and $\mathcal D_r^\ast\neq\varnothing$, the feasible set $\mathcal D_r$ is non-empty. Consider the function
\[
F(d)=d_D(r,d)+s_D(r,d).
\]
Both deficiency and surplus take values in $\mathbb N$. Consequently, the non-empty set $\{F(d):d\in\mathcal D_r\}$ has a minimum. Choose $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r$ satisfying
\[
F(d^\ast)=\min_{d\in\mathcal D_r}F(d).
\]
Suppose, for contradiction, that $d^\ast$ is not Pareto optimal. Then there exists $u\in\mathcal D_r$ such that
$u\prec_r d^\ast$. Hence $d_D(r,u)\le d_D(r,d^\ast)$ and $s_D(r,u)\le s_D(r,d^\ast)$, with at least one strict inequality. Therefore $F(u)<F(d^\ast)$, contradicting the minimality of $d^\ast$. Thus $d^\ast\in\mathcal P_r$, and consequently $\mathcal P_r\neq\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

This proposition guarantees that the theoretical mismodel problem always admits at least one non-dominated description. The same argument applies to any non-empty restricted feasible set, since the objective values remain non-negative integers.

The next proposition establishes why dominated descriptions can be excluded when using strictly increasing scalar evaluation criteria.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:dominated_not_minimal_increasing_scalarization_mismodel}
Let $\phi:\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^2\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}$ be strictly increasing in each argument. If $d\in\mathcal D_r$ is dominated, then $d$ cannot minimize $\phi\bigl(d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\bigr)$ over $\mathcal D_r$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $d$ is dominated, there exists $d'\in\mathcal D_r$ such that $d_D(r,d')\le d_D(r,d)$ and $s_D(r,d')\le s_D(r,d)$, with at least one strict inequality. Because $\phi$ is strictly increasing in each argument,
\[
\phi\bigl(d_D(r,d'),s_D(r,d')\bigr) < \phi\bigl(d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\bigr).
\]
Therefore $d$ cannot minimize the scalar criterion.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that every minimizer of a strictly increasing scalarization must be Pareto optimal. For scalarizations that are merely non-decreasing, such as the maximum, a dominated description may attain the same scalar value as another description. Nevertheless, as will be shown below, at least one Pareto-optimal minimizer can always be selected.

A further consideration concerns the dependence of Pareto optimality on the reference universal machine. Deficiency and surplus are invariant under changes of reference machine only up to additive constants. Since Pareto dominance depends on exact numerical inequalities, comparisons between descriptions whose values differ by only a few bits may change under a different reference machine. Accordingly, the reference description system
and complexity measure are assumed fixed throughout the analysis.

Minimal descriptions provide natural ideal benchmarks for the mismodel problem. Recall that, for every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$, $d_D(r,d^\ast)=O(1)$ and $s_D(r,d^\ast)=O(1)$. Thus, minimal descriptions have negligible deficiency and surplus, although this does not imply that every minimal description is Pareto optimal under exact numerical comparison.

% Subsection: Scalar Mismodel

\subsection*{Scalar Mismodel}

Although the Pareto frontier captures the full trade-off structure, many applications require assigning a single value to the mismodel of each description. A natural scalarization is to penalize the larger of the two components.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:scalar_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. The \emph{mismodel}\index{Mismodel} of $d$ with respect to $r$ is defined by
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\bigl\{d_D(r,d),\,s_D(r,d)\bigr\}.
\]
\end{definition}

This definition corresponds to the Chebyshev scalarization of a bi-objective optimization problem. It assigns to each description the magnitude of its dominant defect, thereby penalizing descriptions with a substantial deficiency or surplus even when the other component is small.

The following proposition establishes the basic bounds on mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mismodel_bounds}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
0\le\nu(r,d) \le \max\{K(r),K(d)\}+O(1).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\min_{d\in\mathcal D_r}\nu(r,d)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the basic properties of deficiency and surplus,
\[
0 \le d_D(r,d)\le K(r)+O(1),
\]
and
\[
0 \le s_D(r,d)\le K(d)+O(1).
\]
Taking the maximum of these inequalities gives
\[
0\le\nu(r,d) \le \max\{K(r),K(d)\}+O(1).
\]
For the second statement, choose any $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$. Since a minimal description has both deficiency and surplus bounded by a constant,
\[
\nu(r,d^\ast) = \max\{d_D(r,d^\ast),s_D(r,d^\ast)\} = O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
0 \le \min_{d\in\mathcal D_r}\nu(r,d) \le \nu(r,d^\ast) = O(1),
\]
which establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that mismodel is always finite and is controlled by the greater of the complexities of the representation and its description. It also establishes that the unrestricted theoretical mismodel problem always admits a description with negligible mismodel. The difficulty in scientific practice is therefore not the existence of such descriptions, but our inability to identify them effectively. For restricted collections of descriptions, the minimum mismodel may be substantially larger, since the available model classes need not contain any minimal description.

A useful property of the Chebyshev scalarization is that, for every $t\ge 0$,
\[
\nu(r,d)\le t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_D(r,d)\le t \text{ and } s_D(r,d)\le t.
\]
Thus, $\nu(r,d)$ is the smallest non-negative number that simultaneously bounds both components. Minimizing mismodel amounts to finding the smallest common bound on deficiency and surplus.

The maximum also favors balanced solutions. Indeed, for any non-negative numbers $a,b$,
\[
\max\{a,b\} = \frac{a+b+|a-b|}{2}.
\]
Thus, among descriptions with the same sum of deficiency and surplus, Chebyshev mismodel prefers those with a smaller difference between the two components.

The following proposition establishes that minimizing Chebyshev mismodel is compatible with Pareto optimality.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:chebyshev_attains_min_on_pareto}
For every representation $r\in\mathcal R$, there exists a Pareto-optimal description $d^\ast\in\mathcal P_r$
such that
\[
\nu(r,d^\ast) = \min_{d\in\mathcal D_r}\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\mathcal D_r$ is non-empty and mismodel takes values in $\mathbb N$, the minimum
\[
M=\min_{d\in\mathcal D_r}\nu(r,d)
\]
exists. Consider the non-empty set of minimizers
\[
\mathcal M = \{d\in\mathcal D_r:\nu(r,d)=M\}.
\]
Choose $d^\ast\in\mathcal M$ minimizing the sum $d_D(r,d) + s_D(r,d)$ over $\mathcal M$. Such a minimum exists because both components take non-negative integer values. Suppose, for contradiction, that $d^\ast$ is dominated. Then there exists $u\in\mathcal D_r$ such that $d_D(r,u)\le d_D(r,d^\ast)$ and $s_D(r,u)\le s_D(r,d^\ast)$, with at least one strict inequality. Consequently,
\[
\nu(r,u)\le\nu(r,d^\ast)=M.
\]
Since $M$ is the minimum possible mismodel, we must have $\nu(r,u)=M$, and therefore
$u\in\mathcal M$. However, dominance also implies
\[
d_D(r,u)+s_D(r,u) < d_D(r,d^\ast)+s_D(r,d^\ast),
\]
contradicting the choice of $d^\ast$. Therefore $d^\ast$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proof}

Thus, although the maximum may admit dominated minimizers, there always exists a Pareto-optimal description attaining the minimum scalar mismodel.

% Subsection: Mismodel and Algorithmic Information Distance

\subsection*{Mismodel and Algorithmic Information Distance}

The definition of mismodel combines two directed conditional Kolmogorov complexities. This suggests a connection with algorithmic information distance, which measures the information needed to transform one string into another and vice versa. To relate this quantity to mismodel, we first establish a property of minimal descriptions.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:minimal_descriptions_mutually_recoverable}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d_1^\ast,d_2^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be two minimal descriptions of $r$. Then
\[
K(d_1^\ast\mid d_2^\ast)=O(1),
\]
and
\[
K(d_2^\ast\mid d_1^\ast)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Since $d^\ast$ is a minimal description, we have $\Gamma(d^\ast)=r$ and $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r)$. By the prefix-complexity chain rule,
\[
K(r,d^\ast) = K(r)+K(d^\ast\mid r,K(r))+O(1).
\]
Since $r$ is computable from $d^\ast$,
\[
K(r,d^\ast)=K(d^\ast)+O(1).
\]
Moreover, under the fixed optimal universal description system,
\[
K(d^\ast)\le\ell(d^\ast)+O(1)
=
K(r)+O(1).
\]
Combining these relationships gives
\[
K(d^\ast\mid r,K(r))=O(1).
\]
Now let $d_1^\ast,d_2^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$. Given $d_2^\ast$, a fixed program can recover $r$ by executing the description and recover $K(r)$ by reading its length. Since
\[
K(d_1^\ast\mid r,K(r))=O(1),
\]
we obtain
\[
K(d_1^\ast\mid d_2^\ast)=O(1).
\]
Exchanging the roles of $d_1^\ast$ and $d_2^\ast$ gives the second inequality.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that all minimal descriptions of a fixed representation are algorithmically equivalent up to an additive constant. Although they may be different binary strings, they contain essentially the same algorithmic information. This property allows mismodel to be characterized as an algorithmic distance from the current description to the set of minimal descriptions.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mismodel_information_distance}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
\nu(r,d) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast} E(d,d^\ast)+O(1),
\]
where
\[
E(d,d^\ast) = \max\{K(d^\ast\mid d),K(d\mid d^\ast)\}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\left\{ \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d^\ast\mid d),
\min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}K(d\mid d^\ast) \right\}.
\]
For any family of pairs of non-negative quantities, the maximum of their separate minima cannot exceed the minimum of their maximum. Therefore,
\[
\nu(r,d) \le \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}E(d,d^\ast).
\]
For the reverse inequality, choose $d_1^\ast,d_2^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ such that
\[
K(d_1^\ast\mid d)=d_D(r,d),
\]
and
\[
K(d\mid d_2^\ast)=s_D(r,d).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:minimal_descriptions_mutually_recoverable},
\[
K(d_2^\ast\mid d_1^\ast)=O(1).
\]
Hence, by the composition inequality for conditional Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
K(d\mid d_1^\ast) \le K(d_2^\ast\mid d_1^\ast) + K(d\mid d_2^\ast) + O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
K(d\mid d_1^\ast) \le s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
It follows that
\[
\begin{aligned}
E(d,d_1^\ast)
& = \max\{K(d_1^\ast\mid d),K(d\mid d_1^\ast)\}\\
& \le \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\}+O(1)\\
& = \nu(r,d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since
\[
\min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}E(d,d^\ast) \le E(d,d_1^\ast),
\]
we obtain
\[
\min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}E(d,d^\ast) \le \nu(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition provides a unified interpretation of mismodel: it measures, up to an additive constant, the shortest symmetric algorithmic information distance between the current extended description and the set of minimal descriptions of the target representation. The result is significant because deficiency and surplus are defined through separate minimizations, which need not initially select the same minimal description. Nevertheless, the algorithmic equivalence of minimal descriptions ensures that these two directions can be combined without changing mismodel by more than an additive constant. Thus, mismodel may be understood as a distance to descriptive optimality, rather than merely as an arbitrary aggregation of two error components.

% Subsection: Mismodel of Exact Descriptions

\subsection*{Mismodel of Exact Descriptions}

A particularly important case occurs when the description already generates the target representation exactly. An exact description may reproduce the target without error while still containing unnecessary algorithmic information or failing to reveal its shortest description. The following proposition provides a characterization of mismodel in this case.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mismodel_exact_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_r$ be an exact description of $r$. Then
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\left\{ K(K(r)\mid d), K(d)-K(r) \right\} + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From the properties of deficiency established in Section~\ref{sec:deficiency_description}, we have
\[
d_D(r,d)=K(K(r)\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Similarly, from the properties of surplus established in Section~\ref{sec:surplus_description}, we have
\[
s_D(r,d)=K(d)-K(r)+O(1).
\] 
Substituting these expressions into the definition of mismodel gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu(r,d)
& = \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\}\\
& = \max\left\{K(K(r)\mid d), K(d)-K(r) \right\} + O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition reveals two distinct sources of mismodel even when a description reproduces the target representation exactly. The first term $K(K(r)\mid d)$ measures the information required to determine the minimum description length of $r$ from the current description. The second term $K(d)-K(r)$ measures the additional algorithmic complexity of the description beyond that of its output. Consequently, exact reconstruction does not guarantee negligible mismodel. A description may generate the target representation perfectly while containing substantial independent algorithmic information that is unnecessary for reconstructing it. Conversely, a description may be considerably longer than a minimal description while having
negligible mismodel, provided that it is algorithmically equivalent to some minimal description.

This distinction is important for scientific modeling. A model that reproduces all available observations need not provide an economical or optimal computational account of them. Exactness is a property of the output, whereas mismodel concerns the algorithmic relationship between the complete description and the class of minimal descriptions.

% Subsection: Negligible Mismodel

\subsection*{Negligible Mismodel}

The characterization of mismodel as algorithmic information distance allows us to state precisely what it means for an extended description to have negligible mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mismodel_equiv_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. The following statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\nu(r,d)=O(1)$.
\item There exists $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ such that $K(d^\ast\mid d)=O(1)$ and $K(d\mid d^\ast)=O(1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $\nu(r,d)=O(1)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:mismodel_information_distance},
\[
\min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}E(d,d^\ast)=O(1).
\]
Since $\mathcal D_r^\ast$ is non-empty, there exists some $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ such that $E(d,d^\ast)=O(1)$. By the definition of $E$, $K(d^\ast\mid d)=O(1)$ and $K(d\mid d^\ast)=O(1)$.

Conversely, suppose that such a minimal description $d^\ast$ exists. By the definitions of deficiency and surplus,
\[
d_D(r,d)\le K(d^\ast\mid d)=O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d)\le K(d\mid d^\ast)=O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\} = O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that an extended description has negligible mismodel precisely when it is algorithmically equivalent, up to a constant amount of information, to some minimal description of the target representation. Such equivalence does not require the current description to have minimum length, nor does
it require the description to generate the target representation exactly. What matters is that the current description and some minimal description can be recovered from one another using only constant additional algorithmic information.

A further consequence concerns the stability of mismodel under reversible transformations of descriptions.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:mismodel_reversible_recoding}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d,s\in\mathcal D_r$ be two extended descriptions of $r$. If $K(d\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid d)=O(1)$, then
\[
\nu(r,d)=\nu(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By the equivalence properties of deficiency and surplus established in the preceding sections,
\[
d_D(r,d)=d_D(r,s)+O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d)=s_D(r,s)+O(1).
\]
Taking the maximum of both components gives
\[
\nu(r,d)=\nu(r,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This corollary shows that mismodel depends on the algorithmic information contained in a description rather than on its superficial syntactic form. In particular, reversible computable recodings implemented by fixed procedures do not substantially change mismodel, provided that both descriptions remain extended descriptions of the same target representation.

% Subsection: Comparison with Alternative Scalarizations

\subsection*{Comparison with Alternative Scalarizations}

The maximum is not the only possible way to combine deficiency and surplus into a scalar quantity. As discussed in the chapter on miscoding and in Section~\ref{sec:multiobjective_optimization}, different scalarizations express different preferences regarding the relative importance of competing objectives.

A general alternative is the weighted power-mean family
\[
\nu^{(p,\lambda)}(r,d) = \left(\lambda\,d_D(r,d)^p + (1-\lambda)\,s_D(r,d)^p \right)^{1/p},
\]
where $p\ge 1$ and $0<\lambda<1$. For $p=1$, this corresponds to a weighted arithmetic mean, which measures the aggregate burden of deficiency and surplus. For $p=2$, it gives a weighted root-mean-square criterion, which penalizes large individual
components more strongly. As $p\to\infty$, the expression converges to the Chebyshev scalarization:
\[
\lim_{p\to\infty}\nu^{(p,\lambda)}(r,d) = \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\} = \nu(r,d).
\]
The parameter $\lambda$ determines the relative importance assigned to deficiency and surplus for finite $p$. Such weighting may be useful when one component is considered more serious than the other in a particular scientific application. However, the selection of $\lambda$ and $p$ introduces preferences that are not determined by the mathematical definitions of deficiency and surplus themselves.

The Chebyshev scalarization is therefore adopted as the canonical scalar measure of mismodel in this book. It treats both components symmetrically and prevents a large value of either component from being compensated by a small value of the other. Alternative scalarizations remain useful as application-dependent decision criteria,
particularly when the scientific objectives justify an asymmetric treatment of the two components.

\section{Relative Mismodel}
\label{sec:relative_mismodel}

The quantity
\[
\nu(r,d)=\max\{d_D(r,d),\,s_D(r,d)\}
\]
measures the mismodel of an extended description $d\in\mathcal D_r$ with respect to a target representation $r\in\mathcal R$ in absolute terms, that is, in bits. This absolute measure is appropriate for the structural theory developed in the previous sections. However, one may wish to compare descriptions of different complexities, or even descriptions associated with different target representations. For this purpose, it is useful to introduce a normalized, dimensionless version of mismodel. The idea is to divide absolute mismodel by a complexity scale that accounts for both the description under consideration and the target representation. This yields a relative score that is less sensitive to differences in scale and therefore more suitable for comparing descriptive discrepancies across different problems.

Recall that, under the fixed optimal universal description system, every minimal description $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ satisfies $K(d^\ast) = K(r) + O(1)$. Consequently, the Kolmogorov complexity of the target representation provides an intrinsic complexity scale for its minimal descriptions. Unlike the admissible representational complexity floor used in relative miscoding, no separate complexity floor is therefore needed at the descriptive level.

\begin{definition}
\index{Relative mismodel}
\label{def:relative_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r$ be an extended description of $r$. We define the \emph{relative mismodel} of $d$ with respect to $r$ as
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) = \frac{\nu(r,d)}{\max\{K(d),K(r)\}} =
\frac{\max\{d_D(r,d),\,s_D(r,d)\}} {\max\{K(d),K(r)\}}.
\]
\end{definition}

The denominator is strictly positive under the fixed optimal universal prefix-free machine used throughout the theory. It provides a common scale for comparing the algorithmic information contained in the description with the information required to describe the target representation optimally.

\begin{notation}
For convenience, we denote the scale by
\[
C_r(d) = \max\{K(d),K(r)\}.
\]
\end{notation}

The normalization preserves the interpretation of absolute mismodel while expressing its magnitude relative to the complexity of the objects being compared. In particular, the same absolute mismodel may be substantial for a simple description but negligible relative to a much more complex one.

The first proposition establishes the basic bounds on relative mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:range_rel_mismodel}
For every representation $r\in\mathcal R$ and every extended description $d\in\mathcal D_r$,
\[
0 \le \nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) \le 1+O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right),
\]
where the constant implicit in the $O$-notation is independent of $r$ and $d$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the basic properties of deficiency and surplus, $d_D(r,d) \ge 0$ and $s_D(r,d) \ge 0$. Consequently,
\[
\nu(r,d)=\max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\} \ge 0,
\]
and therefore $\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d)\ge 0$.

For the upper bound, recall that
\[
d_D(r,d)\le K(r)+O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d)\le K(d)+O(1).
\]
Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu(r,d)
& = \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\}\\
& \le \max\{K(r),K(d)\}+O(1)\\
& = C_r(d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Dividing by $C_r(d)>0$ gives
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) \le 1+O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that relative mismodel behaves as a normalized discrepancy measure. For sufficiently complex descriptions or target representations, its upper bound approaches $1$. Values close to $0$ indicate that the dominant descriptive defect is small relative to the intrinsic complexity scale, whereas values close to $1$ indicate that the defect is of approximately the same magnitude as that scale. Relative mismodel is not necessarily bounded above by $1$ exactly. The additive constants associated with Kolmogorov complexity may produce values slightly greater than $1$, particularly for descriptions of low complexity.

The next result connects relative mismodel with the theoretical ideal of negligible absolute mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:small_abs_implies_small_rel_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. If $\nu(r,d)\le c$ for a fixed constant $c$ independent of $r$ and $d$, then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d)\le\frac{c}{C_r(d)}.
\]
Consequently, for any sequence of representation-description pairs $(r_n,d_n)$ satisfying $\nu(r_n,d_n)=O(1)$ uniformly in $n$, and $C_{r_n}(d_n)\to\infty$, we have
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r_n,d_n)\to 0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Definition~\ref{def:relative_mismodel},
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) = \frac{\nu(r,d)}{C_r(d)}.
\]
If $\nu(r,d)\le c$, then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d)\le\frac{c}{C_r(d)}.
\]
For a sequence with uniformly bounded absolute mismodel and $C_{r_n}(d_n)\to\infty$, the right-hand side tends to zero, establishing the second statement.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that negligible absolute mismodel implies negligible relative mismodel when the intrinsic complexity scale becomes sufficiently large. In particular, since every minimal description $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ satisfies $\nu(r,d^\ast)=O(1)$, we obtain
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d^\ast) = O\left(\frac{1}{K(r)}\right).
\]
Thus, minimal descriptions have asymptotically negligible relative mismodel as the complexity of their target representations increases.

% Subsection: Connection with Normalized Information Distance

\subsection*{Connection with Normalized Information Distance}

In the previous section, mismodel was characterized in terms of symmetric algorithmic information distance. This relationship suggests a natural connection between relative mismodel and normalized information distance. To establish this connection, we represent the information contained in a minimal description of $r$ by the pair
\[
y_r =\langle r,K(r)\rangle.
\]
This pair specifies both the target representation and the length of its shortest description. As established in Section~\ref{sec:mismodel_description}, it is algorithmically equivalent, up to an additive constant, to any minimal description of $r$.

The following proposition shows that relative mismodel coincides with the normalized information distance between the current description and this pair, up to an asymptotically negligible term.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:relative_mismodel_normalized_distance}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$.
Then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) = \operatorname{NID}(d,y_r) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right),
\]
where $y_r=\langle r,K(r)\rangle$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First, we observe that
\[
K(y_r)=K(r)+O(1).
\]
Then, $r$ can be recovered from $y_r$ by a fixed computable procedure, so
\[
K(r)\le K(y_r)+O(1).
\]
Conversely, a minimal description of $r$ determines both $r$ and $K(r)$, and therefore
\[
K(y_r)\le K(r)+O(1).
\]
Next let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be any minimal description of $r$. Given $d^\ast$, a fixed procedure can recover $r$ by executing the description and recover $K(r)$ by reading its length. Consequently,
\[
K(y_r\mid d^\ast)=O(1).
\]
Conversely, given $y_r$, one can enumerate all candidate descriptions of length $K(r)$ and simulate them until one generates $r$. Hence some minimal description is computable from $y_r$ with constant overhead. Since all minimal descriptions of $r$ are mutually recoverable with constant overhead,
\[
K(d^\ast\mid y_r)=O(1).
\]
Therefore $d^\ast$ and $y_r$ are algorithmically equivalent up to an additive constant, and
\[
E(d,d^\ast)=E(d,y_r)+O(1).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:mismodel_information_distance},
\[
\nu(r,d) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast}E(d,d^\ast)+O(1).
\]
It follows that
\[
\nu(r,d) = E(d,y_r) + O(1).
\]
Furthermore,
\[
\max\{K(d),K(y_r)\} = \max\{K(d),K(r)\}+O(1) = C_r(d)+O(1).
\]
Since both $\nu(r,d)$ and $E(d,y_r)$ are bounded above by $C_r(d)+O(1)$, replacing either the numerator or denominator by a quantity differing by $O(1)$ changes the normalized ratio by at most $O(1/C_r(d))$. Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d)
& = \frac{\nu(r,d)}{C_r(d)}\\
& = \frac{E(d,y_r)}{\max\{K(d),K(y_r)\}} + O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right)\\
& = \operatorname{NID}(d,y_r) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that relative mismodel is, up to an asymptotically negligible additive term, the normalized algorithmic information distance between the current description and the information required to identify a minimal description of the target representation. The result provides a stronger justification for the normalization than scale adjustment alone. The denominator $\max\{K(d),K(r)\}$ is precisely the complexity scale naturally associated with normalized information distance. Thus, absolute mismodel measures the algorithmic distance to descriptive optimality, while relative mismodel measures that distance in proportion to the complexity of the objects being compared.

A particularly informative case occurs when the description reconstructs the target representation exactly.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:relative_mismodel_exact_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_r$ be an exact description of $r$. Then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) = \frac{ \max\{K(K(r)\mid d),\,K(d)-K(r)\} }{ \max\{K(d),K(r)\} }
+ O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For an exact description, the characterization of mismodel established in Section~\ref{sec:mismodel_description} gives
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\{K(K(r)\mid d),K(d)-K(r)\}+O(1).
\]
Dividing by $C_r(d)=\max\{K(d),K(r)\}$ yields the result.
\end{proof}

This corollary confirms that exact reconstruction does not necessarily imply negligible relative mismodel. An exact description may contain substantial independent algorithmic information beyond what is required to reconstruct its target. In that case, its surplus, and consequently its relative mismodel, may remain large even though its output is perfectly correct.

% Subsection: Absolute and Relative Mismodel

\subsection*{Absolute and Relative Mismodel}

Absolute and relative mismodel provide complementary perspectives on the quality of a description. Absolute mismodel quantifies the algorithmic information required to move between the current description and the set of minimal descriptions. Relative mismodel expresses that information as a proportion of the intrinsic complexity scale.

An important consequence is that negligible relative mismodel need not imply negligible absolute mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:small_rel_not_small_abs_mismodel}
There exist representational domains and sequences of pairs $(r_n,d_n)$, with $r_n\in\mathcal R$ and $d_n \in \mathcal D_{r_n}$, such that
\[
\nu(r_n,d_n)\to\infty,
\]
while
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r_n,d_n)\to 0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider a representational domain containing a sequence of representations $(r_n)$ whose Kolmogorov complexities tend to infinity, that is, $K(r_n)\to\infty$. For every $n$, choose a minimal description $d_n^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r_n}^\ast$. Let $m_n=\left\lfloor\sqrt{K(r_n)}\right\rfloor$. Choose a binary string $z_n$ of length $m_n$ such that
\[
K(z_n\mid r_n,K(r_n))\ge m_n-O(1).
\]
Such strings exist by the incompressibility argument: among binary strings of a given length, not all can have conditional descriptions substantially shorter than that length. Construct a description $d_n$ by means of a fixed computable encoding of the pair $\langle d_n^\ast,z_n\rangle$ with the following properties: both components can be recovered effectively from $d_n$, and the computation encoded by $d_n$ executes $d_n^\ast$
while ignoring $z_n$. The construction can be implemented within the fixed universal prefix-free description system using a self-delimiting encoding of the two components. By construction,
\[
\Gamma(d_n)=\Gamma(d_n^\ast)=r_n.
\]
Consequently,
\[
d_n\in\bar{\mathcal D}_{r_n} \subseteq\mathcal D_{r_n}.
\]
Since $d_n^\ast$ can be recovered from $d_n$ by a fixed computable procedure,
\[
K(d_n^\ast\mid d_n)=O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
d_D(r_n,d_n)=O(1).
\]
On the other hand, the description $d_n$ contains both $r_n$ and the independent information $z_n$
in effectively recoverable form. By the chain rule for prefix Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
\begin{aligned}
K(d_n)
& \ge K(r_n,z_n)-O(1)\\
& = K(r_n)+K(z_n\mid r_n,K(r_n))-O(1)\\
& \ge K(r_n)+m_n-O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Moreover, since $d_n$ is constructed computably from $d_n^\ast$ and $z_n$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
K(d_n)
& \le K(d_n^\ast)+K(z_n)+O(1)\\
& \le K(r_n)+m_n+O(\log(m_n+2)).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $d_n$ is an exact description of $r_n$, the characterization of surplus gives
\[
s_D(r_n,d_n)=K(d_n)-K(r_n)+O(1).
\]
Combining the previous bounds,
\[
m_n-O(1) \le s_D(r_n,d_n) \le m_n+O(\log(m_n+2)).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\nu(r_n,d_n) = \max\{d_D(r_n,d_n),s_D(r_n,d_n)\} = \Theta(m_n).
\]
Since $m_n\to\infty$, it follows that $\nu(r_n,d_n)\to\infty$. However,
\[
C_{r_n}(d_n) = \max\{K(d_n),K(r_n)\} \ge K(r_n).
\]
Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r_n,d_n)
& = \frac{\nu(r_n,d_n)}{C_{r_n}(d_n)}\\
& \le \frac{O(m_n)}{K(r_n)}\\
& = O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K(r_n)}}\right) \to 0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, the absolute mismodel diverges while the relative mismodel tends to zero.
\end{proof}

This proposition demonstrates that relative closeness to descriptive optimality does not imply absolute algorithmic closeness. The algorithmic information distance between a description and a minimal description may increase without bound, while remaining negligible relative to the overall complexity of the target.

The distinction is important when comparing descriptions of different scales. A discrepancy of several thousand bits may be substantial for a simple model but relatively insignificant for an extremely complex one. For a fixed target representation, however, the relationship between absolute and relative mismodel is more restrictive.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fixed_target_rigidity_rel_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be fixed, and let $(d_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a sequence of extended descriptions in $\mathcal D_r$. If $\nu(r,d_n)\to\infty$, then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_n)\to 1.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From the basic upper bound on mismodel,
\[
\nu(r,d_n) \le \max\{K(d_n),K(r)\}+O(1).
\]
Since $r$ is fixed, $K(r)$ is constant. The assumption $\nu(r,d_n)\to\infty$ therefore implies $K(d_n)\to\infty$. For sufficiently large $n$, $K(d_n) > K(r)$, so $C_r(d_n) = K(d_n)$. By the lower bound on surplus,
\[
s_D(r,d_n)\ge K(d_n)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
Since $\nu(r,d_n)\ge s_D(r,d_n)$, we obtain
\[
\nu(r,d_n)\ge K(d_n)-K(r)-O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_n) \ge 1-\frac{K(r)+O(1)}{K(d_n)}.
\]
On the other hand, by the upper bound on relative mismodel,
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_n) \le 1+O\left(\frac{1}{K(d_n)}\right).
\]
Since $K(r)$ is fixed and $K(d_n)\to\infty$, both bounds converge to $1$. Therefore,
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_n)\to1.
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that, for a fixed target representation, descriptions with increasingly large absolute mismodel eventually have relative mismodel approaching one. Intuitively, once the complexity of the description
greatly exceeds that of the target, most of its algorithmic information cannot be accounted for by a minimal description of the target representation.

This result complements the preceding proposition. When target complexity grows together with descriptive complexity, absolute mismodel may increase while relative mismodel decreases. When the target is fixed, however, unbounded absolute mismodel necessarily produces relative mismodel approaching one.

% Subsection: Invariance under Computable Recoding

\subsection*{Invariance under Computable Recoding}

A normalized measure of descriptive discrepancy should not depend substantially on superficial changes in the encoding of a description. In particular, a fixed reversible computable transformation preserves the algorithmic information contained in a description up to an additive constant. The following proposition establishes the corresponding property for relative mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:relative_mismodel_recoding}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, and let $f:\mathcal B^\ast\to\mathcal B^\ast$ be a fixed total computable bijection with
computable inverse. Suppose that $d\in\mathcal D_r$ and $f(d)\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,f(d)) = \nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right),
\]
where the additive constant may depend on $f$ and its inverse, but not on $r$ or $d$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $f$ and its inverse are fixed computable functions, $K(f(d)\mid d)=O(1)$ and $K(d\mid f(d))=O(1)$. By the invariance of mismodel under reversible computable recoding, established in the previous section,
\[
\nu(r,f(d))=\nu(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Furthermore, invariance of Kolmogorov complexity under fixed computable bijections with computable inverses gives
\[
K(f(d))=K(d)+O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
C_r(f(d))
& = \max\{K(f(d)),K(r)\}\\
& = \max\{K(d),K(r)\}+O(1)\\
& = C_r(d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since
\[
\nu(r,d)\le C_r(d)+O(1),
\]
we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,f(d))
& = \frac{\nu(r,f(d))}{C_r(f(d))}\\
& = \frac{\nu(r,d)+O(1)}{C_r(d)+O(1)}\\
& = \nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_r(d)}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that relative mismodel is stable under reversible computable transformations that preserve the extended-description relationship with the target representation. The requirement that both $d$ and $f(d)$ belong to $\mathcal D_r$ is important: an arbitrary recoding of a description need not preserve
its status as an extended description of the same representation. In practical terms, the proposition establishes that fixed reversible changes of serialization or computational encoding do not substantially
affect relative mismodel, apart from the machine-dependent overhead.

The same principle applies to comparisons between descriptions having an identical normalization scale. Indeed, if $C_r(d_1) = C_r(d_2)$, then
\[
\nu(r,d_1)\le\nu(r,d_2) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_1) \le\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d_2).
\]

Thus, relative mismodel preserves absolute rankings whenever the compared descriptions have the same intrinsic complexity scale. When their scales differ, however, normalization may alter their ordering.

Finally, relative mismodel preserves the bottleneck interpretation of absolute mismodel. Since the denominator is common to both components, we have
\[
\nu^{\mathrm{rel}}(r,d) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{d_D(r,d)}{C_r(d)},
& d_D(r,d)\ge s_D(r,d), \\[10pt] \dfrac{s_D(r,d)}{C_r(d)},
& s_D(r,d)>d_D(r,d).
\end{cases}
\]

Consequently, relative mismodel identifies the same dominant descriptive defect as absolute mismodel, while expressing its magnitude relative to the complexity of the description and target representation.

\section{Decreasing Mismodel}
\label{sec:decreasing_mismodel}

For a fixed target representation $r$, mismodel measures how far an extended description $d \in \mathcal{D}_r$ is from being algorithmically equivalent to a minimal description of $r$. In the theory developed above, this discrepancy is decomposed into two components. The deficiency $d_D(r, d)$ measures how much additional algorithmic information is required to recover a minimal description of $r$ from $d$, whereas the surplus $s_D(r, d)$ measures how much additional algorithmic information is required to recover $d$ from a minimal description of $r$.

Decreasing mismodel is therefore intrinsically a two-objective refinement problem: one aims to reduce missing descriptive information while also removing descriptively extraneous content. In practice, these two goals correspond to two complementary modes of improvement. Enrichment (adding information to a description) is typically aimed at reducing deficiency, whereas simplification (removing or normalizing parts of a description) is typically aimed at reducing surplus.

A specific feature of the description setting is that not every raw edit of a binary string preserves validity. Accordingly, the practically relevant transformations considered below are computable operations that map valid descriptions to valid descriptions.

% Subsection: Augmentation and Deficiency

\subsection*{Augmentation and Deficiency}

We now examine how the deficiency of a description may be reduced through augmentation. Recall that descriptive deficiency measures the algorithmic information required to recover a minimal description of a target representation from a given extended description. Augmentation may supply missing information through additional computational structures, auxiliary data, or explanatory relationships, thereby facilitating the recovery of a minimal description.

Throughout this subsection, let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a target representation, $d\in\mathcal D_r$ an extended description of $r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$ an auxiliary description. We restrict attention to augmentations for which $u=\operatorname{aug}(d,a)\in\mathcal D_r$, since descriptive deficiency is defined relative to a fixed target representation. This restriction is not part of the general augmentation operator, which may also produce descriptions associated with different representations or serve as an intermediate step in a sequence of transformations.

The information-preservation property of augmentation constrains how much descriptive deficiency can change. Since the original description remains recoverable, augmentation cannot substantially increase deficiency. Moreover, the reduction in deficiency is bounded by the additional algorithmic information supplied by the auxiliary description.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:augmentation_deficiency_bound}
\label{prop:augmentation_deficiency_monotonic}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$. Suppose that $u=\operatorname{aug}(d,a)$ is defined and belongs to $\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,d)-K(a\mid d)-O(1) \le d_D(r,u) \le d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the information-loss bound for descriptive deficiency,
\[
d_D(r,d)
\le
d_D(r,u)+K(u\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Since augmentation is a fixed partial computable operation, the augmented description $u$ can be computed from $d$ once the auxiliary description $a$ is supplied. Therefore,
\[
K(u\mid d)\le K(a\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Combining these inequalities gives
\[
d_D(r,d) \le d_D(r,u)+K(a\mid d)+O(1),
\]
and hence
\[
d_D(r,u) \ge d_D(r,d)-K(a\mid d)-O(1).
\]
For the upper bound, the information-preservation property of augmentation guarantees $K(d\mid u)=O(1)$.

Applying the information-loss bound in the opposite direction, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_D(r,u)
& \le d_D(r,d)+K(d\mid u)+O(1)\\
& =d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Combining both bounds establishes the result.
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes two fundamental properties of augmentation. First, deficiency cannot increase by more than an additive constant. Second, the amount by which deficiency can decrease is bounded, up to an additive constant, by $K(a\mid d)$, the additional algorithmic information supplied by the auxiliary description. In particular, if $K(a\mid d)=O(1)$, then
\[
d_D(r,u)=d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Thus, incorporating information that is already computably recoverable from the original description cannot substantially change its deficiency. A significant reduction requires additional information that was not already available in $d$.

To quantify the contribution of an augmentation, we introduce the difference between deficiency before and after the operation.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:marginal_deficiency_reduction}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$. Suppose that $\operatorname{aug}(d,a)$ is defined and belongs to $\mathcal D_r$. We define the \emph{marginal deficiency reduction}\index{Marginal deficiency reduction of a description} of $a$ relative to $d$ and $r$ as
\[
\Delta_D^{\mathrm{aug}}(r,d,a) = d_D(r,d) - d_D(r,\operatorname{aug}(d,a)).
\]
\end{definition}

A positive marginal deficiency reduction indicates that augmentation decreases deficiency, whereas a negative value indicates that deficiency increases. A zero value indicates that deficiency remains unchanged. Proposition~\ref{prop:augmentation_deficiency_bound} immediately gives
\[
-O(1) \le \Delta_D^{\mathrm{aug}}(r,d,a) \le K(a\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Thus, augmentation cannot substantially worsen deficiency, while the maximum possible improvement is controlled by the additional algorithmic information available in the auxiliary description. The sign convention is opposite to that of the corresponding component of the change vector introduced in the chapter on decreasing nescience, where changes are defined as the new value minus the previous one.

A particularly important case occurs when the auxiliary description supplies sufficient information to recover a
minimal description of the target. Since augmentation preserves the original description, the remaining question is how much of the auxiliary information needed for this recovery remains available in the augmented
description. The following proposition provides a general bound without requiring the complete auxiliary description to be recoverable.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:augmentation_to_constant_deficiency}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$. Suppose that $u=\operatorname{aug}(d,a)$ is defined and belongs to $\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,u) \le \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast} K(d^\ast\mid d,a) + K(a\mid u)+O(1).
\]
In particular, if there exists $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ such that $K(d^\ast\mid d,a)=O(1)$ and $K(a\mid u)=O(1)$, then
\[
d_D(r,u)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the information-preservation property of augmentation, the original description $d$ can be recovered from $u$
with constant overhead $K(d\mid u)=O(1)$. Consequently, one can recover the pair $(d,a)$ from $u$ by first reconstructing $d$ and then reconstructing $a$ using a conditional program. Therefore,
\[
K(d,a\mid u)\le K(a\mid u)+O(1).
\]
Let $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast$ be arbitrary. Given $u$, one can first reconstruct $(d,a)$ and then use a shortest conditional program to recover $d^\ast$ from that pair. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
K(d^\ast\mid u)
& \le K(d^\ast\mid d,a)+K(d,a\mid u)+O(1)\\
& \le K(d^\ast\mid d,a)+K(a\mid u)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking minima over all minimal descriptions gives
\[
d_D(r,u) \le \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_r^\ast} K(d^\ast\mid d,a) + K(a\mid u)+O(1).
\]
If both $K(d^\ast\mid d,a)=O(1)$ for some minimal description $d^\ast$ and $K(a\mid u)=O(1)$, then the right-hand side is bounded by a constant. Therefore $d_D(r,u)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

The proposition characterizes a sufficient condition for augmentation to achieve negligible deficiency. The combined information of the original and auxiliary descriptions must permit recovery of a minimal description, and the auxiliary information required for that recovery must remain available from the augmented description. The condition $K(a\mid u)=O(1)$ is sufficient but not necessary. An augmentation may retain only the relevant
information from $a$ while discarding the remainder. If that retained information is sufficient to recover a minimal description, deficiency may still become negligible. Moreover, improved agreement between the output of a model and the target representation does not, by itself, establish that descriptive deficiency has decreased. The relevant property is the algorithmic recoverability of a minimal description.

\begin{example}
Let $r$ encode observations of the trajectory of a dynamical system, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r$ describe a computational model using approximate initial conditions. Consider two auxiliary descriptions. The first, $a_1$, contains additional explanatory material about the numerical method. The second, $a_2$, specifies more
precise initial conditions. Suppose that both augmentations are defined and produce extended descriptions of $r$, namely $u_1=\operatorname{aug}(d,a_1)$ and $u_2=\operatorname{aug}(d,a_2)$. If the information contained in $a_1$ is already computable from $d$ with constant overhead, then $K(a_1\mid d)=O(1)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:augmentation_deficiency_bound},
\[
d_D(r,u_1)=d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
By contrast, $a_2$ may supply information that was not recoverable from the original description. Suppose that the pair $(d,a_2)$ permits a minimal description $d^\ast$ of $r$ to be recovered with constant overhead $K(d^\ast\mid d,a_2)=O(1)$. If the augmented description also retains sufficient information to recover $a_2$, so that $K(a_2\mid u_2)=O(1)$, then Proposition~\ref{prop:augmentation_to_constant_deficiency} gives $d_D(r,u_2)=O(1)$.
\end{example}

The example illustrates the distinction between adding redundant information and supplying information that can reduce deficiency. Whether the added initial conditions satisfy the required algorithmic properties depends on the particular representation and descriptions under consideration.

Reducing deficiency is not sufficient to guarantee that augmentation improves mismodel. Since
\[
\nu(r,d)=\max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\},
\]
a decrease in deficiency may be offset by an increase in surplus. The following proposition establishes the corresponding bounds on the surplus of an augmented description.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:augmentation_surplus_growth}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$. Suppose that $u=\operatorname{aug}(d,a)$ is defined and belongs to $\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d)-O(1) \le s_D(r,u) \le s_D(r,d)+K(a\mid d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the information-growth bound for descriptive surplus,
\[
s_D(r,u) \le s_D(r,d) + K(u\mid d) + O(1).
\]
Since augmentation is a fixed computable operation,
\[
K(u\mid d)\le K(a\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
s_D(r,u) \le s_D(r,d) + K(a\mid d) + O(1).
\]
For the lower bound, information preservation guarantees $K(d\mid u)=O(1)$. Applying the information-growth inequality in the opposite direction gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_D(r,d)
& \le s_D(r,u)+K(d\mid u)+O(1)\\
& = s_D(r,u)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently,
\[
s_D(r,u)\ge s_D(r,d)-O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that augmentation cannot substantially eliminate surplus already present in the original description, because that description remains recoverable. However, it may introduce additional surplus, bounded by the new algorithmic information supplied by the auxiliary description. Together with the deficiency bounds, this establishes the fundamental behavior of augmentation:
\[
\begin{aligned}
-K(a\mid d)-O(1)
& \le d_D(r,u)-d_D(r,d) \le O(1),\\
-O(1)
& \le s_D(r,u)-s_D(r,d) \le K(a\mid d)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, augmentation is particularly suited to addressing descriptive deficiency. When surplus is the dominant defect, a complementary operation that permits information to be discarded may be more appropriate. Nevertheless, an augmentation that increases surplus can still reduce mismodel, provided the improvement in deficiency is sufficient.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:augmentation_decreases_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and $a\in\mathcal D^\top$. Suppose that $u=\operatorname{aug}(d,a)$ is defined and belongs to $\mathcal D_r$. Write $D=d_D(r,d)$ and $S=s_D(r,d)$, and assume that surplus is the dominant defect: $S>D$. Let $c_{\operatorname{red}}\geq 0$ be a fixed constant
\[
s_D(r,u) \le S+K(a\mid d)+c_{\operatorname{aug}}.
\]
If $d_D(r,u)<D$ and $K(a\mid d)+c_{\operatorname{aug}}<D-S$, then
\[
\nu(r,u)<\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $D>S$, the original mismodel is $\nu(r,d) = D$. By assumption $d_D(r,u) < D$. Furthermore, the surplus-growth bound gives
\[
s_D(r,u) \le S+K(a\mid d)+c_{\operatorname{aug}}.
\]
Using
\[
K(a\mid d)+c_{\operatorname{aug}}<D-S,
\]
we obtain $s_D(r,u)<D$. Consequently, both descriptive defects of the augmented description are strictly smaller than the original mismodel $d_D(r,u)<D$ and $s_D(r,u)<D$. Therefore,
\[
\nu(r,u) = \max\{d_D(r,u),s_D(r,u)\} < D =\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes a sufficient condition for deficiency-reducing augmentation to improve overall mismodel. When deficiency dominates, the difference $d_D(r,d)-s_D(r,d)$ provides a margin within which additional surplus may be introduced without preventing a strict reduction in mismodel. An augmentation may therefore reduce mismodel even when it increases surplus, provided that both resulting descriptive defects remain below the previous mismodel. The condition involving $K(a\mid d)$ is sufficient but not necessary, since the bound on surplus growth need not be attained. More generally, strict improvement in mismodel is characterized by $d_D(r,u)<\nu(r,d)$ and $s_D(r,u)<\nu(r,d)$. This is the active-defect condition established
in the chapter on decreasing nescience. It applies regardless of which descriptive defect is initially dominant.

% Subsection: Decreasing surplus via reduction

\subsection*{Decreasing Surplus via Reduction}

A natural way to decrease the surplus of a description is to simplify it by removing computational structures, parameters, or other information that is unnecessary for describing the target representation. In scientific practice, this may involve eliminating irrelevant model components, unnecessary assumptions, or auxiliary information unrelated to the observations.

Throughout this subsection, let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a target representation and $d\in\mathcal D_r$ an extended description of $r$. We restrict attention to reductions for which $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined and $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$, since descriptive surplus and deficiency are defined relative to a fixed target representation.

The computability of reduction imposes fundamental constraints on its effect on descriptive surplus. Since the reduced description can be computed from the original, reduction cannot substantially increase surplus. Moreover, the possible decrease is bounded by the algorithmic information discarded from the retained description.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_reduction_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and suppose that $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined, with $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
s_D(r,d)-K(a\mid d_1)-O(1) \leq s_D(r,d_1) \leq s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the information-growth bound for descriptive surplus,
\[
s_D(r,d_1) \leq s_D(r,d)+K(d_1\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Since reduction is a fixed partial computable operation,
\[
K(d_1\mid d)=O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
s_D(r,d_1)\leq s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
For the lower bound, applying the information-growth inequality in the opposite direction gives
\[
s_D(r,d) \leq s_D(r,d_1)+K(d\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction_information},
\[
K(d\mid d_1)=K(a\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
s_D(r,d) \leq s_D(r,d_1)+K(a\mid d_1)+O(1),
\]
and hence
\[
s_D(r,d_1) \geq
s_D(r,d)-K(a\mid d_1)-O(1).
\]
Combining both bounds establishes the result.
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes two fundamental properties of reduction. First, surplus cannot increase by more than an additive constant. Second, the amount by which surplus can decrease is bounded, up to an additive
constant, by $K(a\mid d_1)$, the information required to reconstruct the discarded description from the reduced one.

This quantity characterizes the information removed from the retained description, rather than the literal length of the discarded component. Consequently, removing a large amount of code does not necessarily produce a substantial decrease in algorithmic surplus. In particular, if
\[
K(a\mid d_1)=O(1),
\]
then
\[
s_D(r,d_1)=s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Thus, removing information that is already recoverable from the reduced description cannot substantially decrease its theoretical surplus.

To quantify the contribution of a reduction, we introduce the difference between surplus before and after the operation.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:marginal_surplus_reduction}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and suppose that $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined, with $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. We define the \emph{marginal surplus reduction} \index{Marginal surplus reduction of a description} of the reduction operator relative to $d$ and $r$ as
\[
\Delta_S^{\mathrm{red}}(r,d) = s_D(r,d)-s_D(r,d_1).
\]
\end{definition}

A positive marginal surplus reduction indicates that reduction decreases surplus, whereas a negative value indicates that surplus increases. A zero value indicates that surplus remains unchanged.

Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_reduction_description} immediately gives
\[
-O(1) \leq \Delta_S^{\mathrm{red}}(r,d) \leq K(a\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
Thus, reduction cannot substantially worsen surplus, while the maximum possible improvement is controlled by the algorithmic information removed from the retained description.

The quantity is defined relative to a fixed reduction operator. Different operators may produce different reduced descriptions and therefore different marginal surplus reductions. As with marginal deficiency reduction, positive values indicate improvement. This sign convention is opposite to that of the corresponding surplus component in the change vector introduced in the chapter on decreasing nescience, where changes are defined as the new value minus the previous one.

Although reduction cannot substantially increase surplus, it may discard information required to recover a minimal description of the target representation. Consequently, a reduction that decreases surplus may also increase descriptive deficiency. The following proposition establishes bounds on this possible information loss.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:reduction_deficiency_bound}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Suppose that $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined, with $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. Then
\[
d_D(r,d)-O(1) \leq d_D(r,d_1) \leq d_D(r,d)+K(a\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the information-loss bound for descriptive deficiency,
\[
d_D(r,d) \leq d_D(r,d_1)+K(d_1\mid d)+O(1).
\]
Since reduction is a fixed partial computable operation,
\[
K(d_1\mid d)=O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
d_D(r,d_1)\geq d_D(r,d)-O(1).
\]
Applying the information-loss inequality in the opposite direction gives
\[
d_D(r,d_1) \leq d_D(r,d)+K(d\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction_information},
\[
K(d\mid d_1)=K(a\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
d_D(r,d_1) \leq d_D(r,d)+K(a\mid d_1)+O(1).
\]
Combining both bounds establishes the result.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that reduction cannot substantially eliminate deficiency already present in the original description. However, deficiency may increase when the discarded information is relevant to recovering a minimal description of the target. Together with the surplus bounds, this establishes the fundamental behavior of reduction:
\[
\begin{aligned}
-O(1)
& \leq d_D(r,d_1)-d_D(r,d) \leq K(a\mid d_1)+O(1),\\
-K(a\mid d_1)-O(1)
& \leq s_D(r,d_1)-s_D(r,d) \leq O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
These inequalities are complementary to those established for augmentation. Whereas augmentation cannot substantially increase deficiency but may introduce surplus, reduction cannot substantially increase surplus but may introduce deficiency. Neither operation guarantees a decrease in mismodel.

A particularly important case occurs when the information removed by reduction can already be recovered from the
reduced description. In this case, reduction preserves not only the information needed to reconstruct the original description, but also its descriptive deficiency and surplus, up to additive constants.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:safe_simplification_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $d\in\mathcal D_r$, and suppose that $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined, with $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. If
\[
K(d\mid d_1)=O(1),
\]
then
\[
d_D(r,d_1)=d_D(r,d)+O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d_1)=s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\nu(r,d_1)=\nu(r,d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since reduction is computable,
\[
K(d_1\mid d)=O(1).
\]
Together with the assumption
\[
K(d\mid d_1)=O(1),
\]
this establishes that the original and reduced descriptions are mutually recoverable with constant overhead. Applying the information-loss bounds for descriptive deficiency in both directions gives
\[
d_D(r,d_1)=d_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Similarly, applying the information-growth bounds for descriptive surplus in both directions gives
\[
s_D(r,d_1)=s_D(r,d)+O(1).
\]
Since mismodel is defined by
\[
\nu(r,d)=\max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\},
\]
and both components change by at most an additive constant, we obtain
\[
\nu(r,d_1)=\nu(r,d)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition formalizes the distinction between shortening a description and eliminating algorithmically
independent information. Removing duplicated routines, macro expansions, boilerplate, or reversible syntactic structures may substantially decrease literal description length. However, if the removed information is recoverable from what remains, the theoretical deficiency and surplus cannot change substantially. Such transformations may still be useful for improving readability or computational efficiency. Nevertheless, they do not necessarily constitute improvements in theoretical mismodel. By contrast, a reduction that removes information not recoverable from the retained description may substantially decrease surplus, although it may also increase deficiency.

\begin{example}
Consider a representation $r$ encoding observations of a physical system, and let $d\in\mathcal D_r$ be a decodable description implementing a computational model of those observations. Suppose that $d$ contains an auxiliary collection of computational records that are not needed to generate its predictions. These records may include historical simulation results, unrelated experimental data, or other information independent of the model. Assume that a reduction operator separates the description into $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$, where $d_1$ retains the computational model and $a$ contains the discarded records. Suppose that the reduced description remains an extended description of the target: $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. If the discarded records contain substantial information not recoverable from the reduced description, then $K(a\mid d_1)$ may be large. The reduction may therefore eliminate a substantial amount of descriptive surplus. However, this improvement is not guaranteed merely by the amount of information discarded. Whether the removed information constitutes surplus depends on its algorithmic relationship with the minimal descriptions of $r$.

Now consider a different reduction that removes only duplicated computational routines. If those routines can be reconstructed from the reduced description with constant overhead, then $K(a\mid d_1)=O(1)$. In this case, the reduction may substantially shorten the description, but $s_D(r,d_1)=s_D(r,d)+O(1)$.
\end{example}

The example illustrates that reducing literal complexity and reducing descriptive surplus are distinct objectives. A successful reduction of surplus must eliminate algorithmically extraneous information, rather than merely shorten the encoding.

Reduction therefore provides a computational mechanism for eliminating descriptive surplus while retaining a potentially simpler description of the target representation. Its effectiveness depends on both the information discarded and the information needed to recover a minimal description from what remains. A reduction may decrease surplus while increasing deficiency, leaving overall mismodel unchanged or even increasing it.

% Subsection: Strict decrease of mismodel

\subsection*{Strict Decrease of Mismodel}

The preceding sections established how augmentation and reduction affect descriptive deficiency and surplus. Augmentation cannot substantially increase deficiency, but may introduce additional surplus. Conversely, reduction cannot substantially increase surplus, but may introduce additional deficiency. Neither operation therefore guarantees a decrease in mismodel. In particular, decreasing one descriptive defect is not sufficient when the other defect remains at or above the previous mismodel value.

The following proposition provides a necessary and sufficient condition for strict improvement.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:sufficient_decrease_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a target representation, and let $d,d'\in\mathcal D_r$ be two extended descriptions of $r$. Then
\[
\nu(r,d')<\nu(r,d)
\]
if and only if
\[
d_D(r,d')<\nu(r,d) \quad \text{and} \quad s_D(r,d')<\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\nu(r,d') = \max\{d_D(r,d'),s_D(r,d')\}.
\]
The maximum of two quantities is strictly smaller than $\nu(r,d)$ if and only if both quantities are strictly smaller than $\nu(r,d)$. Therefore $\nu(r,d')<\nu(r,d)$ if and only if $d_D(r,d')<\nu(r,d)$ and $s_D(r,d')<\nu(r,d)$.
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes that strict improvement requires both resulting defects to fall below the previous mismodel value. It does not require both defects to decrease relative to their individual previous values. When deficiency is dominant, an intervention may decrease mismodel even if surplus increases, provided that the resulting surplus remains below the previous mismodel. Similarly, when surplus is dominant, a reduction may improve mismodel even if deficiency increases. When the two defects are balanced,
\[
d_D(r,d)=s_D(r,d)=\nu(r,d),
\]
both must decrease strictly to obtain a strict improvement. This follows directly from Proposition~\ref{prop:sufficient_decrease_mismodel}, since neither defect can remain at the previous maximum. Thus, the appropriate objective is not necessarily to decrease both defects simultaneously, but to reduce the dominant defect while preventing the other from reaching the previous mismodel threshold.

The general condition can be specialized to the operations introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. For augmentation, Proposition~\ref{prop:augmentation_decreases_mismodel} established a sufficient condition for decreasing mismodel when deficiency is dominant. An augmentation that decreases deficiency also decreases mismodel if the additional surplus remains below the previous mismodel value. The corresponding result for reduction applies when surplus is dominant.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:reduction_decreases_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r$. Suppose that $\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,a)$ is defined, with $d_1\in\mathcal D_r$. Write $D=d_D(r,d)$ and $S=s_D(r,d)$, and assume that surplus is the dominant defect: $S>D$

Let $c_{\operatorname{red}}\geq 0$ be a fixed constant
such that the deficiency bound satisfies
\[
d_D(r,d_1) \leq D+K(a\mid d_1)+c_{\operatorname{red}}.
\]
If
\[
s_D(r,d_1)<S
\]
and
\[
K(a\mid d_1)+c_{\operatorname{red}}<S-D,
\]
then
\[
\nu(r,d_1)<\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $S>D$, the original mismodel is $\nu(r,d)=S$. By assumption, reduction decreases surplus $s_D(r,d_1)<S$.

Furthermore, Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction_deficiency_bound} gives
\[
d_D(r,d_1) \leq D+K(a\mid d_1)+c_{\operatorname{red}}.
\]
Using
\[
K(a\mid d_1)+c_{\operatorname{red}}<S-D,
\]
we obtain
\[
d_D(r,d_1)<S.
\]
Consequently, both descriptive defects of the reduced description are strictly smaller than the original mismodel $d_D(r,d_1)<S$ and $s_D(r,d_1)<S$. Therefore,
\[
\nu(r,d_1) = \max\{d_D(r,d_1),s_D(r,d_1)\} < S = \nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes a sufficient condition under which a reduction that decreases surplus also decreases
overall mismodel. When surplus dominates, the difference
\[
s_D(r,d)-d_D(r,d)
\]
provides a margin within which additional deficiency may be introduced without preventing a strict decrease in mismodel.

The condition involving $K(a\mid d_1)$ is sufficient but not necessary. The information discarded by reduction may be largely unrelated to the information needed to recover a minimal description of the target. Consequently, the actual increase in deficiency may be considerably smaller than the general bound.

Together, the augmentation and reduction propositions establish complementary mechanisms for decreasing mismodel. Augmentation is particularly suited to correcting deficiency, whereas reduction is particularly suited to eliminating surplus. However, the distinction is not absolute. Both operations may affect both descriptive defects, and their effectiveness must be determined from the resulting mismodel rather than from the operation employed. Moreover, the bounds established for augmentation and reduction hold up to additive constants. They do not imply exact monotonicity or guarantee strict improvement. Strict comparisons are made relative to the fixed universal description system adopted throughout the theory.

\section{Mismodel of Conditional Descriptions}
\label{sec:conditional_mismodel}

Scientific descriptions frequently rely on previously established knowledge, such as mathematical laws, computational procedures, experimental results, or other descriptions. The conditional descriptions introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions} formalize this situation by allowing a computation to access an explicitly specified background. When evaluating such descriptions, it is natural to distinguish the information supplied by the description itself from the information already available in the background. We therefore extend the concepts of descriptive deficiency, surplus, and mismodel to conditional descriptions.

Recall that a background is specified by a decodable description $c\in\mathcal D^\top$, whose output $b=\Gamma(c)$ is made available to a conditional computation. For a conditional description $d\in\mathcal D_c^\top$, the conditional description function produces $\Gamma_c(d)=U(d,b)$, where $U$ is the fixed optimal universal conditional interpreter introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. Unless explicitly comparing different backgrounds, the background $c$, and therefore the string $b$, is assumed to be fixed.

As in the unconditional case, descriptive deficiency and surplus are evaluated relative to minimal descriptions of a target representation. However, the corresponding minimal conditional descriptions are defined with respect
to the available background.

We first extend the notion of an extended description to this setting.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:extended_conditional_description}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation and $c\in\mathcal D^\top$ a background description, with $b=\Gamma(c)$. We define the set of \emph{extended conditional descriptions} of $r$ given $c$ as
\[
\mathcal D_{r\mid c} = \left\{ d\in\mathcal D_c^\top \;\middle|\; \begin{aligned}
& \Gamma_c(d)=r\\
& \quad\lor\\
& K(r\mid\Gamma_c(d),b) \leq K(r\mid b)-\tau
\end{aligned}
\right\},
\]
where $\tau$ is the information threshold introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}.
\end{definition}

The definition parallels that of an ordinary extended description. A conditional description is extended if it reconstructs the target exactly or if its output provides a non-trivial amount of information about the target beyond what is already available in the background.

The distinction is important. Information already contained in the background does not constitute an additional informational contribution of the conditional description. By construction, every exact conditional description is extended: $\bar{\mathcal D}_{r\mid c} \subseteq \mathcal D_{r\mid c}$. In particular, the set of minimal conditional descriptions $\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast$, introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, is nonempty and satisfies
\[
\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast \subseteq \bar{\mathcal D}_{r\mid c} \subseteq \mathcal D_{r\mid c}.
\]
Its elements have minimum length $\ell(d^\ast)=K(r\mid b)$. Thus, minimal conditional descriptions provide the
reference for evaluating the descriptive information required beyond the specified background.

We define conditional deficiency and surplus by measuring the algorithmic distance between a conditional description and the minimal conditional descriptions of the same target representation.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:conditional_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ be a representation, $c\in\mathcal D^\top$ a background description, and $d\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}$ an extended conditional description of $r$ given $c$. Writing $b=\Gamma(c)$, we define the
\emph{conditional descriptive deficiency} \index{Conditional descriptive deficiency} as
\[
d_D(r,d\mid c) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast} K(d^\ast\mid d,b),
\]
and the \emph{conditional descriptive surplus} \index{Conditional descriptive surplus} as
\[
s_D(r,d\mid c) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast} K(d\mid d^\ast,b).
\]
The \emph{conditional mismodel} \index{Conditional mismodel} of $d$ relative to $r$ and $c$ is defined as
\[
\nu(r,d\mid c) = \max\{d_D(r,d\mid c),s_D(r,d\mid c)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Conditional deficiency measures the additional information required to recover a minimal conditional description from the current description and the available background. Conditional surplus measures the additional information required to reconstruct the current description from a minimal conditional description and that same background. Their maximum characterizes the dominant descriptive defect relative to the information already available. The background is not charged again when evaluating these quantities. This does not mean that the background contains no information or that it can be obtained without cost. It means only that the assessment is made under the assumption that its output is available.

As in the unconditional case, minimal descriptions have negligible mismodel. In particular, for every
$d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast$, we have $d_D(r,d^\ast\mid c)=O(1)$ and $s_D(r,d^\ast\mid c)=O(1)$, and consequently $\nu(r,d^\ast\mid c)=O(1)$. A conditional description may also have negligible mismodel without being minimal, provided that it contains essentially the same algorithmic information as a minimal conditional description relative to the background.

An exact conditional description generates the target representation when supplied with the background, but it need not be a minimal conditional description.

The following proposition characterizes its deficiency, surplus, and mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:exact_conditional_mismodel}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$, $c\in\mathcal D^\top$, and $d\in\bar{\mathcal D}_{r\mid c}$. Writing $b=\Gamma(c)$,
and $k=K(r\mid b)$, we have
\[
d_D(r,d\mid c) = K(k\mid d,b)+O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d\mid c) = K(d\mid b)-k+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\nu(r,d\mid c) = \max\left\{K(k\mid d,b), K(d\mid b)-k \right\} +O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $d$ is an exact conditional description, we have $U(d,b)=r$. Thus, $r$ can be recovered from $d$ and $b$
with constant overhead. Every minimal conditional description $d^\ast$ has length $k$ and generates $r$ given $b$. Consequently, $k$ can be recovered from $d^\ast$ by reading its length.

Conversely, given $r$, $b$, and $k$, one can enumerate and simulate conditional programs of length $k$ until one producing $r$ is found. Such a program exists by the definition of $k$. Therefore, given $d$ and $b$, recovering a minimal conditional description requires, up to an additive constant, the information needed
to determine $k$:
\[
d_D(r,d\mid c)=K(k\mid d,b)+O(1).
\]
For surplus, the conditional version of the prefix-complexity chain rule gives
\[
K(d\mid b) = K(r\mid b) + K(d\mid r,k,b) + O(1),
\]
because $r$ is computable from $d$ and $b$.

For every $d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}^\ast$, we have
\[
K(d^\ast\mid b)\le\ell(d^\ast)+O(1)=k+O(1).
\]
Since $r$ is computable from $d^\ast$ and $b$, the conditional prefix-complexity chain rule gives
\[
K(d^\ast\mid r,k,b)=O(1).
\]
Conversely, $r$ and $k$ can be recovered from $d^\ast$ and $b$ with constant overhead. Thus, every minimal conditional description is algorithmically equivalent to the pair $(r,k)$ given $b$, and
\[
s_D(r,d\mid c)=K(d\mid r,k,b)+O(1).
\]
Combining these relationships yields
\[
s_D(r,d\mid c) = K(d\mid b)-k+O(1).
\]
The characterization of conditional mismodel follows by taking the maximum of deficiency and surplus.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that exact conditional reconstruction does not guarantee negligible mismodel. The first component measures the information required to determine the minimum additional description length of the target. The second measures the excess algorithmic information contained in the current conditional description beyond that minimum. A description may therefore reproduce its target perfectly using the available background while still containing unnecessary computational information.

Conditional mismodel depends on the information made available through the background. Changing that information changes the conditions under which descriptive quality is evaluated. However, the particular description used to generate a background is irrelevant when its output remains unchanged.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:conditional_mismodel_background_equivalence}
Let $r\in\mathcal R$ and let $c_1,c_2\in\mathcal D^\top$ satisfy $\Gamma(c_1)=\Gamma(c_2)$. Then
\[
\mathcal D_{r\mid c_1} = \mathcal D_{r\mid c_2}.
\]
Moreover, for every description $d$ belonging to this common set,
\[
\nu(r,d\mid c_1)=\nu(r,d\mid c_2).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Conditional evaluation depends on a background description only through its output. Since $\Gamma(c_1) = \Gamma(c_2) = b$, both backgrounds induce the same conditional description function and the same conditional decodability domain. Consequently, they also induce the same sets of extended and minimal conditional descriptions of $r$. Furthermore, the conditional complexities used to define deficiency and surplus are evaluated with the same background string $b$. Their values, and therefore conditional mismodel, are identical.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that conditional mismodel depends on the information supplied by a background, rather than on the length or computational structure of the description used to generate it.

A more general change of background need not preserve conditional mismodel. Supplying additional information may shorten the minimal conditional description of the target, but it may also change the set of descriptions against which deficiency and surplus are evaluated. Consequently, there is no general guarantee that adding background information decreases conditional mismodel for an arbitrary conditional description. Comparisons of conditional mismodel should therefore normally be made under a common fixed background. Otherwise, a change in the measured value may reflect a change in the available information rather than an improvement in the description itself.

\begin{example}
Consider a representation $r$ encoding observations of the motion of a physical system. Suppose that a background description $c$ provides the computational procedures required to solve a particular class of differential equations, together with the numerical conventions used to encode their solutions. A conditional description $d$ may then specify the particular equations, parameters, initial conditions, and evaluation schedule required to produce predictions of the observed motion. The conditional description function generates
\[
\Gamma_c(d) = \langle\hat{x}(t_1),\ldots,\hat{x}(t_n)\rangle.
\]
If the predictions reconstruct $r$ exactly or satisfy
\[
K(r\mid\Gamma_c(d),b)\le K(r\mid b)-\tau,
\]
then $d\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}$. Conditional mismodel evaluates the description relative to the available numerical and computational background. The implementation of the numerical procedures does not have to be included again in $d$. However, the equations and parameters supplied by $d$ remain part of the additional descriptive information unless they are already determined by the background. A more elaborate description may account for additional aspects of the observations, potentially reducing conditional deficiency. Conversely, it
may introduce unnecessary assumptions or parameters, increasing conditional surplus. Whether the modification decreases conditional mismodel depends on both resulting defects, rather than merely on the accuracy of its
predictions or the length of its code.
\end{example}

The general principles established for decreasing mismodel also apply when the background is fixed. Let $d,d'\in\mathcal D_{r\mid c}$ be two extended conditional descriptions of the same representation under the same background. By definition,
\[
\nu(r,d'\mid c)<\nu(r,d\mid c)
\]
if and only if
\[
d_D(r,d'\mid c)<\nu(r,d\mid c)
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d'\mid c)<\nu(r,d\mid c).
\]

Thus, decreasing one conditional descriptive defect is not sufficient when the other remains at or above
the previous mismodel value. When conditional deficiency dominates, refinement may require introducing additional information not already contained in the background. When surplus dominates, it may require removing information that is unnecessary for recovering a minimal conditional description.

The operations of augmentation and reduction introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions} apply directly to ordinary decodable descriptions. Their application to conditional codes requires the corresponding computational transformation to be defined for the conditional description domain. The present section does not assume that every such transformation automatically preserves conditional decodability or extendedness. Moreover, conditional mismodel is generally noncomputable, since its definition depends on minimal conditional descriptions and Kolmogorov complexity. Consequently, the results established here provide theoretical criteria for evaluating conditional mismodel, rather than generally computable procedures. Finally, conditional mismodel evaluates descriptive quality under an assumption of background availability, not background correctness. A background may contain inaccurate information, unsupported assumptions, or computational procedures that are unsuitable for the intended scientific application.

A conditional description may have low mismodel relative to such a background without providing an adequate scientific account of the entity under investigation. Assessing the validity of the background itself requires considering its representations and descriptions separately. Conditional mismodel thus formalizes the quality
of the additional descriptive information required beyond an established background. It provides a means of evaluating scientific descriptions that reuse existing knowledge while preserving the fundamental distinction between deficiency, surplus, and descriptive optimality.

\section{Mismodel of Research Areas}
\label{sec:mismodel_research_areas}

Scientific descriptions frequently account for collections of related entities rather than individual entities considered in isolation. A common mathematical law, for example, may describe several physical systems, with differences between them captured by parameters or initial conditions. Such descriptions may exploit shared regularities that are not apparent when the entities are modeled independently. In Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}, we introduced componentwise and joint descriptions of research areas. We now examine how their descriptive quality can be evaluated using deficiency, surplus, and mismodel.

Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a research area, and let $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\subseteq A$ be a nonempty finite subarea, with a fixed ordering of its entities. For each $e_i$, let $r_i\in\mathcal R_{e_i}$ be an extended representation. The corresponding area representation is $\rho_F=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle$, where the brackets denote the fixed computable, self-delimiting encoding introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Representations}. An area representation is a finite binary string, but it need not belong to the theoretical representation space $\mathcal R$. Consequently, the definitions of extended and minimal descriptions, previously formulated for individual representations, must be applied to the area representation as a computational target. This requires only a direct extension of the existing definitions. For an area representation $\rho_F$, we define
\[
\mathcal D_{\rho_F} = \left\{d\in\mathcal D^\top\;\middle|\;\Gamma(d)=\rho_F \;\lor\;
K(\rho_F\mid\Gamma(d))\leq K(\rho_F)-\tau \right\}.
\]
Likewise, $\mathcal D_{\rho_F}^\ast$ denotes the set of shortest exact descriptions of $\rho_F$:
\[
\mathcal D_{\rho_F}^\ast = \left\{d\in\mathcal D^\top\;\middle|\; \Gamma(d)=\rho_F
\;\land\; \ell(d)=K(\rho_F) \right\}.
\]
These definitions use the same information threshold and universal description system adopted for individual representations. Since every finite binary string admits an exact description, $\mathcal D_{\rho_F}^\ast$ is nonempty. We can therefore extend descriptive deficiency and surplus to area representations without modifying their fundamental definitions.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:mismodel_research_area}
Let $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ be a research area, $F\subseteq A$ a nonempty finite subarea, and $\rho_F$ an area representation of $F$. Let $d_F\in\mathcal D_{\rho_F}$ be an extended description of $\rho_F$. We define the \emph{descriptive deficiency of the area} as
\[
d_D(\rho_F,d_F) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{\rho_F}^\ast} K(d^\ast\mid d_F),
\]
and its \emph{descriptive surplus} as
\[
s_D(\rho_F,d_F) = \min_{d^\ast\in\mathcal D_{\rho_F}^\ast} K(d_F\mid d^\ast).
\]
The \emph{mismodel of the area representation} \index{Mismodel of a research area} is defined as
\[
\nu_A(F,\rho_F,d_F) = \max\{d_D(\rho_F,d_F),s_D(\rho_F,d_F)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

This definition evaluates the quality of a description relative to the complete area representation, rather than to its constituent representations independently.

Deficiency measures the additional algorithmic information required to recover a minimal description of the area representation. Surplus measures the information required to reconstruct the given area description from a minimal one. Their maximum identifies the dominant descriptive defect. The measure depends on the selected finite subarea, its representation, and its description. It does not characterize the entire research area when entities remain outside $F$.

Two different approaches can be used to assess the  descriptive quality of a research area. A componentwise assessment evaluates each individual hypothesis $(r_i,d_i)$ through $\nu(r_i,d_i)$ where $i=1,\ldots,n$. These values characterize how well each constituent representation is described. They may be examined individually or summarized according to the purposes of the investigation. A joint assessment instead considers a single description $d_F$ of the complete area representation $\nu_A(F,\rho_F,d_F)$. The distinction is important because the representations of different entities may contain shared algorithmic information. A joint description can exploit this information, while separate descriptions may reproduce the same computational structures or explanatory relationships independently. Consequently, area mismodel is not generally additive:
\[
\nu_A(F,\rho_F,d_F) \neq \sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu(r_i,d_i)
\]
in general, even when $d_F$ is constructed from the individual descriptions. Moreover, a joint description need not contain separately recoverable descriptions of the individual representations. Conversely, combining extended descriptions of individual representations does not automatically guarantee that the resulting description belongs to $\mathcal D_{\rho_F}$. The two assessments therefore answer different questions. Componentwise mismodel characterizes the descriptive adequacy of individual entities, whereas joint mismodel characterizes the adequacy of a common computational account of the selected area representation.

A particularly important case occurs when the joint description reproduces the area representation exactly. The properties established earlier for exact descriptions extend directly to this case, because the area representation is a finite binary string.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:exact_area_mismodel}
Let $\rho_F$ be an area representation, and let $d_F\in\mathcal D_{\rho_F}$ be an exact description, so that $\Gamma(d_F)=\rho_F$. Then
\[
d_D(\rho_F,d_F) = K(K(\rho_F)\mid d_F)+O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_D(\rho_F,d_F) = K(d_F)-K(\rho_F)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The result follows from the corresponding properties of exact descriptions established earlier in this chapter. Their proofs depend only on the existence of minimal descriptions under the fixed universal description system. Since $\rho_F$ is a finite binary string, it admits minimal descriptions of length $K(\rho_F)$. Moreover, $\Gamma(d_F)=\rho_F$. Applying the exact-description results with $\rho_F$ as the target gives the stated equalities.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that an exact joint description may still have non-negligible mismodel. Even when the entire area representation is reproduced correctly, the description may contain additional algorithmic information beyond what is necessary for its minimal reconstruction. This distinction is particularly relevant when comparing collections of independently constructed models with descriptions based on shared scientific laws.

A componentwise description of an area representation can be constructed by combining individual descriptions, as established in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. When every individual description is exact, the resulting componentwise description is also exact. The following proposition establishes a bound on its surplus when the individual descriptions are minimal.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:componentwise_area_surplus}
Let $\rho_F=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle$ be an area representation, and let $d_i^\ast\in\mathcal D_{r_i}^\ast$ be a minimal description of each component. Construct the componentwise description
\[
d_F = \langle TM_{\mathrm{joint}}, \langle d_1^\ast,\ldots,d_n^\ast\rangle\rangle,
\]
where $TM_{\mathrm{joint}}$ is the fixed procedure that executes the component descriptions and encodes their outputs as a sequence. Then
\[
\Gamma(d_F)=\rho_F,
\]
and
\[
s_D(\rho_F,d_F) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n}K(r_i) + K(n) - K(\rho_F) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since every $d_i^\ast$ is an exact description of $r_i$, the joint computation produces
\[
\Gamma(d_F) = \langle\Gamma(d_1^\ast),\ldots, \Gamma(d_n^\ast)\rangle = \rho_F.
\]
The componentwise description can be constructed by supplying the number of components and their minimal descriptions to a fixed procedure. Consequently,
\[
K(d_F) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(d_i^\ast) + K(n) + O(1).
\]
Since $\ell(d_i^\ast)=K(r_i)$, we obtain
\[
K(d_F) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n}K(r_i)+K(n)+O(1).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:exact_area_mismodel},
\[
s_D(\rho_F,d_F) = K(d_F)-K(\rho_F)+O(1).
\] 
Combining both relationships establishes the result.
\end{proof}

The proposition connects the surplus of a componentwise description with the difference between the information required to describe the representations independently and the complexity of their joint representation.

When the representations exhibit substantial shared regularities, their joint Kolmogorov complexity may be much smaller than the sum of their individual complexities. This creates an opportunity to construct economical joint descriptions that encode those regularities once rather than treating every component independently. However, the bound does not imply that a componentwise description necessarily has substantial surplus. Repeated or otherwise algorithmically dependent description codes may themselves be compressible. A reduction in literal description length therefore need not correspond to a reduction in theoretical surplus.

\begin{example}
Consider a research area consisting of several physical systems governed by the same mathematical law. A componentwise approach may construct an independent description for every system, each containing an implementation of the same equation together with the corresponding parameters and initial conditions. A joint approach may instead encode the common mathematical structure once and apply it to the parameters and conditions of the individual systems. For example, several adsorption systems may be described using the same Langmuir isotherm, with different parameter values for each system, as illustrated in Chapter~\ref{chap:Descriptions}. The joint description may offer a more economical computational account of the area. However, its predictions need not coincide with the experimental observations. If the resulting joint description belongs to $\mathcal D_{\rho_F}$, its mismodel can be evaluated using the definition introduced above. A simpler joint model may reduce descriptive surplus, but it may also increase deficiency by failing to
account for important differences between the individual systems. Consequently, the presence of a shared mathematical law does not, by itself, guarantee lower mismodel.
\end{example}

The mismodel of an area representation evaluates descriptive quality relative to a fixed collection of represented entities. It does not measure the completeness of the research area. In particular, a description may have negligible mismodel with respect to $\rho_F$ while many entities in $A$ remain unrepresented or have no known descriptions. Furthermore, low joint mismodel does not necessarily imply uniformly low descriptive defects for every constituent entity. Descriptive inadequacy may be concentrated in particular components, making componentwise analysis useful even when a joint description is available.

Expanding the finite subarea also changes the target representation. If $F\subseteq F'\subseteq A$, there is no general guarantee that mismodel decreases or increases when the description of $F$ is extended to describe $F'$. The new entities may introduce additional unexplained information, but they may also reveal shared regularities that support a more economical joint description.

In practical applications, research areas are represented through effective area representations and the descriptions available within scientific practice. These descriptions are not guaranteed to satisfy the theoretical extendedness condition. Furthermore, minimal descriptions and theoretical mismodel are generally noncomputable. Practical evaluation must therefore rely on approximations, such as inaccuracy and surfeit, applied to the joint representation and its description. Componentwise evaluation remains important for identifying poorly described entities that might otherwise be obscured by an aggregate assessment.

When comparing research areas of different sizes or descriptive complexities, the relative mismodel introduced earlier in this chapter can also be applied to the area representation:
\[
\nu_A^{\mathrm{rel}}(F,\rho_F,d_F) = \frac{\nu_A(F,\rho_F,d_F)}{\max\{K(d_F),K(\rho_F)\}}.
\]
This normalization provides a common complexity scale, although comparisons across different areas must still account for differences in their coverage, composition, and representational conventions.

Finally, descriptive quality must be distinguished from representational adequacy. A joint description may model an area representation accurately and economically even when some of its constituent representations are inadequate representations of their intended entities. A comprehensive evaluation must therefore consider area miscoding alongside area mismodel. Their joint assessment will be addressed in the chapter on nescience.

Mismodel of research areas thus extends the analysis of descriptive adequacy from individual representations to collections of related entities. Its principal significance lies in the possibility of evaluating scientific descriptions that exploit regularities shared across an area, while preserving the distinction between descriptive quality, individual explanatory adequacy, and the completeness of the area under study.
