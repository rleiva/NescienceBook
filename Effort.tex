\chapter{Effort}
\label{chap:Effort}

\begin{quote}
\begin{flushright}
The effort to understand the universe is one of the very few things that lifts human life a little above the level of farce.\\
Steven Weinberg
\end{flushright}
\end{quote}

The theory of nescience measures the extent to which a research entity remains unknown. Given a representation and a description, nescience evaluates the defects that prevent us from reaching perfect knowledge. In this sense, nescience is a property of a hypothesis: it tells us how far a pair composed of a representation and a description is from an ideal pair.

However, measuring how much we do not know is not the same as measuring how difficult it is to know more. Two hypotheses may have the same nescience and yet differ radically in the amount of work required to improve them. One may lie close to many better hypotheses that can be found by a short computation. Another may require a long search through a large space of possible representations, descriptions, experiments, or transformations before any improvement can be found.

This chapter introduces the concept of \emph{effort}. Effort is not a new component of nescience. It is an orthogonal property that measures the computational difficulty of reducing nescience. More precisely, effort will be understood as the computational complexity of discovering, applying, and validating transformations that reduce nescience, or one of its components.

This formulation is motivated by the computational nature of descriptions. In the theory of nescience, a description is a pair $d=\langle T M,a\rangle$, where $T M$ is a Turing machine and $a$ is an input string. The description is valid when the computation $T M(a)$ halts and produces a representation. Thus, every description has not only a length, which is used in the theory of mismodel, but also a running time. Similarly, the operations used to modify descriptions, such as augmentation and reduction, are computable operations and therefore also have running times. Finally, and most importantly, finding a useful augmentation or reduction requires a search over a space of possible candidates. This search has its own computational complexity, and in scientific practice it is often the dominant source of effort.

The main thesis of this chapter is that research effort is a function of the computational complexity of reducing nescience. This includes the cost of executing descriptions, transforming hypotheses, generating candidates, evaluating whether candidates improve the current hypothesis, and searching until a satisfactory improvement is found.

This concept helps address several important questions. How hard is it to reduce the nescience of a topic by one percent? Which component of nescience is the bottleneck, miscoding or mismodel? Why do some fields progress faster than others? How can computational effort be related to money, energy, or research strategy? These questions are not answered by nescience alone, because nescience measures the state of knowledge, not the cost of improving that state.

\section{Description Time}
\label{sec:description_time}

Descriptions have so far been studied primarily through their length. This is natural because minimal description length is the basis of Kolmogorov complexity and therefore of mismodel. However, a description is not merely a string; it is an executable object. If $d=\langle T M,a\rangle$ is a valid description, then the Turing machine \(T M\), on input \(a\), halts and produces a representation $T M(a)=r$. The time required by this computation is a first and elementary form of effort.

\begin{definition}
Let \(d=\langle T M,a\rangle\in D\) be a valid description. We define the \emph{execution time} of \(d\), denoted by $\operatorname{time}(d)$, as the number of computation steps performed by \(T M\) on input \(a\) before halting.
\end{definition}

If the size of the input is allowed to vary, then a description may also be associated with an ordinary time complexity function.

\begin{definition}
Let \(T M\) be a Turing machine. The \emph{time complexity} of \(T M\) is the function
\[
T_{T M}(n)=\max\{\operatorname{time}_{T M}(a): \ell(a)=n \text{ and } T M(a)\downarrow\},
\]
whenever this maximum is well defined over the inputs under consideration.
\end{definition}

Execution time is not itself the effort of reducing nescience. It is the effort required to use a description once it has already been found. This distinction is important. A description may be short but slow to execute, or long but fast to execute. Mismodel concerns the adequacy and economy of the description; execution time concerns the computational cost of running it.

\begin{example}
A description of a physical system may encode a highly compact system of differential equations together with initial conditions. The description may be short, but computing a high-precision numerical solution may require a long simulation. Conversely, a table of precomputed values may be long and have high surplus, but evaluating it may be computationally cheap.
\end{example}

Thus, execution time is a property of descriptions that is distinct from descriptive length. In applications, both may matter. A scientific description may be preferred because it has low nescience, but rejected in practice because its execution time is too high.

\section{Transformation Effort}
\label{sec:transformation_effort}

Scientific progress requires more than executing existing descriptions. It requires transforming hypotheses into better hypotheses. In the theory of nescience, a hypothesis is a pair $x=(r,d)$, where \(r\) is a representation and \(d\) is a description associated with that representation. Reducing nescience means replacing \(x\) by another hypothesis $y=(r',d')$ whose nescience is lower.

The transformations that generate new hypotheses are themselves computable operations. For example, the augmentation operator on descriptions is a partial computable function $\operatorname{aug}:D\times D\rightharpoonup D$, and the reduction operator is a partial computable function $\operatorname{red}:D\rightharpoonup D\times D$. Both operators have computational costs.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{description transformation} is a partial computable function
\[
q_D:D\rightharpoonup D.
\]
If \(q_D(d)=d'\), we say that \(q_D\) transforms the description \(d\) into the description \(d'\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{representation transformation} is a partial computable function
\[
q_R:B^{\ast}\rightharpoonup B^{\ast}.
\]
If \(q_R(r)=r'\), we say that \(q_R\) transforms the representation \(r\) into the representation \(r'\).
\end{definition}

A transformation of a hypothesis may modify the representation, the description, or both.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{hypothesis transformation} is a partial computable function
\[
q:\mathcal{H}\rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}.
\]
If \(q(x)=y\), we say that \(q\) transforms the hypothesis \(x\) into the hypothesis \(y\).
\end{definition}

Since transformations are computable, they have running times.

\begin{definition}
Let \(q:\mathcal{H}\rightharpoonup \mathcal{H}\) be a hypothesis transformation and let \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(q)\). The \emph{application effort} of \(q\) at \(x\) is
\[
A(q,x)=\operatorname{time}_{q}(x),
\]
the number of computation steps required to compute \(q(x)\).
\end{definition}

Application effort measures the cost of applying a candidate transformation once the candidate has already been identified. In science, however, this is rarely the whole story. The difficult part is often not applying a known transformation, but finding the transformation that works.

\begin{example}
Suppose that a model of projectile motion is improved by augmenting a vacuum model with an air-resistance correction. Once the correction term is known, applying it may be computationally straightforward. The historical and scientific effort lies in discovering that the missing term is needed, determining its form, and validating that it improves the description of observed trajectories.
\end{example}

For this reason, effort must be based not only on transformation time, but also on search time.

\section{Search Effort}
\label{sec:search_effort}

Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) be the current hypothesis. To reduce its nescience, we need to find a transformation \(q\) such that
\[
N(q(x))<N(x),
\]
or, more strongly, such that
\[
N(q(x))\leq N(x)-\varepsilon.
\]
The set of possible transformations is typically enormous. It may contain possible augmentations, reductions, model substitutions, changes of representation, changes of parameters, changes of background knowledge, or combinations of these. A scientific method can therefore be viewed as a search procedure over this space of possible transformations.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\mathcal{Q}\) be a set of admissible hypothesis transformations. A \emph{search procedure over \(\mathcal{Q}\)} is a Turing machine \(S\) that, given a hypothesis \(x\) and a threshold \(\varepsilon>0\), searches through candidate transformations in \(\mathcal{Q}\) and either returns a transformed hypothesis \(y\) or fails to halt.
\end{definition}

The running time of such a procedure is the natural theoretical basis for effort.

\begin{definition}
Let \(S\) be a search procedure, let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\), and let \(\varepsilon>0\). If \(S(x,\varepsilon)\) halts, we denote by
\[
T_S(x,\varepsilon)
\]
the number of computation steps performed by \(S\) before halting.
\end{definition}

A successful search procedure is one whose output reduces nescience by the required amount.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\), \(\varepsilon>0\), and let \(S\) be a search procedure. We say that \(S\) is \emph{successful for \((x,\varepsilon)\)} if \(S(x,\varepsilon)=y\) for some \(y\in\mathcal{H}\) such that
\[
N(y)\leq N(x)-\varepsilon.
\]
\end{definition}

We can now define effort as the minimal running time among successful search procedures.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) be a hypothesis and let \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{computational effort required to reduce nescience by \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\inf_{S}
\left\{
T_S(x,\varepsilon):
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\text{ and }
N(y)\leq N(x)-\varepsilon
\right\},
\]
where the infimum is taken over all search procedures over the admissible transformation space.
\end{definition}

If no successful search procedure exists, or if every successful procedure fails to halt, we write
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\infty.
\]

For relative reductions, we use the following version.

\begin{definition}
Let \(0<\alpha<1\). The \emph{relative effort required to reduce nescience by \(\alpha\)} is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{rel}}(x)
=
\inf_{S}
\left\{
T_S(x,\alpha):
S(x,\alpha)=y
\text{ and }
N(y)\leq (1-\alpha)N(x)
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The particular case \(\alpha=0.01\) corresponds to the effort required to reduce nescience by one percent.

This definition emphasizes that effort is mainly a search complexity. Applying a known transformation may be cheap, but discovering a transformation that reduces nescience may be expensive. This matches scientific practice. Once a good equation, model, representation, or experimental method has been discovered, it may be easy to use. The difficult part is finding it.

\section{Generation, Application, and Evaluation}
\label{sec:generation_application_evaluation}

A search procedure normally performs three different tasks. First, it generates a candidate transformation. Second, it applies that transformation to the current hypothesis. Third, it evaluates the transformed hypothesis to determine whether nescience has decreased.

Let
\[
q_1,q_2,\ldots,q_k
\]
be the sequence of candidate transformations generated by a search procedure before a satisfactory improvement is found. For each candidate, the procedure may incur three costs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a generation cost, for constructing the candidate transformation;
    \item an application cost, for computing the transformed hypothesis;
    \item an evaluation cost, for estimating whether the transformed hypothesis reduces nescience.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}
Let \(S\) be a search procedure that, on input \((x,\varepsilon)\), tests the candidate transformations \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\) before returning a successful hypothesis. The \emph{decomposed search effort} of \(S\) is
\[
T_S(x,\varepsilon)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{k}
\left(
G(q_i,x_i)+A(q_i,x_i)+E(q_i(x_i))
\right)
+O(k),
\]
where \(G\) denotes candidate-generation time, \(A\) denotes application time, \(E\) denotes evaluation time, and \(x_i\) is the hypothesis to which \(q_i\) is applied.
\end{definition}

This decomposition is not intended to introduce three independent theories of effort. It simply makes explicit where the computational cost comes from. In some domains, the dominant term is generation: it is hard to propose useful candidates. In others, the dominant term is application: each candidate is expensive to compute. In others, the dominant term is evaluation: it is difficult to determine whether a candidate is actually better.

\begin{example}
In hyperparameter optimization for a machine learning model, generating a candidate hyperparameter vector is cheap. Applying the candidate requires training a model, which may be expensive. Evaluating the candidate requires computing practical estimates of miscoding, inaccuracy, surfeit, or nescience on validation data. In this case, the application and evaluation costs usually dominate generation cost.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
In theoretical research, applying a candidate transformation may be cheap once it is stated, but generating the candidate may be difficult. Finding the right definition, lemma, representation, or analogy may dominate the total effort.
\end{example}

\section{Effort and Computability}
\label{sec:effort_and_computability}

The definition of effort given above is ideal. It refers to nescience itself, and nescience depends on miscoding and mismodel. These quantities are ultimately grounded in Kolmogorov complexity, admissible representations, and minimal descriptions. Therefore, exact nescience is not generally computable. It follows that exact effort is also not generally computable.

This is not a defect of the theory. The same situation occurs throughout the theory of nescience: ideal quantities provide conceptual reference points, while practical applications use computable approximations.

\begin{proposition}
If exact nescience \(N\) is not computable on a hypothesis class \(\mathcal{H}\), then there is in general no algorithm that computes \(\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) exactly for all \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) and all \(\varepsilon>0\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Computing \(\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) requires deciding, at least for candidate outputs \(y\), whether
\[
N(y)\leq N(x)-\varepsilon.
\]
If \(N\) is not computable, this comparison is not computable in general. Therefore a general algorithm for exact effort would imply the ability to solve comparisons involving a non-computable quantity, which is impossible in general.
\end{proof}

For practical work, we therefore replace \(N\) by a computable approximation \(\widehat{N}\), and we restrict the candidate search space.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\widehat{N}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) be a computable approximation of nescience, and let \(\mathcal{Q}\) be a computably enumerable set of admissible transformations. The \emph{practical effort required to reduce \(\widehat{N}\) by \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Eff}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{Q}}(x)
=
\inf_{S}
\left\{
T_S(x,\varepsilon):
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\text{ and }
\widehat{N}(y)\leq \widehat{N}(x)-\varepsilon
\right\},
\]
where the infimum is taken over search procedures restricted to \(\mathcal{Q}\).
\end{definition}

This is the quantity that can be estimated in applications. It depends on the chosen approximation \(\widehat{N}\), the admissible transformation space \(\mathcal{Q}\), and the search procedure used.

\section{Effort-Bounded Reduction Problems}
\label{sec:effort_bounded_reduction}

The computational-complexity formulation naturally leads to decision problems. Instead of asking for the exact effort, we may ask whether a given reduction is possible within a specified budget.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{effort-bounded nescience reduction problem} is the following decision problem:
\[
\textsc{Nescience-Reduction}.
\]
Given a hypothesis \(x\in\mathcal{H}\), a threshold \(\varepsilon>0\), and a budget \(B\in\mathbb{N}\), decide whether there exists an admissible search procedure \(S\) such that
\[
T_S(x,\varepsilon)\leq B
\]
and
\[
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\quad\text{with}\quad
N(y)\leq N(x)-\varepsilon.
\]
\end{definition}

For practical purposes we use the computable version.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\widehat{N}\) be a computable approximation of nescience and let \(\mathcal{Q}\) be a finite or computably enumerable class of candidate transformations. The \emph{practical effort-bounded nescience reduction problem} asks whether there exists a sequence of candidate transformations from \(\mathcal{Q}\), computable within budget \(B\), that produces a hypothesis \(y\) satisfying
\[
\widehat{N}(y)\leq \widehat{N}(x)-\varepsilon.
\]
\end{definition}

When \(\mathcal{Q}\) is finite and \(\widehat{N}\) is computable, this practical problem is decidable by exhaustive search. It may still be computationally infeasible, but it is not blocked by non-computability.

\begin{proposition}
Let \(\mathcal{Q}\) be finite and let \(\widehat{N}\) be computable. Suppose that every \(q\in\mathcal{Q}\) halts on the hypotheses under consideration. Then the practical effort-bounded nescience reduction problem over \(\mathcal{Q}\) is decidable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since \(\mathcal{Q}\) is finite and every candidate transformation halts on the hypotheses under consideration, one can enumerate all candidate transformations, apply each one within the specified budget, compute \(\widehat{N}\) for the resulting hypotheses, and check whether the required inequality holds. Therefore the decision problem is decidable.
\end{proof}

This proposition clarifies the role of bounded search spaces. They do not solve the theoretical problem of exact nescience reduction, but they make practical effort estimation possible.

\section{Component-wise Effort}
\label{sec:componentwise_effort}

Nescience is composed of miscoding and mismodel. It is therefore useful to define effort not only for nescience as a whole, but also for its components. This allows us to identify whether progress is difficult because the entity is hard to represent, because the representation is hard to describe, or because both are difficult.

Let \(\mu(e,r)\) denote the miscoding of a representation \(r\) for an entity \(e\), and let \(\nu(r,d)\) denote the mismodel of a description \(d\) with respect to \(r\). For a hypothesis \(x=(r,d)\), write
\[
\mu(x)=\mu(e,r),
\qquad
\nu(x)=\nu(r,d).
\]

\begin{definition}
Let \(x=(r,d)\in\mathcal{H}\) and let \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{effort required to reduce miscoding by \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}^{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\inf_{S}
\left\{
T_S(x,\varepsilon):
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\text{ and }
\mu(y)\leq \mu(x)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
Let \(x=(r,d)\in\mathcal{H}\) and let \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{effort required to reduce mismodel by \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\inf_{S}
\left\{
T_S(x,\varepsilon):
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\text{ and }
\nu(y)\leq \nu(x)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The same construction can be applied to the two components of miscoding, deficiency and surplus, and to the practical approximations of mismodel, inaccuracy and surfeit. For example, if \(\iota(r,d)\) denotes inaccuracy and \(\sigma(d)\) denotes surfeit, one may define \(\operatorname{Eff}^{\iota}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) and \(\operatorname{Eff}^{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) analogously.

Component-wise effort identifies the bottleneck of a research problem.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{effort bottleneck} of \(x\) at scale \(\varepsilon\) is the component whose reduction requires the largest effort:
\[
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\arg\max_{c\in\{\mu,\nu\}}
\operatorname{Eff}^{c}_{\varepsilon}(x).
\]
\end{definition}

If the bottleneck is miscoding, then the primary difficulty lies in improving the representation. More modeling effort may be ineffective until the representation is corrected. If the bottleneck is mismodel, then the representation may already contain useful information, but the description fails to capture it adequately or economically.

\begin{example}
In a medical prediction problem, high miscoding effort may indicate that crucial clinical variables are not measured, labels are noisy, or patient subpopulations are poorly represented. Reducing nescience then requires better data collection or better representation design. High mismodel effort, by contrast, may indicate that the dataset is informative but the model class is inadequate. In that case, progress requires searching for better descriptions.
\end{example}

\section{Effort of Mismodel Reduction}
\label{sec:effort_mismodel_reduction}

We now examine mismodel in more detail. Let \(r\) be a representation and let \(d\in D_r\) be a description. Reducing mismodel means finding a new description \(d'\) such that
\[
\nu(r,d')<\nu(r,d),
\]
or, more strongly,
\[
\nu(r,d')\leq \nu(r,d)-\varepsilon.
\]

The two basic structural operations on descriptions are augmentation and reduction. Augmentation is useful when the description is deficient: it lacks information needed to reconstruct the representation. Reduction is useful when the description contains surplus: it includes structure that is not needed.

\begin{definition}
Let \(r\in R\), \(d\in D_r\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). A description \(d'\in D\) is an \emph{\(\varepsilon\)-improving description for \(d\) with respect to \(r\)} if
\[
\nu(r,d')\leq \nu(r,d)-\varepsilon.
\]
\end{definition}

A search procedure for mismodel reduction searches over candidate transformations of \(d\). Typical candidates include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item augmenting \(d\) with an auxiliary description \(c\), producing \(\operatorname{aug}(d,c)\);
    \item reducing \(d\), producing a retained description \(d_1\) such that \(\operatorname{red}(d)=(d_1,d_2)\);
    \item changing model class;
    \item changing parameters within a parametric model class;
    \item conditioning \(d\) on background knowledge;
    \item replacing \(d\) by a newly generated description.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}
Let \(r\in R\), \(d\in D_r\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{mismodel-reduction effort} of \(d\) with respect to \(r\) is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}^{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(r,d)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
T_S(r,d,\varepsilon):
S(r,d,\varepsilon)=d'
\text{ and }
\nu(r,d')\leq \nu(r,d)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

This definition separates the existence of a better description from the cost of finding it. There may exist a short augmentation that dramatically improves the model, but if the search procedure cannot discover it efficiently, the effort remains high.

\begin{example}
Suppose a description \(d\) models a dataset using a linear relation. If the true regularity is quadratic, then an augmentation that introduces a squared term may reduce mismodel substantially. Applying the augmentation is cheap once the term is known. The effort lies in searching over possible nonlinear terms and identifying the one that improves the description.
\end{example}

Reduction behaves similarly. A complex description may contain unnecessary structure. If one knows exactly which part is unnecessary, simplifying the description may be cheap. Discovering that part may be expensive.

\begin{example}
A large ensemble model may contain many components that contribute little to its predictions. Once those components are identified, pruning them is computationally straightforward. The difficult part is searching for a reduction that decreases surfeit without increasing inaccuracy beyond an acceptable level.
\end{example}

\section{Effort of Miscoding Reduction}
\label{sec:effort_miscoding_reduction}

The effort required to reduce miscoding concerns the difficulty of improving the representation of an entity. Let \(e\in E\) be an entity and let \(r\in R_e\) be an effective representation. Reducing miscoding means finding a representation \(r'\in R_e\) such that
\[
\mu(e,r')<\mu(e,r),
\]
or, more strongly,
\[
\mu(e,r')\leq \mu(e,r)-\varepsilon.
\]

Representation transformations may include augmentation, reduction, joint representation, experimental measurement, data cleaning, or a change of representation style.

\begin{definition}
Let \(e\in E\), \(r\in R_e\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). A representation \(r'\in R_e\) is an \emph{\(\varepsilon\)-improving representation for \(r\)} if
\[
\mu(e,r')\leq \mu(e,r)-\varepsilon.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
Let \(e\in E\), \(r\in R_e\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{miscoding-reduction effort} of \(r\) is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}^{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(e,r)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
T_S(e,r,\varepsilon):
S(e,r,\varepsilon)=r'
\text{ and }
\mu(e,r')\leq \mu(e,r)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

As before, the effort is not merely the cost of applying a known correction to \(r\). It is the cost of discovering and validating a representation transformation that reduces miscoding.

\begin{example}
Suppose a dataset used to study a disease lacks a variable measuring exposure to a relevant environmental factor. Adding this variable may reduce deficiency. If the variable is already known and easy to measure, the effort may be low. If the variable is unknown, expensive to measure, or conceptually unclear, the effort may be high.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Suppose a dataset contains an identifier that is correlated with the target variable only because of a sampling artifact. Removing it may reduce surplus. If the artifact is obvious, the reduction effort is low. If the artifact is subtle and can only be detected through extensive validation, the effort is high.
\end{example}

Miscoding effort is often experimental, observational, or conceptual. The relevant computation may not be limited to manipulating strings already present in memory. It may include designing an instrument, collecting new observations, defining new variables, or establishing that a representation is attached to the wrong entity. These activities can still be modeled computationally by encoding the research process as a procedure that generates and evaluates candidate representations.

\section{Local, Global, and Marginal Effort}
\label{sec:local_global_marginal_effort}

Effort can be local or global. Local effort concerns the cost of finding a nearby improvement. Global effort concerns the cost of reaching a specified target level of nescience.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\mathcal{N}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{H}\) be a computably specified neighborhood of \(x\). The \emph{local effort} required to improve \(x\) is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}^{\mathrm{loc}}(x)
=
\inf
\left\{
C(x\to y):
y\in\mathcal{N}(x),\ N(y)<N(x)
\right\},
\]
where \(C(x\to y)\) denotes the least application and evaluation cost of reaching \(y\) from \(x\) within the neighborhood.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
Let \(\tau\geq 0\). The \emph{target effort} required to reach nescience at most \(\tau\) is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\leq\tau}(x)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
T_S(x,\tau):
S(x,\tau)=y
\text{ and }
N(y)\leq\tau
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Marginal effort measures the cost per unit of reduction.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{marginal effort of nescience reduction} at scale \(\varepsilon\) is
\[
\operatorname{ME}_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\frac{\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\varepsilon}.
\]
\end{definition}

For relative reductions,
\[
\operatorname{ME}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{rel}}(x)
=
\frac{\operatorname{Eff}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{rel}}(x)}{\alpha N(x)}.
\]

Marginal effort is the natural formal answer to the question: how hard is it to improve this topic from its current state?

As a field matures, one expects marginal effort to increase. Early stages of inquiry often contain obvious improvements: missing variables, crude measurements, inadequate descriptions, or simple conceptual distinctions. Later stages may require expensive experiments, highly specialized instruments, large computational resources, or difficult theoretical innovations. Thus, a mature field may have low nescience but high marginal effort.

\section{Return on Effort}
\label{sec:return_on_effort}

The dual concept of effort is return on effort. If effort measures the cost of improvement, return on effort measures the improvement obtained per unit cost.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\to y\) be a transition between hypotheses with cost \(C(x\to y)>0\). The \emph{return on effort} of the transition is
\[
\operatorname{RoE}(x\to y)
=
\frac{N(x)-N(y)}{C(x\to y)}.
\]
For a search procedure \(S\) that transforms \(x\) into \(y\), the return on effort is
\[
\operatorname{RoE}(S,x)
=
\frac{N(x)-N(y)}{T_S(x)}.
\]
\end{definition}

A rational research strategy should not always select the action with the largest expected nescience reduction. Under limited resources, it should often select the action with the largest expected reduction per unit of effort.

\begin{definition}
Let \(a\) be a research action available at hypothesis \(x\). If applying \(a\) has uncertain result \(Y\), the \emph{expected return on effort} is
\[
\operatorname{ERoE}(a\mid x)
=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[N(x)-N(Y)]}{\mathbb{E}[C(a\mid x)]}.
\]
\end{definition}

The reciprocal quantity is the expected effort per unit of reduction:
\[
\operatorname{EEUR}(a\mid x)
=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[C(a\mid x)]}{\mathbb{E}[N(x)-N(Y)]},
\]
when the denominator is positive.

\section{Expected and Risk-Adjusted Effort}
\label{sec:expected_and_risk_adjusted_effort}

Research is uncertain. A candidate representation may introduce more miscoding than it removes. A candidate model may overfit. An experiment may fail to discriminate between hypotheses. A theoretical idea may lead nowhere. Therefore, practical effort should usually be treated probabilistically.

Let \(S\) be a search strategy whose output is a random hypothesis \(Y\). The expected reduction is
\[
\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(S\mid x)]
=
\mathbb{E}[N(x)-N(Y)].
\]

\begin{definition}
Let \(S\) be a search strategy at hypothesis \(x\). The \emph{expected effort per unit reduction} of \(S\) is
\[
\operatorname{EEUR}(S\mid x)
=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_S(x)]}{\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(S\mid x)]},
\]
provided that \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(S\mid x)]>0\).
\end{definition}

Two strategies may have the same expected reduction but very different variances. A high-risk strategy may have a small probability of a major breakthrough and a large probability of failure. Depending on the scientific context, such a strategy may or may not be desirable.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\lambda\geq 0\) be a risk-aversion parameter. The \emph{risk-adjusted effort} of a search strategy \(S\) at \(x\) is
\[
\operatorname{RAE}(S\mid x)
=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_S(x)]}{\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(S\mid x)]}
+
\lambda\operatorname{Var}(\Delta N(S\mid x)).
\]
\end{definition}

The parameter \(\lambda\) determines how strongly uncertainty is penalized. A risk-neutral researcher may take \(\lambda=0\). A funding agency responsible for a reliable portfolio of projects may choose a larger value.

\section{Conditional Effort}
\label{sec:conditional_effort}

Background knowledge can reduce nescience, but it can also reduce the effort required to reduce nescience. These are distinct effects. Conditional nescience measures how much ignorance remains when some background knowledge is available. Conditional effort measures how difficult it is to reduce that remaining ignorance given the background.

In the theory of descriptions, background knowledge is naturally represented by a description \(c\in D\), rather than by an arbitrary string. We therefore define conditional effort relative to such a background description.

\begin{definition}
Let \(c\in D\) be a background description. The \emph{conditional effort required to reduce nescience by \(\varepsilon\) given \(c\)} is
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x\mid c)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
T_S(x,\varepsilon\mid c):
S(x,\varepsilon,c)=y
\text{ and }
N(y\mid c)\leq N(x\mid c)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The background description may reduce effort in several ways. It may provide reusable subroutines, restrict the candidate search space, eliminate impossible hypotheses, supply a representation language, or make evaluation cheaper.

\begin{example}
The availability of calculus reduces the effort required to formulate and manipulate descriptions in classical mechanics. Calculus need not directly reduce the nescience of every mechanical hypothesis by itself, but it lowers the cost of generating, transforming, and evaluating candidate descriptions.
\end{example}

Conditional effort is important for education and research planning. A prerequisite theory may be valuable not only because it contains knowledge, but because it lowers the future cost of acquiring additional knowledge.

\section{Effort Landscapes}
\label{sec:effort_landscapes}

The hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) can be viewed as a landscape in which each point \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) has a nescience value \(N(x)\). Scientific progress corresponds to moving through this landscape toward regions of lower nescience. Effort adds a second structure to this landscape: the cost of moving between hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x,y\in\mathcal{H}\). The \emph{effort distance} from \(x\) to \(y\) is
\[
D_{\mathrm{Eff}}(x,y)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
T_S(x):S(x)=y
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The effort distance need not be symmetric. It may be easier to move from \(x\) to \(y\) than from \(y\) to \(x\). For example, adding assumptions to a description may be easier than removing them without increasing inaccuracy; collecting data may be easier than identifying which parts of the data are irrelevant.

\begin{definition}
A hypothesis \(x\) is a \emph{local effort minimum} for nescience if there exist hypotheses \(y\) with \(N(y)<N(x)\), but all such hypotheses lie beyond an effort barrier that exceeds the available resources.
\end{definition}

A research field may stagnate not because better hypotheses do not exist, but because all known paths to them require prohibitive effort. In such cases, progress may require a reframing of the problem.

\begin{definition}
A transformation \(q\) is \emph{effort-reducing} at \(x\) if, for some \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(q(x))<\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x),
\]
even if
\[
N(q(x))\geq N(x).
\]
\end{definition}

An effort-reducing transformation need not immediately improve knowledge. It may instead make future improvements easier. New notation, new instruments, new coordinate systems, new representations, and new computational methods often have this character.

\begin{example}
The periodic table did not merely summarize known chemical elements. It organized them in a way that made future discoveries easier. It reduced the effort required to identify missing elements and predict their properties. In this sense, it functioned as an effort-reducing representation.
\end{example}

\section{Thermodynamic Lower Bounds}
\label{sec:thermodynamic_effort}

Effort has been defined as computational complexity. However, every actual computation must be physically implemented. Strings must be stored in physical media, transformations must be performed by physical devices, and information may be copied, overwritten, or erased. This raises the question of whether there is a physical lower bound on implemented effort.

A fundamental answer is provided by Landauer's principle. According to this principle, the irreversible erasure of one bit of information in a system at temperature \(T\) requires the dissipation of at least
\[
k_B T\ln 2
\]
units of energy as heat, where \(k_B\) is Boltzmann's constant.

This does not mean that effort should be identified with energy. Effort, as defined in this chapter, is a computational-complexity concept. Landauer's principle provides a lower bound on the physical energy required by irreversible information-erasing implementations of computations.

\begin{definition}
Let a physically implemented search procedure \(S\) irreversibly erase \(m(S)\) bits while running at temperature \(T\). The \emph{Landauer lower bound} for the energetic cost of \(S\) is
\[
E_{\min}(S)=m(S)k_B T\ln 2.
\]
\end{definition}

This lower bound is usually far below the actual energy costs of scientific research. Its importance is foundational: it shows that physically implemented information refinement cannot be made entirely free when it involves irreversible erasure.

The connection is especially natural for reduction operations. When reducing surplus or surfeit requires physically erasing bits from a stored representation or description, Landauer's principle gives a minimum energetic cost. However, abstract simplification is not identical to physical erasure. A transformation may be logically reversible, may copy information rather than erase it, or may store discarded information elsewhere. Landauer's bound applies specifically to irreversible erasure.

Thus, thermodynamic effort is not the primary definition of effort, but a fundamental physical lower bound on certain implementations of effort.

\section{Monetary Cost Models}
\label{sec:monetary_cost_models}

Computational effort is measured in steps. Practical scientific research is often constrained by money. To connect the two, we must specify a resource model. There is no universal conversion factor from computational steps to euros, because the cost of a step depends on the implementation: hardware, labor, data acquisition, experimental apparatus, energy, validation, and coordination.

Let
\[
\rho(S,x)
\]
be the resource vector consumed by running a search procedure \(S\) on input \(x\). For example,
\[
\rho(S,x)=
(
\rho_{\mathrm{human}},
\rho_{\mathrm{compute}},
\rho_{\mathrm{data}},
\rho_{\mathrm{experiment}},
\rho_{\mathrm{equipment}},
\rho_{\mathrm{validation}},
\rho_{\mathrm{coordination}}
).
\]
Let
\[
p=
(
p_{\mathrm{human}},
p_{\mathrm{compute}},
p_{\mathrm{data}},
p_{\mathrm{experiment}},
p_{\mathrm{equipment}},
p_{\mathrm{validation}},
p_{\mathrm{coordination}}
)
\]
be the vector of unit prices.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{monetary cost} of running \(S\) on \(x\) is
\[
M(S,x)=p\cdot\rho(S,x).
\]
\end{definition}

The monetary cost of reducing nescience by \(\varepsilon\) is then obtained by minimizing over successful search procedures.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). The \emph{monetary cost required to reduce nescience by \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
\operatorname{MCost}_{\varepsilon}(x)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
M(S,x):
S(x,\varepsilon)=y
\text{ and }
N(y)\leq N(x)-\varepsilon
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

For a relative reduction of one percent,
\[
\operatorname{MCost}_{1\%}(x)
=
\inf_S
\left\{
M(S,x):
S(x,0.01)=y
\text{ and }
N(y)\leq 0.99N(x)
\right\}.
\]

This formalizes the question:
\[
\text{How much does it cost to reduce the nescience of this topic by one percent?}
\]

In practice, the exact value is usually unavailable. One estimates it using a practical approximation of nescience, a bounded candidate space, empirical data about previous research attempts, and a cost model for the resources involved.

\section{Machine Learning Application}
\label{sec:effort_machine_learning}

Machine learning provides a particularly clear application of effort. In supervised learning, the representation \(r\) is usually a dataset, and the description \(d\) is a trained model or learning procedure. Reducing nescience means improving the dataset, improving the model, or both.

A machine-learning search procedure may explore several classes of candidates:
\begin{itemize}
    \item representation transformations, such as feature engineering, feature removal, sample selection, label correction, data augmentation, or measurement improvement;
    \item description transformations, such as model-class selection, hyperparameter optimization, regularization, pruning, ensembling, or loss-function modification;
    \item joint transformations, such as changing both the dataset representation and the model family.
\end{itemize}

For a candidate \(q_i\), the cost usually has the form
\[
G(q_i,x)+A(q_i,x)+E(q_i(x)),
\]
where generation may be cheap, application corresponds to training or transforming data, and evaluation corresponds to computing validation metrics and practical estimates of nescience.

A practical monetary model may take the form
\[
M_{\mathrm{ML}}
=
p_{\mathrm{gpu}}H_{\mathrm{gpu}}
+
p_{\mathrm{cpu}}H_{\mathrm{cpu}}
+
p_{\mathrm{label}}n_{\mathrm{label}}
+
p_{\mathrm{data}}D
+
p_{\mathrm{eng}}H_{\mathrm{eng}}
+
p_{\mathrm{val}}V,
\]
where \(H_{\mathrm{gpu}}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{cpu}}\) are compute hours, \(n_{\mathrm{label}}\) is the number of labels acquired or corrected, \(D\) is external data volume or acquisition cost, \(H_{\mathrm{eng}}\) is engineering time, and \(V\) is validation cost.

\begin{example}
Suppose a classification problem has practical nescience \(\widehat{N}(x)=0.30\). Three search actions are available:
\begin{itemize}
    \item tune hyperparameters, with expected cost \(2{,}000\) euros and expected nescience reduction \(0.002\);
    \item label additional data, with expected cost \(10{,}000\) euros and expected nescience reduction \(0.009\);
    \item remove irrelevant features, with expected cost \(1{,}500\) euros and expected nescience reduction \(0.003\).
\end{itemize}
The expected nescience reductions per euro are
\[
\frac{0.002}{2000}=10^{-6},
\]
\[
\frac{0.009}{10000}=9\cdot 10^{-7},
\]
and
\[
\frac{0.003}{1500}=2\cdot 10^{-6}.
\]
Although labeling additional data yields the largest expected absolute improvement, feature removal gives the best expected return per euro.
\end{example}

Effort also clarifies the role of automated machine learning. AutoML systems reduce the human effort of searching over descriptions, but they do not eliminate effort. They transfer part of the search cost from human reasoning to computation.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\operatorname{Eff}^{\mathrm{human}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) be the effort required to reduce nescience by \(\varepsilon\) using a human-driven search process, and let \(\operatorname{Eff}^{\mathrm{AI}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\) be the effort using an automated or AI-assisted search process. The \emph{effort reduction due to automation} is
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{AI}}(x,\varepsilon)
=
\operatorname{Eff}^{\mathrm{human}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
-
\operatorname{Eff}^{\mathrm{AI}}_{\varepsilon}(x).
\]
\end{definition}

This quantity provides a formal interpretation of the value of AI systems in scientific work: they are useful when they lower the effort required to discover nescience-reducing transformations.

\section{Effort and the Difficulty of Research Areas}
\label{sec:effort_difficulty_research_areas}

The concept of effort provides a way to address the question of whether some research areas are more difficult than others. A research area should not be considered difficult merely because its current nescience is high. It should be considered difficult when reducing its nescience requires high effort.

Let \(A\subseteq E\) be a research area. Suppose that for each entity \(e\in A\) there is a current best hypothesis \(x_e\). We define the difficulty of \(e\) at scale \(\varepsilon\) as the effort required to reduce the nescience of \(x_e\) by \(\varepsilon\).

\begin{definition}
Let \(e\in E\) be an entity with current best hypothesis \(x_e\). The \emph{difficulty of \(e\) at scale \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
D_{\varepsilon}(e)=\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x_e).
\]
\end{definition}

For a finite research area, difficulty can be aggregated.

\begin{definition}
Let \(A\subseteq E\) be a finite research area and let \(w_e\geq 0\) be weights satisfying
\[
\sum_{e\in A}w_e=1.
\]
The \emph{difficulty of the research area \(A\) at scale \(\varepsilon\)} is
\[
D_{\varepsilon}(A)=\sum_{e\in A}w_eD_{\varepsilon}(e).
\]
\end{definition}

The weights may represent relevance, practical importance, funding priority, or proximity to the knowledge frontier. In large areas, one may compute difficulty only over frontier entities, such as open problems or active research questions.

\begin{definition}
Let \(F_A\subseteq A\) be the set of frontier entities of a research area \(A\). The \emph{frontier difficulty} of \(A\) is
\[
D_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{frontier}}(A)
=
\sum_{e\in F_A}w_eD_{\varepsilon}(e).
\]
\end{definition}

This formulation separates two questions:
\[
\text{How much do we not know about an area?}
\]
from
\[
\text{How difficult is it to reduce what we do not know?}
\]

This distinction provides a possible explanation for different historical rates of scientific progress. Some areas may progress rapidly because their entities admit stable representations, efficient descriptions, strong feedback mechanisms, and tractable search spaces. Other areas may progress slowly because their entities are difficult to represent, their descriptions are difficult to validate, or the search space of candidate transformations is poorly structured.

\begin{example}
Many entities in physics admit quantitative representations: measurements, units, equations, coordinate systems, and experimental protocols. These features restrict the hypothesis space and create strong feedback loops between representation, description, and experiment. In many branches of physics, the effort required to reduce nescience may therefore be relatively low compared with the depth of the resulting progress, even when the experiments themselves are technically demanding.

By contrast, many entities in philosophy, such as truth, consciousness, meaning, causality, justice, or knowledge, are harder to represent in stable operational terms. Different traditions may disagree about what counts as an adequate representation, and feedback mechanisms are often weaker than in experimental science. The difficulty may therefore lie not in lack of rigor, but in the higher effort required to reduce miscoding and mismodel for such entities.
\end{example}

This example should not be interpreted as claiming that one field is intellectually superior to another. The point is structural. Different research areas may have different effort profiles.

\section{Difficulty Profiles}
\label{sec:difficulty_profiles}

A single scalar difficulty value may hide the reason why an area is difficult. It is therefore useful to define difficulty profiles.

\begin{definition}
Let \(A\subseteq E\) be a research area. A \emph{difficulty profile} of \(A\) is a vector
\[
\mathbf{D}(A)
=
\left(
D^{\mu}(A),
D^{\nu}(A),
D^{\mathrm{gen}}(A),
D^{\mathrm{app}}(A),
D^{\mathrm{eval}}(A),
D^{\mathrm{exp}}(A),
D^{\mathrm{coord}}(A)
\right),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(D^{\mu}(A)\) is the effort required to reduce miscoding;
    \item \(D^{\nu}(A)\) is the effort required to reduce mismodel;
    \item \(D^{\mathrm{gen}}(A)\) is candidate-generation effort;
    \item \(D^{\mathrm{app}}(A)\) is application or execution effort;
    \item \(D^{\mathrm{eval}}(A)\) is evaluation and validation effort;
    \item \(D^{\mathrm{exp}}(A)\) is experimental effort;
    \item \(D^{\mathrm{coord}}(A)\) is coordination effort.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

This profile gives a more informative view than a scalar measure. Particle physics may have high experimental and monetary effort but strong validation. Philosophy may have lower monetary cost but higher representational and validation effort. Biology may have high miscoding effort because living systems are heterogeneous and measurement is difficult. Mathematics may have low experimental cost but high proof-search effort.

The concept of difficulty profiles avoids simplistic comparisons between disciplines. It allows us to say not only that an area is difficult, but why it is difficult.

\section{Effort and Research Prioritization}
\label{sec:effort_research_prioritization}

Effort is central to research prioritization. A research question should not be evaluated only by its relevance, applicability, or expected nescience reduction. It should also be evaluated by the effort required to obtain that reduction.

Let \(q\) be a research question. Suppose that pursuing \(q\) is expected to reduce nescience by
\[
\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(q)]
\]
and requires expected effort
\[
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Eff}(q)].
\]
A simple measure of research efficiency is
\[
R(q)=
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(q)]}{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Eff}(q)]}.
\]

If \(I(q)\) denotes the interestingness of \(q\), one may include effort as a denominator:
\[
I(q)=
\frac{
\operatorname{Rel}(q)\cdot \operatorname{App}(q)\cdot \mathbb{E}[\Delta N(q)]
}{
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Eff}(q)]
},
\]
where \(\operatorname{Rel}(q)\) is relevance and \(\operatorname{App}(q)\) is applicability. This formula is not intended as a universal definition, but it illustrates how effort can be incorporated into the selection of research questions.

Research funding can also be formulated as a portfolio problem. Suppose that actions \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\) are available, with monetary costs \(M(a_i)\) and expected nescience reductions \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(a_i)]\). Given a budget \(B\), the objective is to choose a subset of actions maximizing expected nescience reduction:
\[
\max_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}[\Delta N(a_i)]
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_{i\in S}M(a_i)\leq B.
\]
This connects the theory of nescience with scientific management and funding allocation.

\section{Limits of Effort Reduction}
\label{sec:limits_effort_reduction}

The effort required to reduce nescience may be infinite.

\begin{definition}
Let \(x\in\mathcal{H}\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). We say that the reduction of nescience by \(\varepsilon\) from \(x\) is \emph{effort-impossible} if
\[
\operatorname{Eff}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\infty.
\]
\end{definition}

This situation may occur for several reasons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item no better hypothesis exists within the chosen hypothesis space;
    \item better hypotheses exist but are not reachable under the admissible transformations;
    \item the improvement requires non-computable information;
    \item the entity is unknowable under the relevant representation framework;
    \item the effort is finite in principle but astronomically large in practice;
    \item the search procedure cannot evaluate improvements because the relevant approximation of nescience is unavailable.
\end{itemize}

These cases should be distinguished. A problem may be theoretically impossible, computationally intractable, economically infeasible, experimentally inaccessible, or conceptually blocked. The correct response depends on the kind of barrier. If the barrier is computational, one may seek better algorithms. If it is experimental, one may need better instruments. If it is representational, one may need a new representation. If it is conceptual, one may need a reframing of the entity itself.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:effort_conclusion}

Nescience measures how much we do not know. Effort measures how difficult it is to reduce what we do not know. The two concepts are orthogonal. A hypothesis may have high nescience and low effort if many easy improvements are available. Another may have low nescience and high effort if it lies near a mature frontier where further progress requires difficult search.

The foundation of effort is computational complexity. Descriptions are Turing machines with inputs; they have execution times. Augmentations, reductions, and other transformations are computable operations; they have application times. Most importantly, the discovery of useful transformations requires search; this search has computational complexity. Thus, effort is best understood as the computational complexity of discovering, applying, and validating nescience-reducing transformations.

This formulation explains why effort is not a component of nescience. Nescience evaluates the quality of a hypothesis. Effort evaluates the cost of improving it. The distinction makes it possible to study marginal effort, expected effort, conditional effort, effort landscapes, and the difficulty of research areas.

The concept also connects the theory of nescience with practical questions. By introducing resource vectors and price vectors, computational effort can be related to monetary cost. This makes it possible, at least in principle, to ask how much it costs to reduce the nescience of a topic by a specified amount. By considering Landauer's principle, one obtains a fundamental physical lower bound on irreversible information-erasing implementations of effort. By applying the theory to machine learning, one can analyze the cost of data acquisition, feature search, model selection, training, validation, and automation.

Finally, effort provides a way to compare research areas. Some fields may progress faster not because their questions are more important or their researchers more capable, but because their entities admit lower-effort reductions of nescience. Stable representations, efficient descriptions, strong validation procedures, and structured search spaces all reduce effort. Conversely, unstable representations, weak feedback mechanisms, and poorly structured candidate spaces increase effort.

In this way, effort complements nescience. Nescience tells us where ignorance remains. Effort tells us how hard it may be to reduce it.
