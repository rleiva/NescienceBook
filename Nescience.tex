\chapter{Nescience}
\label{chap:Nescience}

\begin{quote}
\begin{flushright}
\emph{There are known knowns. These are things we know that we know.
There are known unknowns. That is to say, there are things that we know we don't know.
But there are also unknown unknowns. There are things we don't know we don't know.} \\
Donald Rumsfeld
\end{flushright}
\end{quote}
\bigskip

Following Chapters \ref{chap:Entities}, \ref{chap:Representations}, and \ref{chap:Descriptions}, which introduced the basic objects of the theory, and Chapters \ref{chap:Miscoding} and \ref{chap:Mismodel}, which developed its two fundamental sources of epistemic error, we are now in a position to introduce the central concept of this book: \emph{nescience}. Nescience measures the extent to which our research hypotheses fail to provide an adequate understanding of the entities under investigation.

Unlike Shannon entropy or Kolmogorov complexity, which quantify properties of strings or information sources, nescience is a framework for quantifying ignorance. It characterizes ignorance through two complementary components. The first is \emph{miscoding}, which measures the representational error of a hypothesis, namely how far a candidate representation is from an adequate encoding of the entity under study. The second is \emph{mismodel}, which measures the descriptive error of a hypothesis, namely how far a candidate description is from an adequate model of the chosen representation. Together, these two quantities capture the two principal ways in which scientific understanding may fail: through inadequate representations or inadequate descriptions.

Because these two components are closely related, improving one may require revising the other. A better representation may invalidate a previously successful description, while improvements to a description may expose limitations of the current hypothesis and motivate the search for a different representation-description pair. For this reason, the theory formulates science as a \emph{bi-objective optimization problem}, whose aim is to reduce representational and descriptive error simultaneously. This viewpoint leads naturally to the notions of Pareto optimality and perfect knowledge developed in this chapter.

The same framework also provides a systematic way to classify the domain of inquiry according to the current state of our hypotheses. Some entities belong to the region of the \emph{known knowns}, for which at least one known hypothesis already achieves negligible nescience. Others belong to the \emph{known unknowns}, for which hypotheses exist but none yet attain that level of understanding. Finally, there are the \emph{unknown unknowns}: entities for which no hypothesis has yet been formulated. One of the central goals of the theory is to provide a formal basis for understanding this last region and, ultimately, for developing methods capable of bringing parts of it into scientific inquiry.

The theory also leads to a more subtle conclusion. Scientific progress is not guaranteed simply by accumulating additional hypotheses. In some situations, inquiry reaches a point at which neither component of nescience can be reduced without increasing the other. At such points, further progress requires a change of representation, a new descriptive framework, or a different criterion for selecting among competing hypotheses. Nescience therefore does more than quantify ignorance: it also characterizes the structural limits within which scientific progress must operate.

\section{Nescience}

Intuitively, our understanding of an entity depends on two complementary aspects of scientific work: the construction of a representation that encodes the entity, and the construction of a description that models that representation. According to the theory of nescience, there are two fundamental sources of ignorance, captured by the notions of miscoding and mismodel. Miscoding measures how far a candidate representation is from being algorithmically equivalent to an admissible representation of the entity, whereas mismodel measures how far a candidate description is from being algorithmically equivalent to a minimal description of the given representation.

Together, miscoding and mismodel characterize the nescience associated with a hypothesis. Since these quantities measure distinct sources of epistemic error, neither can generally be reduced to the other. We therefore define nescience as a two-dimensional quantity, preserving both components rather than combining them into a single scalar value.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ be a hypothesis. We define the \emph{nescience}\index{Nescience} of $(r,d)$ with respect to $e$ as
\[
\mathbf N(e,r,d) = \left(\mu(e,r),  \nu(r,d)\right)
\]
\end{definition}

Nescience thus measures two complementary aspects of our lack of knowledge: the inadequacy of the representation with respect to the entity, and the inadequacy of the description with respect to the representation. Although miscoding depends only on $e$ and $r$, whereas mismodel depends on $r$ and $d$, the two components are not independent. Modifying a representation may also affect the adequacy of its description, and improvements in one component may come at the expense of the other.

From this perspective, scientific inquiry consists in finding hypotheses with progressively lower nescience. Since nescience is a vector rather than a scalar, minimizing it requires considering both components simultaneously. This leads naturally to the formulation of science as a bi-objective optimization problem.

\begin{tBox}
\textbf{The Science Problem}
\index{Science problem}
\begin{align*}
 & \text{Given an entity} \quad e\in\mathcal E\\
 & \quad \text{minimize} \quad \mathbf N(e,r,d)\\
 & \quad \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e,
\end{align*}
\end{tBox}

In general, reducing one component of nescience may increase the other, so a hypothesis that simultaneously minimizes both components need not exist.

The feasible region\index{Feasible region} is the set
\[
\mathcal H_e = \{(r,d)\in\mathcal H:r\in\mathcal R_e\},
\]
which contains all extended representation-description pairs associated with the entity $e$. The decision variables\index{Decision variable} are the representation $r$ and its description $d$, while nescience $\mathbf N(e,r,d)$ is the vector-valued objective function\index{Objective function} to be minimized. The corresponding objective space\index{Objective space} is
\[
\mathbf Z_e = \left\{\mathbf N(e,r,d):(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e \right\} \subseteq\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2.
\]
Our primary interest lies in the feasible decision space $\mathcal H_e$, rather than merely in the objective space $\mathbf Z_e$. While the latter characterizes the attainable levels of nescience, the former contains the representations and descriptions through which scientific knowledge is expressed. The objective of scientific inquiry is therefore not simply to identify minimum values of miscoding and mismodel, but to discover the hypotheses that attain the best possible combinations of these values.

\subsection*{Perfect Knowledge}
\label{sec:perfect_knowledge}

Perfect knowledge of an entity therefore requires both an adequate representation of the entity and an optimal description of that representation. This leads to a structural definition of perfect knowledge, based on admissible representations and minimal descriptions.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $(r,d)\in\mathcal{H}_e$ be a hypothesis about $e$. We say that the hypothesis $(r,d)$ attains \emph{perfect knowledge}\index{Perfect knowledge} of the entity $e$ if
\[
r\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_e \qquad\text{and}\qquad d\in\mathcal{D}_r^\star.
\]
\end{definition}

Perfect knowledge is thus attained when the representation is admissible for the entity and its description is minimal. The representation contains the information required to reconstruct the entity without independent, semantically irrelevant content, while the description reconstructs the representation using the shortest possible effective encoding.

This structural definition is preferable to requiring miscoding and mismodel to be exactly zero. Both quantities are based on conditional Kolmogorov complexity, whose values depend on the reference universal machine up to additive constants. Even an admissible representation or a minimal description may therefore have a small nonzero defect, reflecting the fixed computational overhead required to reproduce its conditional input.

For this reason, we also introduce a weaker notion that tolerates constant-size discrepancies. Fix a reference universal machine and constants $c_\mu,c_\nu \geq 0$, chosen independently of the entity and hypothesis, and sufficiently large to accommodate the constant overhead of reproducing a conditional input.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ be an entity, and let $(r,d)\in\mathcal{H}_e$ be a hypothesis about $e$. We say that $(r,d)$ attains \emph{negligible perfect knowledge} \index{Negligible perfect knowledge} of the entity $e$ if
\[
\mu(e,r)\leq c_\mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nu(r,d)\leq c_\nu.
\]
\end{definition}

Equivalently, both defects must be bounded by fixed constants independent of the entity representation, and description. This condition is commonly expressed in algorithmic information
theory by writing
\[
\mu(e,r)=O(1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nu(r,d)=O(1),
\]
where the bounds are understood to be uniform over the objects under consideration.

Negligible perfect knowledge allows a hypothesis to differ from the theoretical ideal by a constant amount of algorithmic information. Unlike exact zero-error conditions, this notion accommodates the additive ambiguities inherent in Kolmogorov complexity. The following proposition establishes its relationship with perfect knowledge.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:minimal_rep_minimal_desc_negligible_pk}
Every hypothesis attaining perfect knowledge also attains negligible perfect knowledge.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $e\in\mathcal{E}$ and let $(r,d)\in\mathcal{H}_e$ attain perfect knowledge. By definition, $r\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_e$ and $d\in\mathcal{D}_r^\star$. Since $r$ is an admissible representation, we may choose $r'=r$ in the definitions of representational deficiency and surplus. Hence
\[
d_R(e,r)\leq K(r\mid r)=O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,r)\leq K(r\mid r)=O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\} = O(1).
\]
Similarly, since $d$ is a minimal description of $r$, we may choose $d'=d$ in the definitions of descriptive
deficiency and surplus. Therefore,
\[
d_D(r,d)\leq K(d\mid d)=O(1),
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d)\leq K(d\mid d)=O(1).
\]
It follows that
\[
\nu(r,d) = \max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\} = O(1).
\]
The bounds on $K(r\mid r)$ and $K(d\mid d)$ are uniform, since a fixed program can reproduce its conditional input. Thus, both defects are bounded by the prescribed constants $c_\mu$ and $c_\nu$, and the hypothesis attains negligible perfect knowledge.
\end{proof}

The converse does not necessarily hold. A representation may differ from an admissible representation by only a constant amount of algorithmic information without itself being admissible. Likewise, a description may be algorithmically close to a minimal description without being minimal. Negligible perfect knowledge therefore permits small structural imperfections that perfect knowledge excludes.

Perfect knowledge provides an ideal reference for scientific inquiry, whereas negligible perfect knowledge accommodates the fixed coding overhead inherent in its mathematical formulation.

\subsection*{Pareto Optimality}
\label{sec:nescience_pareto}

The science problem is a bi-objective optimization problem in which miscoding and mismodel are minimized simultaneously. Given an entity $e\in\mathcal E$, scientific inquiry seeks hypotheses that improve our understanding of $e$ by reducing one or both components of nescience. However, an improvement in one component may come at the expense of the other. This motivates the notion of Pareto dominance.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $(r,d),(r',d')\in\mathcal H_e$ be two hypotheses. We say that $(r,d)$
\emph{dominates}\index{Dominance} $(r',d')$ if
\[
\mu(e,r)\leq\mu(e,r'), \qquad \nu(r,d)\leq\nu(r',d'),
\]
with at least one strict inequality.
\end{definition}

Dominance characterizes unambiguous scientific improvement. A dominating hypothesis reduces at least one source of ignorance without increasing the other. Consequently, dominated hypotheses are suboptimal with respect to the two objectives of the science problem (see Section~\ref{sec:multiobjective_optimization}).

Nevertheless, improvements in miscoding and mismodel need not coincide. Changing the representation may reduce miscoding while making the current description less adequate. Conversely, improving the description of a fixed representation reduces mismodel without changing miscoding. When these objectives cannot be minimized simultaneously, scientific inquiry encounters trade-offs between alternative hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity. A hypothesis $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ is \emph{Pareto optimal}
\index{Pareto optimal} if no other hypothesis in $\mathcal H_e$ dominates it.

We define the set of Pareto-optimal hypotheses of $e$ as
\[
\mathcal P_e = \left\{ (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e : \nexists (r',d')\in\mathcal H_e \text{ that dominates } (r,d) \right\}.
\]
The corresponding \emph{Pareto frontier}\index{Pareto frontier} is the set of objective vectors associated with Pareto-optimal hypotheses:
\[
\mathcal F_e = \left\{ \bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\bigr) : (r,d)\in\mathcal P_e \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Pareto-optimal hypotheses represent the best attainable trade-offs between miscoding and mismodel within the feasible hypothesis space. For such hypotheses, neither component can be strictly reduced without increasing the other. The Pareto frontier therefore identifies the objective vectors from which a decision maker may select a preferred solution.

An important property of the science problem follows from the discrete nature of its objective functions. Since miscoding and mismodel are defined in terms of conditional Kolmogorov complexity, both take non-negative integer values. This guarantees the existence of Pareto-optimal hypotheses whenever the feasible set is nonempty.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:science_pareto_existence}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\mathcal H_e\neq\varnothing$. Then
\[
\mathcal P_e\neq\varnothing.
\]
Moreover, the Pareto frontier $\mathcal F_e$ is finite.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mathcal H_e$ is nonempty and miscoding takes values in $\mathbb N_0$, there exists a hypothesis
$h_0=(r_0,d_0)\in\mathcal H_e$ minimizing miscoding. Among all hypotheses attaining this minimum, choose one that minimizes mismodel. Such a hypothesis exists because mismodel also takes values in $\mathbb N_0$.

The hypothesis $h_0$ is Pareto optimal. Indeed, any hypothesis dominating $h_0$ would either have smaller miscoding, contradicting its minimality, or have equal miscoding and smaller mismodel, contradicting the choice of $h_0$. Hence $\mathcal P_e\neq\varnothing$.

To prove finiteness, let $a=\mu(e, r_0)$ and  $b=\nu(r_0,d_0)$. Every Pareto-optimal objective vector different from $(a,b)$ must have its first coordinate greater than $a$ and its second coordinate strictly smaller than $b$; otherwise, it would be dominated by $(a,b)$.

Since mismodel takes non-negative integer values, the second coordinate of any such vector belongs to
$\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}$. Furthermore, two distinct Pareto-optimal objective vectors cannot have the same second coordinate, since the one with smaller first coordinate would dominate the other.

Therefore $\mathcal F_e$ contains at most $b+1$ distinct objective vectors, and is finite.
\end{proof}

This result establishes that the science problem always admits at least one Pareto-optimal hypothesis when its
feasible set is nonempty. Moreover, although infinitely many hypotheses may be Pareto optimal, their objective
vectors form a finite frontier.

Perfect knowledge, negligible perfect knowledge, and Pareto optimality characterize different aspects of scientific understanding. Perfect knowledge requires an admissible representation and a minimal description, and therefore implies negligible perfect knowledge, in which both miscoding and mismodel are bounded by fixed constants. Pareto optimality, by contrast, is a relative notion: it requires only that no other feasible hypothesis dominates the one under consideration. Consequently, a Pareto-optimal hypothesis may still exhibit substantial miscoding or mismodel, while a hypothesis attaining negligible perfect knowledge may nevertheless be dominated by another with smaller defects.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the motion of a harmonic oscillator. Suppose that two hypotheses are
currently known, with objective vectors $h_1:(3,12)$, and $h_2:(10,4)$, where the first coordinate denotes miscoding and the second denotes mismodel. The first hypothesis uses a relatively accurate representation but an inadequate description. The second uses a poorer representation but describes it more effectively. Neither hypothesis dominates the other. Thus, both are Pareto optimal among the currently known hypotheses, even though neither attains negligible perfect knowledge.

Now suppose that a hypothesis $h^\star=(r,d^\star)$ is discovered, where $r$ is an admissible representation and $d^\star$ is a minimal description. By definition, $h^\star$ attains perfect knowledge and therefore negligible perfect knowledge.

Consider also a hypothesis $h'=(r,d')$ obtained by adding a fixed redundant instruction to $d^\star$. This modification leaves the reconstructed representation unchanged and introduces only constant-size descriptive overhead. Hence $h'$ attains negligible perfect knowledge, but not perfect knowledge, because $d'$ is not minimal. For illustration, suppose their objective vectors are $h^\star:(2,2)$ and $h':(2,3)$. The hypothesis $h^\star$ dominates all three alternatives. In particular, $h'$ illustrates that negligible perfect knowledge does not imply Pareto optimality.
\end{example}

These numerical values are illustrative rather than computed Kolmogorov complexities. The example distinguishes structural perfection, negligible defects, and Pareto optimality relative to the hypotheses under consideration.

\subsection*{Ideal Objective Vector}
\label{sec:ideal_objective_vector}

In multi-objective optimization, the \emph{ideal objective vector} \index{Ideal vector} is the point in objective space whose coordinates are the minimum attainable values of the individual objectives. Although these minima need not be attainable simultaneously, the ideal vector provides a natural reference for evaluating solutions and interpreting the Pareto frontier.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\mathcal H_e\neq\varnothing$. We define the \emph{ideal objective vector} of the science problem for $e$ as
\[
\mathbf z_e^I = \left(\min_{(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e}\mu(e,r), \min_{(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e}\nu(r,d) \right).
\]
\end{definition}

Since miscoding and mismodel are non-negative integer-valued quantities, both minima exist whenever the hypothesis space is nonempty. Consequently, the ideal vector is well defined, although it need not correspond to any feasible hypothesis.

An important property of the science problem is that, for knowable entities, the ideal objective vector lies within a constant-size distance of the origin.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity admitting at least one admissible representation. Then
\[
\mathbf z_e^I=(O(1),O(1)).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\bar r\in\overline{\mathcal R}_e$ be an admissible representation of $e$, and let $d^\star\in\mathcal D_{\bar r}^\star$ be a minimal description of $\bar r$. By the properties of miscoding and mismodel, $\mu(e,\bar r)=O(1)$, $\nu(\bar r,d^\star)=O(1)$. Since $(\bar r,d^\star)\in\mathcal H_e$, the coordinates of the ideal objective vector cannot exceed the corresponding values of this hypothesis. Therefore,
\[
0\leq z_{e,\mu}^I\leq\mu(e,\bar r)=O(1),
\]
and
\[
0\leq z_{e,\nu}^I\leq\nu(\bar r,d^\star)=O(1).
\]
Hence $\mathbf z_e^I=(O(1),O(1))$.
\end{proof}

This result establishes that the ideal objective vector of a knowable entity differs from the origin by at most constant-size coding overhead. Nevertheless, the two points need not coincide. The origin $(0,0)$ remains a universal lower-bound reference, representing the complete absence of both defects.

A hypothesis that attains both coordinates of the ideal objective vector is necessarily Pareto optimal. However,
such a hypothesis need not exist, since the individual minima may be attained by different hypotheses. Moreover, attaining the ideal objective vector should not be confused with perfect knowledge, which is defined structurally in terms of admissible representations and minimal descriptions.

Finally, the absolute values of miscoding and mismodel are not universally bounded above. When bounded measures are required, the corresponding relative versions introduced in Chapters \ref{chap:Miscoding} and \ref{chap:Mismodel} may be considered.

% Section: Trade-offs

\subsection*{Trade-offs}
\label{sec:nescience_tradeoffs}

The science problem seeks to minimize miscoding and mismodel simultaneously. However, this long-term objective does not imply that every individual step of scientific inquiry should improve both quantities. For example, adopting a better representation may initially increase mismodel because existing descriptions are no longer adequate. Such temporary deterioration may be justified if it opens the way to hypotheses with lower overall nescience. Understanding the trade-offs between miscoding and mismodel is therefore essential to understanding how scientific knowledge can progress.

A natural way to quantify the relationship between miscoding and mismodel is to compare their changes between two hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let $\mathbf{x}_1=(r_1,d_1)$ and $\mathbf{x}_2=(r_2,d_2)$ be two hypotheses in $\mathcal H_e$ such that $\mu(e,r_1)\neq\mu(e,r_2)$. We define the \emph{nescience trade-off ratio} \index{Trade-off ratio} between these hypotheses as
\[
\Delta_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2) = \frac{\Delta_\nu(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2)}{\Delta_\mu(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2)} 
= \frac{\nu(r_2,d_2)-\nu(r_1,d_1)} {\mu(e,r_2)-\mu(e,r_1)}.
\]
\end{definition}

The trade-off ratio measures the change in mismodel per unit change in miscoding. A positive value indicates that both objectives change in the same direction, while a negative value indicates that one improves at the expense of the other. A zero value means that mismodel remains unchanged despite a change in miscoding. When miscoding remains unchanged, the ratio is undefined. In that case, a strict difference in mismodel establishes dominance directly.

The ratio is symmetric:
\[
\Delta_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2) = \Delta_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{x}_2,\mathbf{x}_1).
\]
It therefore describes the relationship between two objective vectors, but not the direction of improvement.

A negative trade-off ratio does not necessarily imply that either hypothesis is Pareto optimal. Both hypotheses may be dominated by a third alternative. However, for distinct objective vectors belonging to the Pareto frontier, the ratio must always be negative.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2\in\mathcal P_e$ be Pareto-optimal hypotheses with distinct objective vectors.
Then 
\[
\Delta_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2) < 0.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, without loss of generality, that
\[
\mu(e,r_1)<\mu(e,r_2).
\]
Since both hypotheses are Pareto optimal, we must have
\[
\nu(r_1,d_1)>\nu(r_2,d_2).
\]
Otherwise, $\mathbf{x}_1$ would dominate $\mathbf{x}_2$. Consequently, the numerator of the trade-off ratio is
negative while its denominator is positive, and therefore
\[
\Delta_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2)<0.
\]
If the miscoding values were equal, the hypothesis with smaller mismodel would dominate the other, unless their objective vectors were identical. The same reasoning applies to equal mismodel values.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that distinct points on the Pareto frontier necessarily represent conflicting objectives. A reduction in miscoding along the frontier must be accompanied by an increase in mismodel, and conversely. The trade-off ratio quantifies this exchange, although its magnitude depends on the particular points being compared.

The unrestricted science problem has an additional property arising from the existence of minimal descriptions.
Recall that every representation admits at least one minimal exact description, and that such descriptions
have negligible mismodel. This places a uniform bound on the descriptive component of every Pareto-optimal hypothesis.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:pareto_mismodel_bound}
There exists a constant $c_\nu$, independent of the entity and hypothesis, such that every $(r,d)\in\mathcal P_e$ satisfies
\[
\nu(r,d)\leq c_\nu.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(r,d)\in\mathcal P_e$ be a Pareto-optimal hypothesis, and choose a minimal description $d^\star\in\mathcal D_r^\star$. By the definitions of descriptive deficiency and surplus,
\[
d_D(r,d^\star)\leq K(d^\star\mid d^\star),
\]
and
\[
s_D(r,d^\star)\leq K(d^\star\mid d^\star).
\]
Since a fixed program can reproduce its conditional input, there exists a constant $c_\nu$ such that
\[
K(d^\star\mid d^\star)\leq c_\nu
\]
for every description $d^\star$. Hence
\[
\nu(r,d^\star)\leq c_\nu.
\]
Suppose now that $\nu(r,d)>c_\nu$. Then
\[
\nu(r,d^\star)<\nu(r,d).
\]
Since the representation is unchanged, the hypotheses $(r,d^\star)$ and $(r,d)$ have identical miscoding.
Therefore $(r,d^\star)$ dominates $(r,d)$, contradicting Pareto optimality.

Consequently, $\nu(r,d)\leq c_\nu$.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that the theoretical Pareto frontier is confined to a bounded region in the mismodel direction, even though mismodel itself is not universally bounded over all hypotheses. This result reveals an important distinction between descriptive adequacy and scientific understanding. Every finite representation admits a minimal description, regardless of how poorly it represents the underlying entity. Consequently, negligible mismodel alone does not guarantee adequate knowledge of that entity: a description may reproduce an incomplete, misleading, or erroneous representation without correcting its defects. This observation highlights the fundamental importance of representational adequacy in scientific understanding. No description, however accurate or economical, can compensate for a representation that fundamentally mischaracterizes the entity under investigation. Nevertheless, an adequate representation alone does not constitute complete understanding: it must also be accompanied by an adequate description. Scientific knowledge therefore requires addressing both
sources of epistemic error.

% Section: Local Pareto Optimality

\subsection*{Local Pareto Optimality}
\label{sec:local_pareto_optimality}

Pareto optimality is a global property: a hypothesis is Pareto optimal if no other hypothesis about the same entity dominates it. However, scientific inquiry often proceeds through limited modifications of existing representations and descriptions. This motivates a local notion of Pareto optimality, in which hypotheses are compared only with alternatives that are sufficiently close in algorithmic terms.

To formalize this notion, we measure the proximity between two hypotheses through the algorithmic information required to transform their representations and descriptions into one another.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, let $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ be a hypothesis, and let $\varepsilon>0$. We define the \emph{algorithmic neighborhood} \index{Algorithmic neighborhood} of $(r,d)$ relative to $e$ as
\[
\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d) = \left\{ (r',d') \in\mathcal H_e : 
K(r'\mid r)+K(r\mid r')\leq\varepsilon \land K(d'\mid d)+K(d\mid d')\leq\varepsilon \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Two hypotheses belong to the same algorithmic neighborhood when their representations and descriptions can be transformed into one another using programs of bounded length. Requiring transformations in both directions ensures that proximity accounts for the information needed both to introduce and to reverse a modification.

The neighborhood is restricted to $\mathcal H_e$, so every hypothesis under consideration concerns the same entity. Nevertheless, algorithmic proximity does not necessarily imply semantic similarity. A short program may produce a substantial transformation, while a seemingly minor scientific modification may require considerable additional information. Moreover, program length measures the information needed to specify a transformation, not the computational effort required to execute it.

We can now introduce local Pareto optimality.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ a hypothesis, and $\varepsilon>0$. We say that $(r,d)$ is \emph{locally Pareto optimal} \index{Local Pareto optimal} with respect to $\varepsilon$ if no hypothesis in $\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d)$ dominates it.

We denote the set of locally Pareto-optimal hypotheses by
\[
\mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e = \left\{(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e:
\nexists(r',d')\in\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d) \text{ that dominates }(r,d) \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

A locally Pareto optimal hypothesis cannot be improved through a dominating alternative within its algorithmic neighborhood. This does not imply that its miscoding or mismodel is negligible, nor that better hypotheses do not exist elsewhere in the hypothesis space. Local Pareto optimality therefore expresses a restricted form of optimality, determined by the permitted algorithmic transformations.

An important property of algorithmic neighborhoods is that they contain only finitely many hypotheses for any fixed radius.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$, and $\varepsilon>0$. Then $\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d)$ is finite.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $(r',d')\in\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d)$, then, in particular $K(r'\mid r)\leq\varepsilon$ and $K(d'\mid d)\leq\varepsilon$. For a fixed input $r$, only finitely many programs have length at most $\varepsilon$. Consequently, only finitely many strings $r'$ can satisfy the first inequality.

The same argument applies to descriptions: for a fixed input $d$, only finitely many strings $d'$ can satisfy
the second inequality. Therefore, only finitely many representation-description pairs can belong to $\mathcal N_{\varepsilon,e}(r,d)$.
\end{proof}

This result shows that algorithmic neighborhoods provide finite subsets of the hypothesis space, even when $\mathcal H_e$ is infinite. However, their finiteness does not imply that their elements can be effectively identified, since conditional Kolmogorov complexity is generally not computable.

Global and local Pareto optimality are closely related. A globally Pareto-optimal hypothesis must also be locally Pareto optimal, since restricting the comparison to a neighborhood cannot introduce a dominating alternative.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:global_implies_local_pareto_eps}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Then
\[
\mathcal P_e \subseteq \mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(r,d)\in\mathcal P_e$. By definition, no hypothesis in $\mathcal H_e$ dominates $(r,d)$.

Since $\mathcal N^\varepsilon_e(r,d) \subseteq\mathcal H_e$, no hypothesis in its algorithmic neighborhood can
dominate it either. Therefore, $(r,d)\in\mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e$.
\end{proof}

The converse does not generally follow. A hypothesis may be locally Pareto optimal even though another hypothesis outside its neighborhood dominates it. In such a case, the dominating alternative requires a transformation whose bidirectional algorithmic information exceeds the specified radius in at least one of the two components.

\begin{example}
A hypothesis about a physical phenomenon may be based on a particular collection of measurements and a mathematical description of those measurements. It may be locally Pareto optimal under small changes to its representation and description, while another hypothesis based on substantially different observations and a different descriptive framework achieves lower miscoding and mismodel. Whether these hypotheses are algorithmically close depends on the information required to transform their encodings, rather than merely on their scientific interpretation.
\end{example}

Local Pareto optimality also depends on the neighborhood radius. Increasing the radius enlarges the set of hypotheses against which a candidate is compared.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ and $0<\varepsilon_1\leq\varepsilon_2$. Then
\[
\mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon_2)}_e \subseteq \mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon_1)}_e.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$, the definition of algorithmic neighborhood gives
\[
\mathcal N^{\varepsilon_1}_e(r,d) \subseteq \mathcal N^{\varepsilon_2}_e(r,d).
\]
If no hypothesis in the larger neighborhood dominates $(r,d)$, then no hypothesis in the smaller neighborhood can dominate it. Therefore, local Pareto optimality at radius $\varepsilon_2$ implies local Pareto optimality at radius $\varepsilon_1$.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that local Pareto optimality becomes more demanding as the neighborhood radius increases. A hypothesis that is locally Pareto optimal for a large radius is protected against a broader collection of dominating alternatives.

In fact, global Pareto optimality can be characterized entirely in terms of local Pareto optimality over all possible radii.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity. Then
\[
\mathcal P_e = \bigcap_{\varepsilon>0} \mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition \ref{prop:global_implies_local_pareto_eps}, every globally Pareto-optimal hypothesis is locally
Pareto optimal for every $\varepsilon>0$. Hence
\[
\mathcal P_e \subseteq \bigcap_{\varepsilon>0} \mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e.
\]
Conversely, suppose that $(r,d)\notin\mathcal P_e$. Then there exists a hypothesis $(r',d')\in\mathcal H_e$ that dominates $(r,d)$.

Since $r,r',d,d'$ are finite binary strings, their conditional Kolmogorov complexities are finite. Therefore, we can choose $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
K(r'\mid r)+K(r\mid r')\leq\varepsilon
\]
and
\[
K(d'\mid d)+K(d\mid d')\leq\varepsilon.
\]
It follows that
\[
(r',d')\in\mathcal N_{\varepsilon,e}(r,d).
\]
Since $(r',d')$ dominates $(r,d)$, the hypothesis $(r,d)$ is not locally Pareto optimal for this radius. Hence
\[
(r,d)\notin
\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0} \mathcal P^{\mathrm{loc}(\varepsilon)}_e.
\]
Combining both inclusions establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that global Pareto optimality is equivalent to local Pareto optimality at every algorithmic scale. A hypothesis that is locally Pareto optimal only for a restricted range of radii may still be dominated by a more distant alternative. By contrast, a hypothesis that remains locally Pareto optimal for arbitrarily large radii must be globally Pareto optimal.

Local Pareto optimality therefore provides a scale dependent characterization of the science problem. It distinguishes the absence of improvements within bounded algorithmic transformations from the absence of improvements throughout the entire hypothesis space. This distinction is important when examining the structure of scientific inquiry, since the lack of a nearby dominating hypothesis does not establish that the limits of scientific understanding have been reached.

\section{Scalar Nescience}
\label{sec:scalar_nescience}

From a mathematical perspective, any Pareto-optimal hypothesis is a valid solution to the Science Problem. In scientific practice, however, the objective is generally not to identify the entire Pareto set, but to find a single hypothesis that best satisfies the goals, constraints, and standards of the investigation.

In multi-objective optimization, a decision maker\index{Decision maker} (see Section \ref{sec:multiobjective_optimization}) expresses preferences among alternative solutions. These preferences can be represented by a scalarization function that assigns a numerical value to each objective vector, thereby reducing the problem of selecting a hypothesis to a single-objective optimization problem.

\begin{definition}
Let $V:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ be a \emph{scalarization function}\index{Scalarization function}. We define the \emph{scalar nescience}\index{Scalar nescience} induced by $V$ for an entity $e\in\mathcal E$ and a hypothesis $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ as
\[
N_V(e,r,d) = V\bigl(\mathbf N(e,r,d)\bigr) = V\bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

The function $V$, also called a \emph{value function} \index{Value function}, expresses the preferences of the decision maker. Depending on the objectives of the investigation, it may treat miscoding and mismodel symmetrically, assign greater importance to one of them, or penalize imbalances between the two. Consequently, different scalarization functions may assign different rankings to the same collection of hypotheses.

The corresponding scalar optimization problem is
\[
\min_{(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e} N_V(e,r,d).
\]

A desirable property of a scalarization function is that it should not select a hypothesis dominated by another feasible alternative. The following proposition establishes a sufficient condition for this property.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:strictly_increasing_dm_pareto}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity and let $V:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ be strictly increasing in each coordinate. Then every global minimizer of
\[
\min_{(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e} V\bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\bigr)
\]
is Pareto optimal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(r^\star,d^\star)$ be a global minimizer. Suppose, for contradiction, that it is not Pareto optimal. Then there exists another hypothesis $(r',d')\in\mathcal H_e$ such that $\mu(e,r')\leq\mu(e,r^\star)$, and $\nu(r',d')\leq\nu(r^\star,d^\star)$, with at least one strict inequality. Since $V$ is strictly increasing in each coordinate, we obtain
\[
V\bigl(\mu(e,r'),\nu(r',d')\bigr) < V\bigl(\mu(e,r^\star),\nu(r^\star,d^\star)\bigr),
\]
contradicting the optimality of $(r^\star,d^\star)$. Therefore $(r^\star,d^\star)$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that any strictly increasing scalarization preserves the principle of Pareto dominance: a dominated hypothesis cannot minimize scalar nescience. However, the minimizer need not be unique, and different scalarization functions may select different Pareto-optimal hypotheses.

Unlike miscoding and mismodel, which aggregate their respective deficiency and surplus components through the maximum, scalar nescience does not have a uniquely prescribed aggregation rule. Representational and descriptive errors concern different aspects of scientific understanding, and their relative importance may depend on the objectives of the investigation.

Scalar nescience should therefore be understood as a decision-making criterion built upon the more fundamental bi-objective formulation. The theory specifies what constitutes representational and descriptive error, while the scalarization function determines how these errors are balanced when selecting a hypothesis.

The methods presented in the follwoing sections provide alternative criteria for selecting a hypothesis from the bi-objective Science Problem. Each expresses a different interpretation of the relationship between miscoding and mismodel, and none is universally preferable. The choice of scalarization depends on the objectives and constraints of the investigation. The fundamental formulation of nescience, however, remains bi-objective and independent of the decision criterion adopted.

% Section: Global Criterion

\subsection*{Global Criterion}
\label{sec:nescience_global_criterion}

The global criterion method (see Section \ref{sub:multiobjective_global_criterion}) selects a hypothesis by minimizing the distance between its objective vector and the ideal objective vector. Recall that, for an entity $e\in\mathcal E$, the ideal vector is
\[
\mathbf z_e^I = \bigl(z_{e,\mu}^I,z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr),
\]
where each coordinate represents the minimum attainable value of the corresponding objective function. Although these minima need not be attained simultaneously, the ideal vector provides a natural reference for evaluating competing hypotheses.

A general formulation of the global criterion uses the $L_p$ distance to the ideal vector:
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \left( \bigl(\mu(e,r)-z_{e,\mu}^I\bigr)^p
  + \bigl(\nu(r,d)-z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr)^p \right)^{1/p}\\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e, \qquad 1\leq p<\infty.
\end{align*}

The parameter $p$ determines how deviations from the ideal vector are aggregated. For $p=1$, the criterion minimizes their sum, while $p=2$ corresponds to the Euclidean distance. Larger values of $p$ place increasing emphasis on the largest deviation, thereby discouraging highly unbalanced solutions.

For every finite $p\geq1$, the global criterion is strictly increasing in both miscoding and mismodel. Consequently, every global minimizer is Pareto optimal, as established by Proposition \ref{prop:strictly_increasing_dm_pareto}.

In the theory of nescience, the ideal objective vector is generally unknown. Moreover, since miscoding and mismodel are defined through conditional Kolmogorov complexity, their minimum values need not be exactly zero, even for admissible representations and minimal descriptions. Nevertheless, for every knowable entity, the coordinates of the ideal vector are bounded by constants independent of the hypothesis:
\[
\mathbf z_e^I=(O(1),O(1)).
\]
This motivates replacing the ideal vector by the origin, which is a universal lower bound for both objectives. The resulting \emph{origin-based global criterion} is
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \left( \mu(e,r)^p+\nu(r,d)^p \right)^{1/p} \\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e, \qquad 1\leq p<\infty.
\end{align*}

This scalarization measures the distance from the complete absence of representational and descriptive error. It
does not require knowledge of the ideal objective vector and preserves Pareto optimality for every finite $p\geq1$.

An important property is that replacing the ideal vector with the origin introduces only a bounded difference between the corresponding theoretical distance values.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:origin_global_criterion_bound}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $1\leq p<\infty$. Define
\[
V_I(r,d) = \left(\bigl(\mu(e,r)-z_{e,\mu}^I\bigr)^p + \bigl(\nu(r,d)-z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr)^p \right)^{1/p}
\]
and
\[
V_0(r,d) = \left(\mu(e,r)^p+\nu(r,d)^p \right)^{1/p}.
\]
Then, for every $(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$,
\[
0 \leq V_0(r,d)-V_I(r,d) \leq \|\mathbf z_e^I\|_p=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathbf y=\bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\bigr)$. By definition of the ideal objective vector, $\mathbf y\geq\mathbf z_e^I\geq(0,0)$ componentwise. Therefore,
\[
\|\mathbf y-\mathbf z_e^I\|_p \leq\|\mathbf y\|_p.
\]
Moreover, by the triangle inequality,
\[
\|\mathbf y\|_p \leq \|\mathbf y-\mathbf z_e^I\|_p + \|\mathbf z_e^I\|_p.
\]
Combining these inequalities gives
\[
0 \leq V_0(r,d)-V_I(r,d) \leq\|\mathbf z_e^I\|_p.
\]
Since both coordinates of the ideal vector are $O(1)$ for knowable entities, its $L_p$ norm is also $O(1)$.
\end{proof}

Thus, using the origin instead of the ideal vector changes the theoretical global criterion by at most a constant-size amount. However, this does not guarantee that the two criteria select the same hypothesis.

For $p=1$, the relationship is particularly simple:
\[
V_I(r,d) = V_0(r,d)-z_{e,\mu}^I-z_{e,\nu}^I.
\]
Since the difference is constant across hypotheses, both criteria induce exactly the same ordering and
have the same global minimizers.

For $p>1$, replacing the ideal vector by the origin may change the ordering of hypotheses, as the following
example illustrates.

\begin{example}
Consider a science problem whose Pareto frontier consists of two objective vectors $A=(2,8)$ and $B=(6,5)$. The ideal objective vector is $\mathbf z_e^I=(2,5)$. Using the Euclidean distance to the ideal vector gives $V_I(A)=3$ and $V_I(B)=4$, so the global criterion selects $A$. Using the origin instead gives $V_0(A)=\sqrt{68}$ and $V_0(B)=\sqrt{61}$, so the origin-based criterion selects $B$. Both hypotheses are Pareto optimal.
\end{example}

The example shows that changing the reference point may alter the preferred trade-off without violating Pareto optimality.

In the limit $p\to\infty$, the origin-based global criterion becomes the Tchebycheff method\index{Tchebycheff method}:
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \max\{\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\}\\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e.
\end{align*}

This criterion minimizes the largest of the two defects. It is particularly interesting in the theory of nescience because it follows the same maximum-based aggregation principle used to define miscoding and mismodel from their respective deficiency and surplus components. Unlike the finite-$p$ criteria, however, the Tchebycheff criterion is not strictly increasing in both coordinates. Consequently, a dominated hypothesis may attain the same scalar value as a Pareto-optimal hypothesis.

The global criterion offers a geometrically intuitive method for selecting a hypothesis, but it does not
generally recover every Pareto-optimal solution. Different values of $p$ may favor different regions
of the Pareto frontier, and replacing the ideal vector by the origin may further influence the selection.

Furthermore, although miscoding and mismodel are both measured in bits, their numerical ranges may differ substantially. Consequently, an unweighted global criterion may implicitly assign greater importance to one objective. This can be addressed through fixed positive scaling factors or by considering the corresponding relative measures introduced in Chapters \ref{chap:Miscoding} and \ref{chap:Mismodel}. Such transformations must be interpreted carefully, since changing the objectives may also change their ordering and ideal values.

The origin-based global criterion is therefore a natural scalarization for the theory of nescience: it avoids dependence on an unknown ideal vector, preserves Pareto optimality for finite $p$, and differs from the ideal-vector criterion by at most constant-size coding overhead.

% Section: Weighted Sum Method

\subsection*{Weighted Sum Method}
\label{sec:nescience_weighted_sum}

The weighted-sum method (see Section \ref{sub:multiobjective_global_criterion}) is a standard approach to incorporating preferences into multi-objective optimization. It assigns a positive weight to each objective and minimizes their weighted sum. For the science problem, this gives
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad w_\mu\,\mu(e,r)+w_\nu\,\nu(r,d) \\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e,
\end{align*}
where
\[
w_\mu>0, \qquad w_\nu>0, \qquad w_\mu + w_\nu = 1.
\]
The weights determine the relative influence of miscoding and mismodel in the selection of a hypothesis. Equal weights treat both objectives symmetrically, while unequal weights express a preference for reducing one component over the other.

A desirable property of the weighted-sum method is that it
cannot select a dominated hypothesis as a global minimizer,
provided both weights are strictly positive.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity and let $w_\mu,w_\nu>0$. Then every global minimizer of the weighted-sum problem over $\mathcal H_e$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The scalarization function
\[
V(x,y)=w_\mu x+w_\nu y
\]
is strictly increasing in each coordinate whenever $w_\mu>0$ and $w_\nu>0$. The conclusion therefore follows from Proposition \ref{prop:strictly_increasing_dm_pareto}.
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes that the weighted-sum method preserves Pareto dominance. However, the resulting hypothesis need not be unique, since several Pareto-optimal hypotheses may attain the same minimum weighted sum.

The weights should not be interpreted merely as absolute measures of importance. More precisely, their ratio expresses the rate at which the decision maker is willing to exchange miscoding for mismodel. Consider a transition between two hypotheses that decreases miscoding but increases mismodel:
\[
\Delta_\mu<0, \qquad \Delta_\nu>0.
\]
The transition reduces the weighted sum precisely when
\[
w_\mu\Delta_\mu + w_\nu\Delta_\nu < 0,
\]
or equivalently,
\[
\frac{\Delta_\nu}{-\Delta_\mu} < \frac{w_\mu}{w_\nu}.
\]
Thus, the ratio $w_\mu/w_\nu$ determines the maximum increase in mismodel that the decision maker is willing to accept per unit decrease in miscoding. This provides a direct interpretation of the trade-off ratios introduced in Section \ref{sec:nescience_tradeoffs}.

An additional property of the weighted-sum method is that its solutions do not depend on whether the objectives are measured from the origin or from the ideal objective vector.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity with ideal objective vector
\[
\mathbf z_e^I = \bigl(z_{e,\mu}^I,z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr).
\]
For any fixed weights $w_\mu,w_\nu>0$, minimizing
\[
w_\mu\mu(e,r)+w_\nu\nu(r,d)
\]
over $\mathcal H_e$ is equivalent to minimizing
\[
w_\mu\bigl(\mu(e,r)-z_{e,\mu}^I\bigr) + w_\nu\bigl(\nu(r,d)-z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr).
\]
In particular, both formulations have exactly the same global minimizers.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding the second expression gives
\[
w_\mu\mu(e,r)+w_\nu\nu(r,d) - w_\mu z_{e,\mu}^I - w_\nu z_{e,\nu}^I.
\]
Since the final two terms are constant with respect to the hypothesis $(r,d)$, they do not affect the ordering of hypotheses or the set of minimizers.
\end{proof}

This property is particularly convenient in the theory of nescience, since the ideal objective vector is generally unknown. Unlike other global criteria, the weighted-sum method does not require approximating the ideal vector in order to preserve the selection of hypotheses.

\begin{example}
Consider a scientist comparing three Pareto-optimal hypotheses with objective vectors
\[
A=(1,7),\qquad B=(4,5),\qquad C=(7,1),
\]
where the first coordinate is miscoding and the second is mismodel. These values are illustrative. If the scientist prioritizes representational adequacy, the weights
\[
w_\mu=0.8,\qquad w_\nu=0.2
\]
give the weighted scores
\[
V(A)=2.2,\qquad V(B)=4.2,\qquad V(C)=5.8.
\]
The method therefore selects $A$, which has the smallest miscoding.

Conversely, with
\[
w_\mu=0.2,\qquad w_\nu=0.8,
\]
the scores become
\[
V(A)=5.8,\qquad V(B)=4.8,\qquad V(C)=2.2.
\]
The method now selects $C$, which has the smallest mismodel.

Despite being Pareto optimal, hypothesis $B$ cannot minimize the weighted sum for any strictly positive choice of weights. This illustrates a limitation of the method: not every Pareto-optimal hypothesis can necessarily be recovered by varying the weights.
\end{example}

The weighted-sum method can recover only supported Pareto-optimal objective vectors, that is, those lying on a supporting line of the attainable objective set. When this set is nonconvex, some Pareto-optimal hypotheses may remain inaccessible to the method, regardless of the chosen weights. Since the science problem is formulated over discrete representations and descriptions, its attainable objective set need not be convex. This limitation is therefore particularly relevant to the theory of nescience.

Finally, although miscoding and mismodel are both measured in bits, they may have substantially different numerical ranges. The interpretation of the weights therefore depends on the scales of the objectives. Using fixed positive scaling factors preserves Pareto dominance, but may require adjusting the weights to maintain the same preferences.

% Section: Weighted Tchebycheff Method

\subsection*{Weighted Tchebycheff Method}

The weighted Tchebycheff method is a particular case of the global criterion that minimizes the largest weighted deviation from the ideal objective vector. For the science problem, it takes the form
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \max\left\{w_\mu\bigl(\mu(e,r)-z_{e,\mu}^I\bigr),
w_\nu\bigl(\nu(r,d)-z_{e,\nu}^I\bigr) \right\} \\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e,
\end{align*}
where
\[
w_\mu>0,\qquad w_\nu>0,\qquad w_\mu+w_\nu=1.
\]
As discussed in the global criterion method, the ideal objective vector is generally unknown. We may therefore replace it with the origin, obtaining the
\emph{origin-based weighted Tchebycheff criterion}:
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \max\left\{w_\mu\mu(e,r),w_\nu\nu(r,d) \right\} \\
& \text{subject to} \quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e.
\end{align*}
Unlike the weighted-sum method, replacing the ideal vector with the origin may change the selected hypothesis. Nevertheless, the origin provides a convenient reference that does not require knowledge of the individual minima.

The weighted Tchebycheff method minimizes the largest weighted defect, preventing a very small value in one objective from compensating for a large value in the other. It is therefore particularly appropriate when the decision maker wishes to control both sources of ignorance rather than allowing substantial imbalances between them.

This criterion has a natural interpretation within the theory of nescience. Recall that miscoding and mismodel are themselves defined through the maximum of their respective deficiency and surplus components. Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\max\{w_\mu\mu(e,r),w_\nu\nu(r,d)\} \\
&\qquad = \max\{w_\mu d_R(e,r), w_\mu s_R(e,r), w_\nu d_D(r,d), w_\nu s_D(r,d)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, the weighted Tchebycheff criterion minimizes the largest of the four weighted epistemic defects. With equal weights, it is equivalent, up to a constant multiplicative factor, to minimizing the largest defect without weighting.

An important advantage over the weighted-sum method is that weighted Tchebycheff scalarization can recover Pareto-optimal hypotheses that do not lie on supported regions of the Pareto frontier.

\begin{example}
Consider three Pareto-optimal objective vectors:
\[
A=(1,7),\qquad B=(4,5),\qquad C=(7,1).
\]
Using equal weights,
\[
w_\mu=w_\nu=\frac12,
\]
the weighted Tchebycheff scores are
\[
V(A)=3.5,\qquad V(B)=2.5,\qquad V(C)=3.5.
\]
The method therefore selects $B$, which cannot be selected by any strictly positive weighted sum. This illustrates the ability of weighted Tchebycheff scalarization to recover unsupported Pareto-optimal solutions.
\end{example}

However, the weighted Tchebycheff function is nondecreasing, but not strictly increasing, in each coordinate. Consequently, a dominated hypothesis may attain the same scalar value as a Pareto-optimal hypothesis. This limitation can be addressed through a secondary selection criterion.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:weighted_tchebycheff_pareto}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let $w_\mu,w_\nu>0$. Among the global minimizers of
\[
V_\infty(e,r,d) = \max\{w_\mu\mu(e,r),w_\nu\nu(r,d)\}
\]
over $\mathcal H_e$, select a hypothesis minimizing
\[
w_\mu\mu(e,r)+w_\nu\nu(r,d).
\]
Then the selected hypothesis is Pareto optimal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(r^\star,d^\star)$ be a hypothesis selected according to the stated criterion. Suppose, for
contradiction, that another hypothesis $(r',d')\in\mathcal H_e$ dominates it. Since both weights are positive, dominance implies
\[
V_\infty(e,r',d') \leq V_\infty(e,r^\star,d^\star).
\]
By the global optimality of $(r^\star,d^\star)$, both hypotheses must attain the same minimum Tchebycheff value. However, because $(r',d')$ dominates $(r^\star, d^\star)$ and both weights are positive,
\[
w_\mu\mu(e,r')+w_\nu\nu(r',d') < w_\mu\mu(e,r^\star)+w_\nu\nu(r^\star,d^\star).
\]
This contradicts the secondary minimization criterion. Therefore $(r^\star,d^\star)$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proof}

The weighted Tchebycheff method provides an alternative to the weighted sum by emphasizing the largest source of epistemic error. Its consistency with the maximum-based definitions of miscoding and mismodel makes it particularly natural within the theory of nescience. Moreover, it can recover Pareto-optimal hypotheses that weighted sums may miss. Nevertheless, the relative weights remain a choice of the decision maker, rather than an intrinsic property of nescience itself.

% Subsection: $\varepsilon$-Constraint Method

\subsection*{$\varepsilon$-Constraint Method}

The $\varepsilon$-constraint method (see Section \ref{sub:multiobjective_global_criterion}) selects a hypothesis by minimizing one objective while imposing an upper bound on the other. Unlike the weighted-sum and global-criterion methods, it does not combine miscoding and mismodel into a single numerical measure. Instead, it expresses a preference by restricting the feasible hypothesis space.

For the science problem, one possible formulation is
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize} \quad \nu(r,d) \\
& \text{subject to} \quad \mu(e,r)\leq\varepsilon,\\
& \phantom{\text{subject to} \quad} (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e,
\end{align*}
where $\varepsilon\geq0$ is the maximum acceptable miscoding. The reverse formulation, minimizing miscoding subject to an upper bound on mismodel, is equally valid.

This method is particularly appropriate when the investigation imposes explicit requirements on representational or descriptive quality. For example, a scientist may seek the best possible description among representations whose miscoding does not exceed a prescribed tolerance. Such a requirement may be more meaningful than assigning arbitrary weights to both objectives.

An important advantage of the $\varepsilon$-constraint method is that it can recover Pareto-optimal solutions that the weighted-sum method cannot, including those lying on nonconvex regions of the Pareto frontier. In fact, every Pareto-optimal objective vector can be recovered by choosing a suitable value of $\varepsilon$.

However, a global minimizer of the constrained problem is not necessarily Pareto optimal, since several hypotheses may attain the same minimum mismodel while having different miscoding. The following proposition establishes how this ambiguity can be resolved.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and suppose the $\varepsilon$-constraint problem has a nonempty feasible set. Among the hypotheses minimizing mismodel subject to $\mu(e,r)\leq\varepsilon$, select one with minimum miscoding. Then the selected hypothesis is Pareto optimal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(r^\star,d^\star)$ be a hypothesis selected according to the stated criterion. Suppose, for contradiction, that another hypothesis $(r',d')\in\mathcal H_e$ dominates it. Then
\[
\mu(e,r')\leq\mu(e,r^\star)\leq\varepsilon,
\]
and
\[
\nu(r',d')\leq\nu(r^\star,d^\star),
\]
with at least one strict inequality.

If the inequality in mismodel is strict, this contradicts the minimality of $\nu(r^\star,d^\star)$ under the constraint. Otherwise, the inequality in miscoding must be strict, contradicting the selection of minimum miscoding among hypotheses attaining the minimum mismodel.

Therefore $(r^\star,d^\star)$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proof}

The main limitation of the method is the choice of $\varepsilon$, which must reflect a meaningful scientific requirement. An excessively restrictive threshold may leave the problem infeasible, while a permissive threshold may provide little control over miscoding.

The $\varepsilon$-constraint method therefore offers a complementary approach to hypothesis selection. Its principal value lies in enforcing explicit requirements and recovering Pareto-optimal trade-offs that may remain inaccessible to weighted-sum scalarization. However, since it restricts the feasible space rather than assigning a finite scalar value to every hypothesis, it is better understood as a constrained decision method than as an independent definition of scalar nescience.

\section{Effective Nescience}
\label{sec:effective_nescience}

The theoretical characterization of nescience is based on the pair $(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d))$, which measures representational and descriptive defects, respectively. However, these quantities are generally inaccessible in scientific practice, since they depend on admissible representations and minimal descriptions that cannot usually be determined. Moreover, scientific inquiry proceeds through known hypotheses, which need not satisfy the theoretical conditions defining $\mathcal H_e$. As discussed in the chapter on Descriptions, a known hypothesis may be based on an incorrect representation or an inadequate description, and may even have been rejected by the scientific community.

To evaluate such hypotheses, we rely on the effective measures of miscoding and mismodel introduced in the preceding chapters. These measures provide non-negative estimates of representational and descriptive defects, without requiring the corresponding hypotheses to satisfy their ideal theoretical conditions. We can therefore introduce effective nescience as the counterpart of theoretical nescience for known hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let $(r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ be a known hypothesis. We define the \emph{effective nescience} \index{Effective nescience} of $(r,d)$ with respect to $e$ as
\[
\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r,d) = \bigl(\widehat\mu(e,r), \widehat\nu(r,d)\bigr).
\]
Given a scalarization function $V:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2\to\mathbb R$, we define the corresponding \emph{scalar effective nescience} \index{Scalar effective nescience} as
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r,d) = V\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r,d)\bigr) = V\bigl(\widehat\mu(e,r),\widehat\nu(r,d)\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

Effective nescience preserves the distinction between the two fundamental sources of epistemic error. Effective miscoding evaluates the adequacy of the representation with respect to the entity, whereas effective mismodel evaluates the adequacy and economy of the description with respect to the representation.

As in the theoretical formulation, nescience is fundamentally bi-objective. Scalar effective nescience provides a decision criterion when hypotheses must be ranked or a single hypothesis must be selected. Different scalarization functions may assign different rankings to the same collection of hypotheses.

An important property of effective nescience is that componentwise improvement of its two objectives is preserved by increasing scalarization functions.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $(r_1,d_1),(r_2,d_2)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ be two known hypotheses. Suppose that $\widehat\mu(e,r_2)\leq\widehat\mu(e,r_1)$, and $\widehat\nu(r_2,d_2)\leq\widehat\nu(r_1,d_1)$. If $V$ is nondecreasing in each coordinate, then
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r_2,d_2) \leq \widehat N_V(e,r_1,d_1).
\]
Moreover, if $V$ is strictly increasing in each coordinate and at least one of the preceding inequalities is strict, then
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r_2,d_2) < \widehat N_V(e,r_1,d_1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By assumption,
\[
\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r_2,d_2) \leq \widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r_1,d_1)
\]
componentwise. Since $V$ is nondecreasing in each coordinate,
\[
V\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r_2,d_2)\bigr) \leq V\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r_1,d_1)\bigr).
\]
If $V$ is strictly increasing and at least one component decreases strictly, the inequality is strict.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that scalar effective nescience respects componentwise improvements under an increasing scalarization. A hypothesis that reduces both effective defects, or reduces one without increasing the other, cannot be considered worse according to such a criterion.

The converse does not generally hold. A reduction in scalar effective nescience may result from a sufficiently large improvement in one component despite deterioration in the other. Consequently, scalar improvement need not imply componentwise improvement.

A particularly useful case arises when two hypotheses share the same representation. Since effective miscoding depends only on the entity and its representation, changing the description cannot affect that component. The following proposition makes this observation precise.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $(r,d_1),(r,d_2)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ be two known hypotheses sharing the same representation. If $V$ is strictly increasing in its second coordinate, then
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r,d_1) < \widehat N_V(e,r,d_2)
\]
if and only if
\[
\widehat\nu(r,d_1) < \widehat\nu(r,d_2).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since both hypotheses share the representation $r$, their effective miscoding is identical: $\widehat\mu(e,r) = \widehat\mu(e,r)$. Therefore,
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r,d_i) = V\bigl(\widehat\mu(e,r),\widehat\nu(r,d_i)\bigr), \qquad i=1,2.
\]
Since $V$ is strictly increasing in its second coordinate, the ordering of these scalar values coincides with the ordering of their effective mismodel values.
\end{proof}

This result shows that, when the representation is fixed, comparing hypotheses through scalar effective nescience is equivalent to comparing their effective mismodel. However, the result does not imply that optimizing descriptions independently is sufficient to solve the Science Problem. A different representation may lead to a hypothesis with substantially lower effective nescience.

\subsection*{Relationship with Theoretical Nescience}

Effective nescience is intended to approximate theoretical nescience, but the relationship between them requires careful interpretation. The effectiveness of miscoding and mismodel does not imply that they are exact. In general, we cannot assume that $\widehat\mu(e,r)=\mu(e,r)$ or $\widehat\nu(r,d)=\nu(r,d)$, even when the corresponding theoretical quantities are defined. The effective measures may depend on available observations, computational approximations, reference information, and assumptions about the entity being investigated. Consequently, low effective nescience does not necessarily establish low theoretical nescience.

This distinction is particularly relevant when a hypothesis is associated with an incorrect entity. A description may provide an accurate and concise account of the available representation, while the representation itself fails to capture the intended entity. If effective miscoding does not detect this failure, the hypothesis may receive an artificially low effective nescience.

Nevertheless, when the approximation errors of the effective measures can be bounded, it is possible to establish quantitative guarantees about their relationship with theoretical nescience. The following proposition provides such a guarantee for scalar effective nescience.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:effective_nescience_error_bound},
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $(r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e\cap\mathcal H_e$ be a known hypothesis that also satisfies the theoretical conditions of the Science Problem. Suppose that
\[
\left|\widehat\mu(e,r)-\mu(e,r)\right| \leq\varepsilon_\mu,
\]
and
\[
\left|\widehat\nu(r,d)-\nu(r,d)\right| \leq\varepsilon_\nu,
\]
for some $\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\geq0$. Let $V:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2\to\mathbb R$ be $L$-Lipschitz continuous with respect to the maximum norm, that is,
\[
|V(\mathbf x)-V(\mathbf y)| \leq L\|\mathbf x-\mathbf y\|_\infty
\]
for all $\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2$. Then
\[
\left|\widehat N_V(e,r,d)-N_V(e,r,d)\right| \leq L\max\{\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the assumptions on the approximation errors,
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\left\|\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r,d)-\mathbf N(e,r,d)\right\|_\infty\\
&\qquad=\max\left\{|\widehat\mu(e,r)-\mu(e,r)|,\,|\widehat\nu(r,d)-\nu(r,d)|\right\}\\
&\qquad\leq\max\{\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Applying the Lipschitz property of $V$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\left|\widehat N_V(e,r,d)-N_V(e,r,d)\right|\\
&\qquad=\left|V\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,r,d)\bigr)-V\bigl(\mathbf N(e,r,d)\bigr)\right|\\
&\qquad\leq L\max\{\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that bounded approximation errors in effective miscoding and mismodel lead to bounded errors in scalar effective nescience, provided the scalarization satisfies the stated regularity condition. In particular, if both effective measures are exact, then effective nescience coincides with theoretical nescience for every hypothesis belonging to both domains.

A further consequence concerns the reliability of comparisons between hypotheses.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:effective_nescience_ranking}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $h_1,h_2\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e\cap\mathcal H_e$ be two hypotheses. Suppose that the assumptions of Proposition \ref{prop:effective_nescience_error_bound} hold for both hypotheses, with common error bounds $\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu$, and let $\varepsilon = L\max\{\varepsilon_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\}$. If
\[
\widehat N_V(e,h_1)+2\varepsilon < \widehat N_V(e,h_2),
\]
where the notation $\widehat N_V(e,h_i)$ denotes the scalar effective nescience of hypothesis $h_i$, then
\[
N_V(e,h_1)<N_V(e,h_2).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition
\ref{prop:effective_nescience_error_bound},
\[
N_V(e,h_1)
\leq
\widehat N_V(e,h_1)+\varepsilon,
\]
and
\[
N_V(e,h_2)
\geq
\widehat N_V(e,h_2)-\varepsilon.
\]

By assumption,
\[
\widehat N_V(e,h_1)+\varepsilon
<
\widehat N_V(e,h_2)-\varepsilon.
\]

Combining these inequalities yields
\[
N_V(e,h_1)<N_V(e,h_2).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that sufficiently large differences in scalar effective nescience guarantee the same ordering under theoretical scalar nescience, provided the approximation errors are bounded. Conversely, when the difference between the effective scores is small relative to the possible approximation errors, their theoretical ranking cannot generally be established from those scores alone.

The error bounds required by these propositions are additional assumptions; they do not follow merely from the definitions of effective miscoding and mismodel. Furthermore, these results apply only to hypotheses for which theoretical nescience is defined. Known hypotheses that do not belong to $\mathcal H_e$ cannot be compared with theoretical nescience in this manner.

Thus, effective nescience characterizes our available assessment of a hypothesis, rather than providing an infallible measure of the knowledge it contains. Its principal value is that it extends the bi-objective framework of nescience to the hypotheses considered in scientific practice, while preserving a clear distinction between theoretical epistemic defects and their effective approximations.

\subsection*{Effective Pareto Optimality}

As in the theoretical Science Problem, effective nescience consists of two potentially conflicting objectives. A known hypothesis may exhibit lower effective miscoding but higher effective mismodel than another, preventing their direct comparison without introducing additional preferences. The natural comparison criterion is therefore Pareto dominance, applied to the effective nescience vectors of known hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $h_1,h_2\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ be two known hypotheses. We say that $h_1$ \emph{effectively dominates} \index{Effective dominance} $h_2$ if
\[
\widehat{\mathbf N}(e,h_1) \leq \widehat{\mathbf N}(e,h_2)
\]
componentwise, with at least one strict inequality.
\end{definition}

Effective dominance identifies hypotheses that are preferable
according to both effective measures, without requiring
a scalarization function. A hypothesis is effectively Pareto
optimal when no other known hypothesis dominates it.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity. We define the \emph{effective Pareto set} \index{Effective Pareto set} as
\[
\widehat{\mathcal P}_e = \left\{ h\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e: \nexists h'\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e 
    \text{ that effectively dominates }h \right\}.
\]
The corresponding \emph{effective Pareto frontier} \index{Effective Pareto frontier} is
\[
\widehat{\mathcal F}_e = \left\{ \widehat{\mathbf N}(e,h): h\in\widehat{\mathcal P}_e \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The effective Pareto set contains the known hypotheses for which no simultaneous improvement in effective miscoding and mismodel is available. The effective Pareto frontier contains their corresponding objective vectors. Different hypotheses may belong to the same point of the frontier, since distinct representations and descriptions may have identical effective nescience values.

The following proposition establishes the existence of effectively Pareto-optimal hypotheses in finite collections.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:effective_pareto_existence}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity. If $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ is finite and nonempty, then
\[
\widehat{\mathcal P}_e\neq\varnothing.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ is finite and nonempty, there exists a hypothesis $h^\star$ minimizing
\[
\widehat\mu(e,r)+\widehat\nu(r,d)
\]
over all $(r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$. Suppose that another known hypothesis effectively dominates $h^\star$. Then both effective components are no greater, and at least one is strictly smaller. Consequently, their sum is strictly smaller than that of $h^\star$, contradicting its minimality. Therefore $h^\star$ is effectively Pareto optimal, and $\widehat{\mathcal P}_e$ is nonempty.
\end{proof}

Effective Pareto optimality must be distinguished from theoretical Pareto optimality. The effective measures may fail to preserve the dominance relations established by theoretical miscoding and mismodel. Moreover,
$\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ may contain hypotheses that do not belong to the theoretical space $\mathcal H_e$. Consequently, membership in $\widehat{\mathcal P}_e$ does not imply membership in $\mathcal P_e$, even when
the known hypothesis satisfies the theoretical conditions. The effective Pareto set therefore characterizes the best available trade-offs according to our current assessment of known hypotheses, rather than the globally optimal solutions of the theoretical Science Problem.

\section{Decreasing Nescience}
\label{sec:decreasing_nescience}

The theory of nescience characterizes ignorance through two complementary quantities: miscoding $\mu(e,r)$ and mismodel $\nu(r,d)$. Their simultaneous minimization defines the Science Problem, while Pareto optimality establishes which hypotheses cannot be improved without worsening at least one objective. However, these two quantities do not reveal the specific sources of epistemic error. Since both are defined as maxima of more primitive defects, a high miscoding may arise from representational deficiency, surplus, or both. Similarly, a high mismodel may result from descriptive deficiency, surplus, or both. Understanding these components is therefore essential for determining how a hypothesis might be improved.

To obtain a more detailed characterization, we consider the four primitive defects:
\[
d_R(e,r),\quad s_R(e,r),\quad d_D(r,d),\quad s_D(r,d).
\]
Each suggests a corresponding corrective operation: augmentation and reduction for representations, and enrichment and simplification for descriptions. This leads to an auxiliary four-objective optimization problem:
\begin{align*}
& \text{minimize}\quad \bigl(d_R(e,r),\, s_R(e,r),\, d_D(r,d),\, s_D(r,d) \bigr)\\
& \text{subject to}\quad (r,d)\in\mathcal H_e.
\end{align*}

This formulation does not replace the bi-objective Science Problem. Rather, it provides a finer description of the defects underlying miscoding and mismodel, allowing us to analyze their individual variations and interactions.

We begin by collecting the four fundamental defects into a single vector. This representation makes it possible to study how changes to a hypothesis affect each source of epistemic error independently.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity and $\mathbf{x}=(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$ a hypothesis. We define its \emph{primitive nescience vector} \index{Primitive nescience vector} as
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl(d_R(e,r),\, s_R(e,r),\, d_D(r,d),\, s_D(r,d) \bigr)
\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^4.
\]
\end{definition}

The first two components characterize the representational defects, while the remaining two characterize the descriptive defects. The canonical bi-objective characterization of nescience is recovered by taking the maximum within each pair:
\[
\mu(e,r)=\max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\},
\]
\[
\nu(r,d)=\max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\}.
\]
An important question is whether improvements in the primitive defects necessarily lead to improvements in miscoding and mismodel. The following proposition establishes the relationship between these two levels.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $\mathbf{x}=(r,d)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(r',d')$ be two hypotheses in $\mathcal H_e$. If $\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y}) \leq \mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})$ componentwise, then
\[
\mu(e,r')\leq\mu(e,r), \qquad \nu(r',d')\leq\nu(r,d).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By assumption $d_R(e,r') \leq d_R(e,r)$ and $s_R(e,r')\leq s_R(e,r)$. Since the maximum function is nondecreasing in each argument,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(e,r')
&=\max\{d_R(e,r'),s_R(e,r')\}\\
&\leq\max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\}\\
&=\mu(e,r).
\end{aligned}
\]
Similarly, from $d_D(r',d')\leq d_D(r,d)$ and $s_D(r',d')\leq s_D(r,d)$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu(r',d')
&=\max\{d_D(r',d'),s_D(r',d')\}\\
&\leq\max\{d_D(r,d),s_D(r,d)\}\\
&=\nu(r,d).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that componentwise non-increase of the primitive defects guarantees that neither miscoding nor mismodel deteriorates. However, a strict decrease in one primitive defect does not necessarily produce a strict decrease in either aggregate objective.

\begin{example}
Consider two illustrative primitive vectors:
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})=(5,2,4,1), \qquad \mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})=(5,1,4,1).
\]
Although representational surplus decreases, both
hypotheses have the same miscoding and mismodel:
\[
\bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d)\bigr)=(5,4).
\]
\end{example}

Conversely, a hypothesis may reduce both miscoding and mismodel even when some primitive defects increase. Thus, improvement in the four-dimensional formulation is a sufficient condition for non-deterioration of the two aggregate objectives, but it is not a necessary condition for their strict improvement.

To compare two hypotheses, it is useful to measure the changes in their primitive defects. These changes identify which sources of epistemic error improve, which deteriorate, and which remain unchanged.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $\mathbf{x}=(r,d)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(r',d')$ be two hypotheses in $\mathcal H_e$. We define the \emph{change vector}\index{Change vector} from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ as
\[
\Delta_e(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})-\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x}) =
\bigl(\Delta d_R,\, \Delta s_R,\, \Delta d_D,\, \Delta s_D \bigr),
\]
where
\begin{align*}
\Delta d_R
&=d_R(e,r')-d_R(e,r),\\
\Delta s_R
&=s_R(e,r')-s_R(e,r),\\
\Delta d_D
&=d_D(r',d')-d_D(r,d),\\
\Delta s_D
&=s_D(r',d')-s_D(r,d).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

Negative components of the change vector indicate improvement, positive components indicate deterioration, and zero components indicate no change.

Since the primitive defects may have different magnitudes, relative changes can sometimes provide a more informative comparison than absolute differences. This motivates the following normalized version.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal H_e$. Write
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x}) = (n_1(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,n_4(\mathbf{x})).
\]
We define the \emph{normalized variation vector} \index{Normalized variation vector} as
\[
\widehat{\Delta}_e(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) =
\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_1,\ldots, \widehat{\Delta}_4 \bigr),
\]
where, for each $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$,
\[
\widehat{\Delta}_i =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{n_i(\mathbf{y})-n_i(\mathbf{x})}
      {n_i(\mathbf{x})},
&\text{if }n_i(\mathbf{x})>0,\\[1.2ex] 0,
&\text{if }n_i(\mathbf{x})=n_i(\mathbf{y})=0,\\[0.8ex] +\infty,
&\text{if }n_i(\mathbf{x})=0<n_i(\mathbf{y}).
\end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

For example, a normalized variation of $-0.2$ indicates a reduction of $20\%$ in the corresponding defect, whereas a value of $0.1$ indicates an increase of $10\%$.

Normalization preserves the direction of change in each primitive defect and therefore preserves componentwise dominance. However, it changes the relative magnitudes assigned to different improvements. In particular, normalized variations must be interpreted carefully when the original defect is small.

Not every change in a primitive defect affects miscoding or mismodel. Since both quantities are defined through maxima, their current values are determined by the largest defects in each pair. We call these components \emph{active defects}. Identifying them allows us to distinguish changes that directly affect the aggregate objectives from changes that leave their values unchanged.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $\mathbf{x}=(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e$. Write
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x}) = (n_1(\mathbf{x}),n_2(\mathbf{x}),
 n_3(\mathbf{x}),n_4(\mathbf{x})),
\]
where the coordinates correspond, respectively, to representational deficiency, representational surplus, descriptive deficiency, and descriptive surplus. We define the \emph{active defect set} \index{Active defect set} of $\mathbf{x}$ as
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_e(\mathbf{x})={}
& \{i\in\{1,2\}:n_i(\mathbf{x})=\mu(e,r)\}\\
&\cup \{i\in\{3,4\}:n_i(\mathbf{x})=\nu(r,d)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{definition}

The active defect set identifies the primitive components attaining the maxima that define miscoding and mismodel. When deficiency and surplus are equal, both components are active. Thus, each hypothesis has at least one active representational defect and at least one active descriptive defect.

The following proposition establishes precisely when changes in the primitive defects produce a strict reduction in miscoding or mismodel.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $\mathbf{x}=(r,d)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(r',d')$ be two hypotheses in $\mathcal H_e$. Then
\[
\mu(e,r')<\mu(e,r)
\]
if and only if
\[
d_R(e,r')<\mu(e,r) \qquad\text{and}\qquad s_R(e,r')<\mu(e,r).
\]
Similarly,
\[
\nu(r',d')<\nu(r,d)
\]
if and only if
\[
d_D(r',d')<\nu(r,d) \qquad\text{and}\qquad s_D(r',d')<\nu(r,d).
\]
In particular, if $\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})$ componentwise, then either aggregate objective decreases strictly if and only if every initially active defect belonging to its corresponding pair decreases strictly.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\mu(e,r') = \max\{d_R(e,r'),s_R(e,r')\}.
\]
Therefore, $\mu(e,r')<\mu(e,r)$ holds if and only if both arguments of the maximum are strictly smaller than $\mu(e,r)$.

The same argument applies to mismodel:
\[
\nu(r',d') = \max\{d_D(r',d'),s_D(r',d')\}.
\]
Now suppose that $\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})\leq\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})$ componentwise. Every initially inactive defect is strictly smaller than its original aggregate maximum. Since it cannot increase, it remains below that maximum in $\mathbf{y}$. Consequently, strict reduction of an aggregate requires and is guaranteed by strict reduction of all defects that originally attained its maximum.
\end{proof}

This proposition clarifies the role of active defects. Reducing an inactive defect while leaving all active
defects unchanged cannot reduce the corresponding aggregate objective, provided no other defect increases above its current maximum. Conversely, reducing only one active defect may also be insufficient when several defects share the maximum. In that case, all active defects must decrease before the aggregate objective can decrease.

An important consequence is that improvements in miscoding and mismodel need not require every primitive defect to decrease. A reduction in an active defect may compensate for an increase in an inactive defect, provided the latter remains below the previous maximum. The active defect set therefore provides a more precise account of which changes affect the current values of miscoding and mismodel. Together with the primitive nescience vector and its variations, it establishes the mathematical foundation for analyzing corrective operations on hypotheses.

These results do not prescribe a particular strategy for scientific inquiry. Rather, they characterize the relationships between the four primitive defects and the two fundamental objectives of the Science Problem, providing the basis for the subsequent study of methods for decreasing nescience.

% Section: Corrective Operations

\subsection*{Corrective Operations}
\label{sec:corrective_operations}

The four primitive defects suggest four complementary ways of
modifying a hypothesis. Representational deficiency may be
addressed through augmentation, by incorporating missing
information into the representation, while representational
surplus may be addressed through reduction, by removing
irrelevant information. Similarly, descriptive deficiency
may be addressed through enrichment, by introducing additional
explanatory structure, while descriptive surplus may be
addressed through simplification, by eliminating unnecessary
elements of a description.

These operations provide a connection between the diagnosis
of epistemic errors and the modification of hypotheses.
However, their effects are not necessarily independent.
Changing a representation may also affect the adequacy of
its description, while modifying a description may improve
one descriptive defect at the expense of another. Consequently,
the intended purpose of an operation does not guarantee
that it will reduce the corresponding defect or nescience
as a whole.

The four primitive defects are therefore useful for guiding
the construction of alternative hypotheses, but the resulting
candidates must still be evaluated according to their
miscoding and mismodel.

\paragraph{Descent Candidates.}

We begin by distinguishing improvements in the four
primitive defects from improvements in the two fundamental
objectives of the Science Problem.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let
$\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal H_e$ be two hypotheses.

We say that $\mathbf{y}$ is a
\emph{four-dimensional descent candidate}
\index{Four-dimensional descent candidate}
for $\mathbf{x}$ if
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})
\leq
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})
\]
componentwise, with at least one strict inequality.
\end{definition}

A four-dimensional descent candidate decreases at least
one primitive defect without increasing any of the others.
Equivalently, it dominates the current hypothesis in the
four-dimensional space of primitive defects.

By Proposition \ref{prop:4d_implies_2d_improvement},
such a candidate cannot increase miscoding or mismodel.
However, it need not strictly decrease either aggregate
objective, since the improved primitive defect may not
determine the current maximum.

Conversely, strict improvement in miscoding or mismodel
does not require all primitive defects to decrease.
For example, consider two hypotheses with primitive
nescience vectors
\[
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x})=(10,2,8,1),
\qquad
\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{y})=(9,3,7,2).
\]
Although both surplus components increase, the corresponding
objective vectors are
\[
(10,8)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(9,7),
\]
respectively. Thus, $\mathbf{y}$ dominates $\mathbf{x}$
in the bi-objective Science Problem, even though it is not
a four-dimensional descent candidate.

For this reason, four-dimensional descent provides a
sufficient but unnecessarily restrictive criterion for
reducing nescience. The following definition captures
the less restrictive condition corresponding directly
to the Science Problem.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let
$\mathbf{x}=(r,d)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(r',d')$
be two hypotheses in $\mathcal H_e$.

We say that $\mathbf{y}$ is a
\emph{nescience descent candidate}
\index{Nescience descent candidate}
for $\mathbf{x}$ if
\[
\mu(e,r')\leq\mu(e,r),
\qquad
\nu(r',d')\leq\nu(r,d),
\]
with at least one strict inequality.
\end{definition}

A nescience descent candidate is therefore a hypothesis
that dominates the current one. Unlike four-dimensional
descent, this condition permits increases in individual
primitive defects, provided that neither miscoding nor
mismodel increases.

Nescience descent is a conservative criterion for scientific
improvement. It guarantees that every accepted transition
reduces at least one source of epistemic error without
worsening the other. Nevertheless, as discussed in the
preceding section on trade-offs, some paths toward better
hypotheses may require temporary deterioration in one
objective. Such transitions are not included in the
descent criterion introduced here.

\paragraph{Active Normalized Descent Score.}

When several descent candidates are available, their
relative improvements can be compared using the active
defects of the current hypothesis.

Recall that the active defect set $A_e(\mathbf{x})$
contains the indices of the primitive defects that
determine the current values of miscoding and mismodel.
We use their normalized variations to assign a score
to each descent candidate.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let
$\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal H_e$,
where $\mathbf{y}$ is a nescience descent candidate
for $\mathbf{x}$.

We define the \emph{active normalized descent score}
\index{Active normalized descent score} as
\[
\Sigma_e(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
=
-\sum_{i\in A_e(\mathbf{x})}
\widehat{\Delta}_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).
\]
\end{definition}

The score measures the total relative reduction in
the primitive defects that currently determine
miscoding and mismodel. Larger values correspond
to greater aggregate relative improvements in these
active components.

An important property is that every nescience descent
candidate receives a strictly positive score.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:score_positive}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let
$\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal H_e$ be a nescience descent
candidate for $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal H_e$.
Then
\[
\Sigma_e(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})>0.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mathbf{y}$ dominates $\mathbf{x}$,
neither miscoding nor mismodel increases.

Consider any active representational defect of
$\mathbf{x}$. Its value equals $\mu(e,r)$.
Since the new miscoding does not exceed this value,
the corresponding primitive defect cannot increase.
The same argument applies to every active
descriptive defect.

Consequently, all normalized variations appearing
in the definition of $\Sigma_e$ are non-positive.

Moreover, at least one of the two aggregate objectives
decreases strictly. By Proposition
\ref{prop:active_defects_strict_improvement},
all initially active defects belonging to that
objective must decrease strictly.

Therefore at least one normalized variation in
the sum is strictly negative, while none is positive.
Hence
\[
\Sigma_e(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})>0.
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that the active normalized
descent score is consistent with the bi-objective
definition of improvement. Every accepted descent
candidate has a positive score, although different
scores may rank the possible improvements differently.

The score is a criterion for selecting among descent
candidates, rather than a fundamental measure of
nescience. In particular, its dependence on normalized
primitive defects introduces a preference between
different forms of improvement.

\paragraph{Operation-Generated Candidates.}

The corrective operations provide alternative ways
to construct candidate hypotheses. Augmentation and
reduction modify the representation, potentially
requiring a corresponding modification of the
description. Enrichment and simplification modify
the description while retaining the same representation.

In every case, a generated candidate must remain a
valid hypothesis about the entity under investigation.
We collect the candidates generated by the four
operations into a common set.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let
$\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal H_e$ be a hypothesis.

Denote by
\[
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{aug}}(\mathbf{x}),
\quad
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{red}}(\mathbf{x}),
\quad
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{enr}}(\mathbf{x}),
\quad
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{sim}}(\mathbf{x})
\]
the subsets of $\mathcal H_e$ generated by
augmentation, reduction, enrichment, and
simplification, respectively.

We define the \emph{operation-generated candidate set}
\index{Operation-generated candidate set} as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal C_e(\mathbf{x})
={}&
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{aug}}(\mathbf{x})
\cup
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{red}}(\mathbf{x})\\
&\cup
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{enr}}(\mathbf{x})
\cup
\mathcal C_e^{\mathrm{sim}}(\mathbf{x}).
\end{aligned}
\]

The corresponding \emph{descent set}
\index{Descent set} is
\[
\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal C_e(\mathbf{x}):
\mathbf{y}\text{ is a nescience descent candidate
for }\mathbf{x}
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The descent set contains precisely those generated
hypotheses that dominate the current hypothesis in
the original Science Problem. It may include candidates
that improve all four primitive defects, as well as
candidates that improve miscoding or mismodel while
increasing some inactive defects.

This distinction is important because the purpose
of the corrective operations is to reduce nescience,
not necessarily to decrease every primitive defect
independently.

\paragraph{Defect-Guided Descent Algorithm.}

Using the operation-generated candidate sets, we can
formulate an iterative procedure for reducing nescience.
At each iteration, the procedure identifies the active
defects, generates alternative hypotheses, and selects
a dominating candidate according to the active
normalized descent score.

For the selection step, we assume that the candidate
generation procedures return finite sets. This
restriction guarantees that a maximizing candidate
exists whenever the descent set is nonempty.

\begin{tBox}
\textbf{Defect-Guided Descent Algorithm}
\index{Defect-guided descent algorithm}

Given an entity $e\in\mathcal E$ and an initial
hypothesis $\mathbf{x}_0=(r_0,d_0)\in\mathcal H_e$,
define recursively:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the primitive nescience vector
$\mathbf n_e(\mathbf{x}_t)$ and the active defect set
$A_e(\mathbf{x}_t)$.

\item Generate the candidate set
$\mathcal C_e(\mathbf{x}_t)$ by applying augmentation,
reduction, enrichment, and simplification to
$\mathbf{x}_t$.

\item Form the descent set
$\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x}_t)$.

\item If
$\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x}_t)=\varnothing$,
stop.

\item Otherwise, choose
\[
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
\in
\arg\max_{
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x}_t)
}
\Sigma_e(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{y}).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{tBox}

The algorithm is defect-guided because its selection
criterion depends on the defects currently determining
miscoding and mismodel. However, candidates are accepted
according to bi-objective dominance, ensuring consistency
with the original Science Problem.

An important consequence is that every accepted
transition produces a strict improvement in at least
one of the two objectives.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:defect_guided_monotone}
Let $(\mathbf{x}_t)_{t\geq0}$ be a sequence
produced by the defect-guided descent algorithm,
where $\mathbf{x}_t=(r_t,d_t)$.

For every iteration at which $\mathbf{x}_{t+1}$
is defined,
\[
\mu(e,r_{t+1})\leq\mu(e,r_t),
\]
and
\[
\nu(r_{t+1},d_{t+1})
\leq
\nu(r_t,d_t),
\]
with at least one strict inequality.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By construction,
\[
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
\in
\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x}_t).
\]

Therefore $\mathbf{x}_{t+1}$ is a nescience
descent candidate for $\mathbf{x}_t$.
The stated inequalities follow directly
from the definition of nescience descent.
\end{proof}

Thus, the algorithm generates a sequence in which
miscoding and mismodel never increase, and at least
one decreases strictly at every accepted step.

Since both quantities are non-negative integer-valued,
this also establishes finite termination.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:defect_guided_finite_termination}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and suppose that every
iteration of the defect-guided descent algorithm
can be completed.

Then the algorithm terminates after finitely
many iterations.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathbf{x}_t=(r_t,d_t)$ and define
\[
Q_t
=
\mu(e,r_t)+\nu(r_t,d_t).
\]

Since miscoding and mismodel are non-negative
integer-valued quantities,
\[
Q_t\in\mathbb N_0.
\]

By Proposition \ref{prop:defect_guided_monotone},
every accepted transition decreases at least
one objective strictly without increasing the other.
Therefore,
\[
Q_{t+1}<Q_t.
\]

Consequently, $(Q_t)$ is a strictly decreasing
sequence of non-negative integers, which must
be finite.

Hence the algorithm terminates after finitely
many accepted transitions.
\end{proof}

The proposition guarantees finite termination in
the theoretical setting. It does not imply that
the required computations are effective, since
the primitive defects depend on Kolmogorov
complexity and on generally inaccessible
admissible representations and minimal descriptions.

Moreover, finite termination does not guarantee
global or local Pareto optimality. The algorithm
may stop because its corrective operations fail
to generate a suitable dominating candidate,
even when better hypotheses exist elsewhere.

\paragraph{Operation-Stationarity.}

The defect-guided descent algorithm terminates when
none of the available corrective operations generates
a hypothesis that strictly dominates the current one.
We call this condition operation-stationarity,
distinguishing it from Pareto optimality.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ and
$\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal H_e$.

We say that $\mathbf{x}$ is
\emph{operation-stationary}
\index{Operation-stationary hypothesis}
if
\[
\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x})
=
\varnothing.
\]
\end{definition}

Operation-stationarity provides the termination
condition for the defect-guided descent algorithm.
The following proposition makes this relationship
explicit.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:halt_iff_stationary}
The defect-guided descent algorithm halts at
a hypothesis $\mathbf{x}_t$ if and only if
$\mathbf{x}_t$ is operation-stationary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algorithm halts precisely when its descent
set is empty:
\[
\mathcal C_e^\downarrow(\mathbf{x}_t)
=
\varnothing.
\]
This is exactly the definition of
operation-stationarity.
\end{proof}

Operation-stationarity does not imply global
Pareto optimality, nor does it generally imply
local Pareto optimality with respect to the
algorithmic neighborhoods introduced earlier.
It establishes only that no hypothesis generated
by the available corrective operations dominates
the current hypothesis.

Furthermore, a sequence restricted to dominating
transitions cannot explore paths that temporarily
increase miscoding or mismodel. Such paths may
nevertheless lead to hypotheses with substantially
lower nescience. Operation-stationarity should
therefore be interpreted as a limitation of the
chosen descent procedure, not as evidence that
scientific inquiry has reached its ultimate limit.

\paragraph{Pruning by Active Defects.}

The active defect set provides an additional way
to analyze candidate transformations. Since
miscoding and mismodel are determined by the
maximum of their respective primitive defects,
changes that leave these maxima unchanged do
not produce immediate improvements in nescience.

The following proposition establishes a sufficient
condition under which modifying primitive defects
leaves both aggregate objectives unchanged.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:inactive_defect_no_change}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let
$\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal H_e$.

Suppose that every primitive defect active at
$\mathbf{x}$ remains unchanged at $\mathbf{y}$.
Assume also that every initially inactive defect
remains no greater than the original maximum
of its corresponding pair.

Then
\[
\mu(e,r_{\mathbf{y}})
=
\mu(e,r_{\mathbf{x}}),
\]
and
\[
\nu(r_{\mathbf{y}},d_{\mathbf{y}})
=
\nu(r_{\mathbf{x}},d_{\mathbf{x}}).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Consider the two representational defects.
By assumption, every defect attaining the
original maximum $\mu(e,r_{\mathbf{x}})$
remains unchanged.

Furthermore, neither initially inactive defect
can exceed this maximum. Therefore,
\[
\mu(e,r_{\mathbf{y}})
=
\mu(e,r_{\mathbf{x}}).
\]

The same argument applies to the descriptive
defects, yielding
\[
\nu(r_{\mathbf{y}},d_{\mathbf{y}})
=
\nu(r_{\mathbf{x}},d_{\mathbf{x}}).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition provides a pruning principle
for the immediate selection of descent candidates.
If a transformation changes only inactive defects,
without altering either aggregate objective, it
cannot belong to the descent set.

However, this does not imply that such transformations
are scientifically useless. They may prepare the
hypothesis for subsequent improvements or make
alternative transformations possible. Pruning them
is therefore justified only within the restricted
objective of finding an immediate nescience
descent candidate.

\paragraph{Bounded Search Regions.}

The hypothesis space $\mathcal H_e$ is generally
infinite, and exhaustive exploration is impractical.
One way to restrict the search is to impose
explicit upper bounds on the lengths of
representations and descriptions.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity and
$L_R,L_D\in\mathbb N$.

We define the \emph{bounded search region}
\index{Bounded search region} as
\[
\mathcal H_{e,L_R,L_D}
=
\left\{
(r,d)\in\mathcal H_e:
\ell(r)\leq L_R,\;
\ell(d)\leq L_D
\right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Because representations and descriptions are
finite binary strings, imposing length bounds
restricts the number of possible hypotheses.
The following proposition establishes that
the resulting region is finite.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bounded_region_finite}
For every entity $e\in\mathcal E$ and every
$L_R,L_D\in\mathbb N$, the bounded search
region $\mathcal H_{e,L_R,L_D}$ is finite.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The set of binary strings of length at most
$L_R$ is finite. Similarly, since
$\mathcal D\subseteq\mathcal B^\ast$,
the set of descriptions of length at most
$L_D$ is finite.

Therefore,
\[
\mathcal H_{e,L_R,L_D}
\subseteq
\{r\in\mathcal B^\ast:\ell(r)\leq L_R\}
\times
\{d\in\mathcal D:\ell(d)\leq L_D\}
\]
is finite.
\end{proof}

Bounded search regions transform an infinite
hypothesis space into a finite one. However,
finiteness does not imply effective enumerability
or decidable membership in $\mathcal H_e$,
since the underlying theoretical sets are
generally inaccessible.

Moreover, length restrictions may exclude
hypotheses requiring more elaborate representations
or descriptions. Bounded regions should therefore
be regarded as restrictions on the search rather
than as modifications of the fundamental
definition of nescience.

The corrective operations and the defect-guided
descent algorithm provide a structured way to
explore changes in representations and descriptions.
Their principal contribution is to connect the
four primitive defects with the simultaneous
minimization of miscoding and mismodel.

However, the resulting procedure is only one
possible approach to the Science Problem.
Its guarantees concern the monotonic improvement
of accepted hypotheses and finite termination,
not the discovery of globally Pareto-optimal
solutions or the identification of perfect knowledge.

\subsection*{The Parametric Case}
\label{sec:nescience_parametric_case}

The previous framework becomes more structured when descriptions are restricted to a parametric model class. Recall that, in Chapter \ref{chap:Descriptions}, a parametric model class was defined as a family of descriptions
\[
\mathcal M=\{d_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\subseteq\mathcal D,
\]
where $\Theta$ is a parameter space. Typically, each description $d_\theta=\langle TM_m,\theta\rangle$ consists of a fixed model structure $TM_m$ and a finite encoding of its parameters $\theta$.

Restricting descriptions to a model class reduces the space of hypotheses under consideration. However, a description belonging to $\mathcal M$ need not be an extended description of every representation. The feasible region must therefore preserve the relationship between representations and their associated descriptions.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and let $\mathcal M=\{d_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\subseteq\mathcal D$
be a parametric model class. We define the \emph{parametric feasible region} \index{Parametric feasible region} as
\[
\mathcal H_{e,\Theta} = \left\{(r,d_\theta)\in\mathcal H_e: \theta\in\Theta\right\}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\mathcal H_{e,\Theta} = \left\{(r,d_\theta): r\in\mathcal R_e,\;
    \theta\in\Theta,\; d_\theta\in\mathcal D_r \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The parametric feasible region contains those hypotheses whose descriptions belong to the selected model class. Unlike the unrestricted hypothesis space, it excludes descriptions that cannot be expressed within that class.

For a fixed representation $r\in\mathcal R_e$, we denote the corresponding feasible parameter set by
\[
\Theta_r = \{\theta\in\Theta:d_\theta\in\mathcal D_r\}.
\]

The science problem restricted to the parametric model class can then be expressed as
\begin{align*}
&\text{minimize}\quad \bigl(\mu(e,r),\nu(r,d_\theta)\bigr)\\
&\text{subject to}\quad r\in\mathcal R_e,\quad\theta\in\Theta_r.
\end{align*}

This formulation preserves the simultaneous minimization of miscoding and mismodel while restricting the descriptions to a particular family of models.

For a hypothesis $(r,d_\theta)$, the primitive nescience vector becomes
\[
\mathbf n(r,\theta) = \bigl(d_R(e,r),\, s_R(e,r),\, d_D(r,d_\theta),\, s_D(r,d_\theta) \bigr),
\]
where the entity $e$ is fixed.

The representational components depend only on $r$, whereas the descriptive components depend on both $r$ and $\theta$. Consequently, modifying the parameters while keeping the representation fixed leaves miscoding unchanged, although it may alter descriptive deficiency and surplus.

We can therefore characterize parametric modifications according to their effects on these two descriptive defects.

\begin{definition}
Let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be a fixed representation, and let $\theta,\theta'\in\Theta_r$. A \emph{parametric enrichment step} \index{Parametric enrichment step} is a transition
\[
\theta\mapsto\theta'
\]
such that
\[
d_D(r,d_{\theta'}) < d_D(r,d_\theta).
\]
A \emph{parametric simplification step} \index{Parametric simplification step} is a transition
\[
\theta\mapsto\theta'
\]
such that
\[
s_D(r,d_{\theta'}) < s_D(r,d_\theta).
\]
\end{definition}

These definitions characterize parameter modifications by the defects they reduce. They do not imply that a reduction in one component necessarily decreases mismodel, since the other component may increase. Moreover, a parameter modification may reduce both defects simultaneously.

The following proposition establishes a sufficient condition under which parameter refinement cannot increase mismodel, and identifies when the improvement is strict.

\begin{proposition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be fixed, and let $\theta,\theta'\in\Theta_r$. Suppose that $d_D(r,d_{\theta'}) \leq d_D(r,d_\theta)$, and $s_D(r,d_{\theta'}) \leq s_D(r,d_\theta)$. Then
\[
\nu(r,d_{\theta'}) \leq \nu(r,d_\theta).
\]
Moreover, the inequality is strict if and only if every descriptive defect attaining the original maximum $\nu(r,d_\theta)$ decreases strictly.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\nu(r,d_\theta) = \max\{d_D(r,d_\theta), s_D(r,d_\theta)\}.
\]
Since neither descriptive defect increases, monotonicity of the maximum gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nu(r,d_{\theta'})
&=
\max\{
d_D(r,d_{\theta'}),
s_D(r,d_{\theta'})
\}\\
&\leq
\max\{
d_D(r,d_\theta),
s_D(r,d_\theta)
\}\\
&=\nu(r,d_\theta).
\end{aligned}
\]
Let $M=\nu(r,d_\theta)$. Strict improvement occurs precisely when $d_D(r,d_{\theta'})<M$ and $s_D(r,d_{\theta'})<M$. Under the assumed non-increase of both components, this condition is equivalent to requiring that every component attaining the original maximum $M$ decreases strictly.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that reducing descriptive deficiency or surplus does not necessarily reduce mismodel. In particular, when both defects attain the same maximum, both must decrease for mismodel to decrease strictly. If mismodel does decrease, the resulting hypothesis dominates the original one, since miscoding remains unchanged.

The parametric formulation also clarifies the distinction between parameter refinement and changes to the model structure. Adjusting parameters within $\Theta_r$ preserves membership in the selected model class. By contrast, introducing new explanatory structures, removing model components, or changing the family of models may require replacing $\mathcal M$ with another model class.

Likewise, modifying the representation through augmentation or reduction changes the target of description, and may require modifying the model parameters or selecting a different model class.

Thus, parametric models provide additional structure for exploring the hypothesis space without changing the fundamental formulation of the science problem. Representations and descriptions remain jointly optimized, while the parametric model class determines which descriptions are available for consideration.

\section{Best Hypothesis}

In many scientific contexts, the objective is not to identify every possible hypothesis, but to determine
which of the known alternatives offers the best available understanding of the entity under investigation. Since miscoding and mismodel are distinct objectives, the selection of a single hypothesis requires a decision criterion. We use a scalarization function $V$ for this purpose, as introduced in the preceding section on Scalar Nescience.

We call the hypothesis that minimizes effective scalar nescience among the known alternatives the \emph{current best hypothesis}.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity with a nonempty set of known hypotheses $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$, and let $V:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2\to\mathbb R$ be a scalarization function. We define the set of \emph{current best hypotheses} \index{Current best hypothesis} of $e$ as
\[
\mathcal B_{e,V} = \operatorname*{argmin}_{ (r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e} \widehat N_V(e,r,d).
\]
Whenever this set is nonempty, each $(r^\star,d^\star)\in\mathcal B_{e,V}$ is called a current best hypothesis of $e$, relative to $V$.
\end{definition}

The current best hypothesis minimizes neither effective miscoding nor effective mismodel independently. Rather, it minimizes their scalarization, reflecting the preferences encoded by the chosen decision criterion.

The current best hypothesis need not be unique. Different representation-description pairs may have the same scalar effective nescience, even when their individual components differ.

We begin by establishing a sufficient condition for the existence of a current best hypothesis.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:current_best_existence}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity, and suppose that $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ is finite and nonempty, with effective nescience defined for every hypothesis. Then, for any scalarization function
$V:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^2\to\mathbb R$,
\[
\mathcal B_{e,V}\neq\varnothing.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e$ is finite and nonempty, the set
\[
\left\{\widehat N_V(e,r,d): (r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e \right\}
\]
is a finite nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$, and therefore has a minimum. Any hypothesis attaining this minimum belongs to $\mathcal B_{e,V}$. Hence the set of current best hypotheses is nonempty.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that a current best hypothesis exists whenever a finite collection of known hypotheses can be evaluated using effective nescience. The result does not guarantee uniqueness, nor does it establish any form of theoretical optimality.

We can also define the minimum effective scalar nescience attained by the current best hypotheses.

\begin{definition}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity and let $V$ be a scalarization function such that $\mathcal B_{e,V}\neq\varnothing$. We define the \emph{current best nescience} \index{Current best nescience} of $e$, relative to $V$, as
\[
\widehat b_{e,V} = \min_{(r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e} \widehat N_V(e,r,d).
\]
\end{definition}

The quantity $\widehat b_{e,V}$ summarizes the lowest scalar effective nescience currently attained by the
known hypotheses. It therefore provides a numerical reference for evaluating alternative hypotheses about the same entity. Its value depends on the adopted scalarization and on the effective measures used to assess the
available hypotheses. Consequently, it should not be interpreted as an absolute quantity independent of the evaluation framework.

\subsection*{Current Best and Perfect Knowledge}

The concept of current best hypothesis must be distinguished from perfect knowledge and negligible perfect knowledge.

Recall that perfect knowledge of a knowable entity $e$ is attained by a hypothesis $(r,d)$ satisfying $r\in\overline{\mathcal R}_e$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r^\star$. Perfect knowledge is therefore a structural property, requiring an admissible representation and a minimal description. It implies negligible perfect knowledge, for which theoretical miscoding and mismodel satisfy $\mu(e,r)=O(1)$ and $\nu(r,d)=O(1)$, with constants fixed by the underlying coding conventions. The converse does not generally hold: a hypothesis may have negligible theoretical defects without satisfying the structural requirements of perfect knowledge.

By contrast, the current best hypothesis is defined through the minimization of effective scalar nescience over the known hypotheses. Consequently, being a current best hypothesis implies neither perfect knowledge nor negligible perfect knowledge. In particular, even if a known hypothesis satisfies $\widehat\mu(e,r)=0$ and $ \widehat\nu(r,d)=0$, we cannot conclude that $r\in\overline{\mathcal R}_e$ and $d\in\mathcal D_r^\star$. The effective measures may fail to detect important defects, and their vanishing does not establish the absence of theoretical epistemic error.

The converse is also not guaranteed. A hypothesis attaining perfect knowledge may receive a relatively high effective nescience value because the available evaluation methods are inadequate or because the evidence required to recognize its quality is missing. Furthermore, perfect knowledge does not necessarily minimize every theoretical scalarization. It guarantees only constant-size defects, rather than the exact numerical minima of miscoding and mismodel.

Thus, perfect knowledge, negligible perfect knowledge, and current best status express distinct properties. The first characterizes an ideal structural relationship between an entity, its representation, and its description. The second characterizes hypotheses with negligible theoretical defects. The third identifies the preferred known hypothesis under a chosen effective evaluation criterion. This distinction is particularly important in scientific practice. A hypothesis may be regarded as the best available explanation for a phenomenon and nevertheless be based on an inadequate representation. Conversely, a theoretically excellent hypothesis may be rejected or overlooked because its advantages are not yet recognized by the available evaluation procedures.

The current best hypothesis should therefore be understood as a provisional assessment of scientific understanding, rather than evidence that the limits of knowledge have been reached.

\subsection*{Decreasing Current Best Nescience}

Once a current best hypothesis has been identified, scientific inquiry may seek alternative representations and descriptions that provide lower effective nescience. Such improvements may arise by modifying the representation, refining its description, or replacing both components simultaneously. The relevant criterion is the effective nescience of the resulting hypothesis, rather than the particular operation used to construct it.

We begin by considering the addition of a new hypothesis to the collection of known hypotheses. Let $h'=(r',d')$ be a newly proposed hypothesis about $e$, and suppose that its effective nescience can be evaluated using the same measures and scalarization as the existing hypotheses. The expanded known hypothesis space is
\[
\widehat{\mathcal H}_e^{+} = \widehat{\mathcal H}_e\cup\{h'\}.
\]
An immediate consequence of this expansion is that current best nescience cannot increase when the evaluation criterion remains unchanged.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:current_best_expansion}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and suppose that $\mathcal B_{e,V}\neq\varnothing$. Let $h'=(r',d')$ be a new hypothesis, and assume that the effective measures and scalarization remain unchanged for the previously known hypotheses. Then the current best nescience over $\widehat{\mathcal H}_e^{+}$ satisfies
\[
\widehat b_{e,V}^{+} = \min\left\{ \widehat b_{e,V}, \widehat N_V(e,r',d') \right\}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\widehat b_{e,V}^{+} = \min_{(r,d)\in\widehat{\mathcal H}_e^{+}} \widehat N_V(e,r,d).
\]
Since
\[
\widehat{\mathcal H}_e^{+} = \widehat{\mathcal H}_e\cup\{(r',d')\},
\]
minimization over the expanded set is equivalent to taking the minimum between the previous minimum and the score of the new hypothesis. Therefore,
\[
\widehat b_{e,V}^{+} = \min\left\{ \widehat b_{e,V}, \widehat N_V(e,r',d') \right\}.
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that the discovery of an additional hypothesis cannot worsen the best attainable effective nescience, provided the evaluation procedure remains fixed. A strict improvement occurs precisely when
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r',d') < \widehat b_{e,V}.
\]

The new hypothesis need not dominate the previous current best hypothesis in the Pareto sense. It may improve one effective objective while worsening the other, provided the improvement is preferred under the adopted scalarization.

The effective dominance provides a particularly strong sufficient condition for decreasing current best nescience.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:current_best_strict_improvement}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$, and let $V$ be strictly increasing in each coordinate. Suppose that a new hypothesis $(r',d')$ effectively dominates some $(r^\star,d^\star)\in\mathcal B_{e,V}$. Then
\[
\widehat b_{e,V}^{+} < \widehat b_{e,V}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $(r',d')$ effectively dominates $(r^\star,d^\star)$, we have
\[
\widehat\mu(e,r') \leq \widehat\mu(e,r^\star),
\]
and
\[
\widehat\nu(r',d') \leq \widehat\nu(r^\star,d^\star),
\]
with at least one strict inequality. By strict monotonicity of $V$,
\[
\widehat N_V(e,r',d') < \widehat N_V(e,r^\star,d^\star) = \widehat b_{e,V}.
\]
The result follows from Proposition \ref{prop:current_best_expansion}.
\end{proof}

The proposition establishes that effective dominance of a current best hypothesis guarantees a strict improvement under every strictly increasing scalarization. However, dominance is sufficient rather than necessary, since scalarization permits trade-offs between miscoding and mismodel.

This distinction is important when designing strategies for scientific discovery. Improving a description while keeping its representation fixed may reduce effective mismodel without changing effective miscoding. Conversely, modifying the representation may reduce effective miscoding, but may also require developing a new description. Neither strategy is universally preferable. A description may be nearly optimal for its representation while the representation itself remains inadequate. Likewise, a more informative representation may initially be associated with a poor description, requiring additional work before its potential advantages become apparent.

The current best hypothesis therefore provides a reference for evaluating candidate improvements, but it does not determine which transformations should be explored. Moreover, a strategy that accepts only hypotheses with immediately lower effective nescience may exclude intermediate hypotheses that eventually lead to better solutions. Scientific inquiry may require temporary increases in miscoding or mismodel, exploration of unfamiliar model classes, or substantial changes in the representation of the entity. Finally, a reduction in effective nescience does not necessarily imply a reduction in theoretical nescience. The effective measures may overlook important errors or reward hypotheses that exploit limitations of the evaluation procedure. Thus, decreasing current best nescience is a useful operational objective, but its scientific significance depends on the reliability of the underlying effective measures.

\section{Nescience of Areas}
\label{sec:nescience_areas}

As discussed in Section \ref{sec:research_areas}, entities can be grouped into research areas, defined as subsets $\mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{E}$ of the overall entity space. The concept of a research area is meaningful when the entities in $\mathcal{A}$ share a common epistemic domain or possess a unifying property, such as belonging to the same scientific field or addressing a related phenomenon. To quantify how much we do not know about a research area $\mathcal{A}$, we must work with the subset of entities within $\mathcal{A}$ for which at least one hypothesis is currently known $\hat{\mathcal{A}} \subseteq \mathcal{A}$. A representation of the area $\mathcal{A}$ is then a symbolic encoding $R_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}$ of the known entities in $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$, suitable for further descriptive analysis. Given this representation, we define a description of the area $\mathcal{A}$ as a string $d_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}$ that models or summarizes the structure or content of $R_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}$.

The concept of nescience can be extended from individual entities to entire research areas, allowing us to quantitatively assess how well a given area is understood. This extension relies on generalizing the core components of nescience to sets of entities. We use the notions of combined miscoding of the known subset (see Section \ref{sec:miscoding_areas}), combined inaccuracy (see Section \ref{sec:inaccuracy_areas}), and combined surfeit (see Section \ref{sec:surfeit_areas}) to define a composite measure of nescience at the area level.

In practice, due to the difficulty of precisely identifying the entire known subset $\hat{\mathcal{A}} \subseteq \mathcal{A}$, it is often convenient to use approximations. One practical and interpretable approximation is the notion of average nescience, which provides a summary of the epistemic quality of the known entities within the area.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{E}$ be an area, and $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ be the subset of known entities. The \emph{average nescience of the area} is defined as:
\[
\bar{\nu}(\mathcal{A}) = \frac{1}{|\hat{\mathcal{A}}|} \sum_{e \in \hat{\mathcal{A}}} \nu(e),
\]
where $\nu(e)$ denotes the nescience of the current best hypothesis about entity $e$ given a particular decision maker.
\end{definition}

For instance, when dealing with research topics, a research area may correspond to a broad disciplinary domain, such as biology, mathematics, or physics, each comprising a collection of entities that fall under that domain. In this context, the area of biology would include all research topics formally classified within the biological sciences. This classification allows us to compute and compare the nescience of distinct knowledge domains by aggregating the nescience of the individual topics they encompass (see Section \ref{sec:measuring_research_areas}). Such comparisons provide insight into which areas of human knowledge remain most poorly understood and where future research efforts might be most impactful.
