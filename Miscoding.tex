\chapter{Miscoding}
\label{chap:Miscoding}

\begin{quote}
    \begin{flushright}
        \emph{All great work is the fruit of patience and perseverance,\\
            combined with tenacious concentration on a subject\\
            over a period of months or years.}\\
        Santiago Ramón y Cajal
    \end{flushright}
\end{quote}
\bigskip

In many areas of science, the entities we aim to study (the set $\mathcal{E}$) often include abstract concepts or complex phenomena that are difficult to investigate directly. These may be ideas or processes that resist straightforward description or modeling. To gain insight into such entities, we must rely on indirect methods. As discussed in Chapter \ref{chap:Representations}, the theory of nescience suggests using representations, that is, sequences of symbols, rather than attempting to interact directly with the entities themselves.

However, this approach introduces its own challenges. Because our understanding of the elements in $\mathcal{E}$ is incomplete (otherwise, further research would be unnecessary), the representations we construct are typically imperfect, they lack the completeness and precision we would ideally desire. These imperfections can lead to significant problems: inaccuracies in representation can propagate into the models derived from them, potentially distorting our interpretation and understanding. It is therefore essential to recognize this source of error and to carefully consider its implications.

To address this issue, we introduce the concept of \emph{miscoding}, a measure used to quantify the error that arises from misrepresenting or inaccurately encoding entities. Miscoding is defined in terms of the length of the shortest computer program capable of transforming an incorrect representation into an admissible one. In essence, it measures the amount of effort, as reflected by the program's length, required to correct a faulty representation.

However, there is a significant complication: applying this idea in practice is not straightforward, since the admissible representations of entities are unknown. As a result, we cannot directly measure the gap between a given representation and an ideal one. Nevertheless, we propose a theoretical framework based on the oracle machine (an abstract construct) used to define the set $\mathcal{E}$. This oracle machine is assumed to be capable of recognizing admissible representations for all entities in the set.

Gaining a deeper understanding of scientific representation and the difficulties involved in reducing miscoding can significantly advance scientific research, enabling the development of more accurate and comprehensive models of the natural world. In this chapter, we formally introduce the concept of \emph{miscoding} and explore its key properties.

\section{Deficiency of a Representation}
\label{sec:deficiency}

A representation $r$ of an entity $e$ may be incomplete, in the sense that it may fail to contain some of the information required to reconstruct the entity by the underlying representation oracle. In scientific practice, such incompleteness appears whenever the available observations, measurements, or encodings do not yet fully constrain the phenomenon under study. We formalize this notion by quantifying how much additional algorithmic information is needed to recover an admissible representation of $e$ from the given representation $r$. We call this quantity the \emph{deficiency} of $r$ with respect to $e$.

Throughout this section, we assume that a representation oracle is fixed and that the entity under consideration admits at least one admissible representation under this oracle. We begin by defining deficiency in terms of conditional Kolmogorov complexity.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:deficiency}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\bar{\mathcal R}_e\neq\varnothing$, and let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be an extended representation of $e$. We define the \emph{deficiency}\index{Deficiency of a representation} of $r$ with respect to $e$ as
\[
d_R(e,r) = \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e}K(\bar r\mid r).
\]
\end{definition}

The quantity $K(\bar r\mid r)$ measures the length of the shortest computer program that, given $r$, outputs an admissible representation $\bar r$ of $e$. Minimizing over $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ selects the admissible representation that is easiest to recover from $r$. Since $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ is nonempty and conditional Kolmogorov complexity takes values in $\mathbb N$, the minimum is well defined.

Deficiency can therefore be interpreted as the minimum amount of additional algorithmic information required to recover an admissible representation of the entity. This quantity measures information in bits, not the computational time or effort required to perform the reconstruction. The representation oracle determines which encodings are admissible, whereas conditional Kolmogorov complexity measures the information required to recover one of them.

\begin{example}
Let $e$ be an entity corresponding to a particular finite undirected graph, for example a molecular interaction network or a transportation network. An admissible representation $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ may encode the complete adjacency list of the graph, allowing the underlying oracle to reconstruct its full structure. In practice, however, one often works with partial observations. For instance, a representation $r\in\mathcal R_e$ may encode a subgraph obtained from incomplete measurements, missing edges, or partially observed nodes. Assume that this partial representation contains nontrivial algorithmic information about an admissible representation of the complete graph, so that it satisfies the definition of extended representation. The deficiency $d_R(e,r)$ measures, in algorithmic terms, the additional information required to recover an admissible representation of the complete network from the available partial observations.
\end{example}

Deficiency is nonnegative and bounded above by the complexity of an admissible representation of the entity.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_bounds}
For every $r\in\mathcal R_e$,
\[
0 \le d_R(e,r) \le \bar\kappa_e+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, the conditional Kolmogorov complexity $K(\bar r\mid r)$ is nonnegative. Taking the minimum over $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ gives $d_R(e,r)\ge 0$.

For the upper bound, choose an admissible representation $\bar r_0\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ satisfying $K(\bar r_0)=\bar\kappa_e$. By the definition of deficiency $d_R(e,r)\le K(\bar r_0\mid r)$. A program can ignore its conditional input and output $\bar r_0$. Therefore,
\[
K(\bar r_0\mid r) \le K(\bar r_0)+O(1) =\bar\kappa_e+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
0\le d_R(e,r) \le \bar\kappa_e+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that deficiency is always finite for an entity admitting an admissible representation. In the worst case, the missing information can be supplied by providing an admissible encoding of the entity from scratch, without using the information already contained in the original representation.

The next proposition establishes that admissible extended representations are essentially complete: they require no substantial additional information to recover an admissible representation of the entity.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_admissible}
Let $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e\cap\mathcal R_e$ be an admissible extended representation of $e$. Then
\[
d_R(e,r)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, we may choose $\bar r=r$ in Definition~\ref{def:deficiency}. Hence $d_R(e,r)\le K(r\mid r)$. A fixed program can output its conditional input, so $K(r\mid r)=O(1)$. Together with the nonnegativity of deficiency, this gives $d_R(e,r)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

This result formalizes the intuition that admissible extended representations already contain the information necessary for reconstructing the entity. The converse does not generally hold: a representation may have negligible deficiency while containing substantial information unrelated to the entity.

The following result provides an upper bound on the deficiency of one representation in terms of another and the information required to recover the latter from the former. This property allows us to determine how deficiency changes when one representation is transformed into another.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_triangle}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ admit at least one admissible representation, and let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
d_R(e,r) \le d_R(e,s) + K(s\mid r) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ be arbitrary. Given $r$, one may first execute a shortest conditional program that reconstructs $s$ from $r$, and then execute a shortest conditional program that reconstructs $\bar r$ from $s$. By the composition property of conditional Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
K(\bar r\mid r) \le K(s\mid r)+K(\bar r\mid s)+O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_R(e,r)
&= \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e}K(\bar r\mid r)\\
&\le K(s\mid r) + \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e}K(\bar r\mid s) +O(1)\\
&= K(s\mid r)+d_R(e,s)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
This establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that the deficiency of $r$ cannot exceed that of $s$ by more than the additional information required to recover $s$ from $r$, up to a constant. In particular, if $s$ can be recovered from $r$ using a short program, then $r$ cannot be substantially more deficient than $s$. The result provides a general foundation for studying modifications of representations. 

An important special case occurs when a new representation preserves the information contained in an existing one. This gives rise to a monotonicity principle for computable enrichment.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_enrichment}
Let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$, and suppose that $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$. Then
\[
d_R(e,s)\le d_R(e,r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_triangle} with the roles of $r$ and $s$ exchanged yields
\[
d_R(e,s) \le d_R(e,r) + K(r\mid s) + O(1).
\]
By hypothesis $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$, and therefore
\[
d_R(e,s)\le d_R(e,r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This result expresses a fundamental monotonicity principle: preserving the information contained in a representation cannot substantially increase its deficiency. In particular, if a new representation allows the original one to be recovered by a fixed computable procedure, then the new representation cannot require substantially more information to recover an admissible encoding of the entity. However, this does not imply that every computable enrichment is beneficial. Additional information may be redundant or unrelated to the entity.

The preceding results suggest that deficiency depends primarily on the algorithmic information available in a representation, rather than on its particular syntactic form. We now formalize this observation for representations that can be reconstructed from one another at constant algorithmic cost.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_algorithmic_equivalence}
Let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$ satisfy $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid r)=O(1)$. Then
\[
d_R(e,r)=d_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_triangle},
\[
d_R(e,r) \le d_R(e,s) + K(s\mid r) + O(1).
\]
Since $K(s\mid r)=O(1)$,
\[
d_R(e,r)\le d_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
Exchanging the roles of $r$ and $s$ gives
\[
d_R(e,s) \le d_R(e,r) + K(r\mid s) + O(1).
\]
Using $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$, we obtain
\[
d_R(e,s)\le d_R(e,r)+O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
d_R(e,r)=d_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that algorithmically equivalent representations have essentially the same deficiency. In particular, two representations that can be transformed into one another by fixed computable procedures contain the same recoverable algorithmic information about the entity, up to a constant overhead. For example, two losslessly equivalent encodings of an experimental dataset have negligible differences in deficiency when their conversion procedures have constant descriptional complexity. The relevant property is therefore not equality of strings, but equivalence of the information they make available.

As with Kolmogorov complexity itself, deficiency depends on the choice of reference universal machine only up to an additive constant. This provides an important robustness property: its essential algorithmic interpretation does not depend on the particular computational formalism used to express conditional programs. To isolate this dependence, we hold the representation oracle and the admissible representation set fixed while changing the reference universal machine.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficiency_machine_invariance}
Let $U$ and $V$ be two reference universal prefix-free machines, and let $d_R^{\,U}(e,r)$ and $d_R^{\,V}(e,r)$ denote the deficiency of $r$ computed using the corresponding conditional Kolmogorov complexities $K_U(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ and $K_V(\cdot\mid\cdot)$. Assume that the admissible representation set $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ remains fixed. Then, for every $r\in\mathcal R_e$,
\[
d_R^{\,U}(e,r) = d_R^{\,V}(e,r) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the invariance theorem for conditional Kolmogorov complexity,
there exist constants $c_{UV}$ and $c_{VU}$, depending only
on the reference machines, such that for every pair of
binary strings $x,y$,
\[
K_U(x\mid y)\le K_V(x\mid y)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
K_V(x\mid y)\le K_U(x\mid y)+c_{VU}.
\]
Applying the first inequality to each $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ gives
\[
K_U(\bar r\mid r) \le K_V(\bar r\mid r) + c_{UV}.
\]
Taking minima over $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_R^{\,U}(e,r)
&= \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e}K_U(\bar r\mid r)\\
&\le \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} \bigl(K_V(\bar r\mid r)+c_{UV}\bigr)\\
&= d_R^{\,V}(e,r) + c_{UV}.
\end{aligned}
\]
By symmetry,
\[
d_R^{\,V}(e,r)\le d_R^{\,U}(e,r)+c_{VU}.
\]
Combining both inequalities gives
\[
d_R^{\,U}(e,r)=d_R^{\,V}(e,r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that deficiency is robust under the choice of universal machine in the same sense as conditional Kolmogorov complexity. Although its exact numerical value may change by a constant, its interpretation as missing algorithmic information remains stable.

The assumption that the admissible representation set is fixed is important. Since admissibility itself is defined using Kolmogorov complexity, changing the reference machine may also affect the set of representations satisfying its defining conditions. Such changes concern the reference set and must be distinguished from the ordinary machine dependence of conditional complexity.

\section{Surplus of a Representation}
\label{sec:surplus}

A representation $r$ of an entity $e$ may contain more information than is needed to represent the entity. Some of this additional content may be redundant, that is, information that is not essential for reconstruction but may still be useful for identifying models. However, a representation may also contain extraneous information that is unrelated to the entity and may complicate the modeling task. We call this latter form of representational defect \emph{surplus}.

We formalize surplus by measuring the additional algorithmic information required to reconstruct a representation from an admissible representation of the same entity.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:surplus}
\index{Surplus of a representation}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, so that $\bar{\mathcal R}_e\neq\varnothing$, and let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be an extended representation of $e$. We define the \emph{surplus} of $r$ relative to $e$ as
\[
s_R(e,r) = \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e}K(r\mid\bar r).
\]
\end{definition}

The quantity $K(r\mid\bar r)$ measures the length of the shortest program that, given an admissible representation $\bar r$ of $e$, outputs $r$. Minimizing over all admissible representations selects the one from which $r$ is easiest to reconstruct. Since $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ is nonempty and conditional Kolmogorov complexity takes values in $\mathbb N$, the minimum is well defined.

Surplus therefore measures the additional algorithmic information needed to generate an extended representation from an admissible one. Its interpretation as extraneous information is grounded in the admissibility criterion established in Chapter~3.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:surplus_irrelevant_variable}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the relationship between certain biological features of plants and their species identity. An admissible representation $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ may encode the morphological measurements required to study this relationship, such as leaf shape, petal length, and stem height. A dataset $r\in\mathcal R_e$ may contain these measurements together with additional fields, such as sensor serial numbers or identifiers of the technicians who recorded the samples. Suppose these additional fields contain independent information unrelated to the entity being represented. Although the biological measurements provide genuine information about $e$, reconstructing the full dataset from an admissible representation may require specifying the additional fields. The surplus $s_R(e,r)$ measures the minimum algorithmic information required for this reconstruction beyond that provided by an admissible representation.
\end{example}

The first proposition establishes that surplus is nonnegative and bounded above by the algorithmic complexity of the representation itself.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_upper_bound}
For every $r\in\mathcal R_e$,
\[
0 \le s_R(e,r) \le K(r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, the conditional complexity $K(r\mid\bar r)$ is nonnegative. Therefore $s_R(e,r) \ge 0$.

For the upper bound, fix any $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$. By definition $s_R(e,r) \le K(r\mid\bar r)$. Since a program can ignore its conditional input and output $r$, we have
\[
K(r\mid\bar r) \le K(r) + O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
s_R(e,r) \le K(r) + O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that surplus is always finite and cannot exceed, up to an additive constant, the algorithmic complexity of the representation itself. In the worst case, the entire representation can be reconstructed from scratch, without using the information contained in an admissible representation of the entity.

The next proposition establishes that admissible representations have negligible surplus.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_admissible}
Let $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e\cap\mathcal R_e$ be an admissible extended representation of $e$. Then
\[
s_R(e,r)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$, we may choose $\bar r=r$ in Definition~\ref{def:surplus}. Hence $s_R(e,r)\le K(r\mid r)$. A fixed program can output its conditional input, so $K(r\mid r)=O(1)$. Together with nonnegativity, this yields $s_R(e,r)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

This result is consistent with the definition of admissibility: an admissible representation requires no substantial additional information to reconstruct itself from an admissible reference. The converse does not generally follow, since negligible surplus does not necessarily imply that the representation satisfies every condition required for admissibility.

An important property of surplus concerns its behavior under computable transformations. The following result bounds the surplus of one representation in terms of another and the information required to reconstruct the former from the latter.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_triangle}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,s) + K(r\mid s) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ be arbitrary. Given $\bar r$, one may first execute a shortest conditional program that reconstructs $s$ from $\bar r$, and then execute a shortest conditional program that reconstructs $r$ from $s$. By the composition property of conditional Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
K(r\mid\bar r) \le K(s\mid\bar r)+K(r\mid s)+O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over all admissible representations gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_R(e,r)
& = \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} K(r\mid\bar r)\\
& \le \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} \bigl(K(s\mid\bar r)+K(r\mid s)+O(1)\bigr)\\
& = s_R(e,s)+K(r\mid s)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that the surplus of $r$ cannot exceed that of $s$ by more than the additional information required to reconstruct $r$ from $s$, up to a constant. It provides a general bound from which the effects of particular representation transformations can be derived.

An immediate consequence is a monotonicity property for transformations that require only a constant amount of additional algorithmic information.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:surplus_monotonicity}
Let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$ satisfy $K(r\mid s) = O(1)$. Then
\[
s_R(e,r)\le s_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_triangle},
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,s) + K(r\mid s) + O(1).
\]
Since $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$, the result follows.
\end{proof}

This result establishes that a representation obtained from another through a fixed computable transformation cannot have substantially greater surplus. The constant may depend on the transformation, but not on the particular representations to which it is applied.

The preceding results also imply that surplus is preserved, up to a constant, when two representations contain the same recoverable algorithmic information.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_algorithmic_equivalence}
Let $r,s\in\mathcal R_e$ satisfy $K(r\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid r)=O(1)$. Then
\[
s_R(e,r)=s_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_triangle},
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,s) + K(r\mid s) + O(1) = s_R(e,s) + O(1).
\]
Exchanging the roles of $r$ and $s$ gives
\[
s_R(e,s) \le s_R(e,r) + K(s\mid r) + O(1) = s_R(e,r) + O(1).
\]
Combining both inequalities establishes the result.
\end{proof}

Thus, algorithmically equivalent representations have essentially the same surplus. This confirms that surplus,
like deficiency, depends on recoverable algorithmic information rather than on the particular encoding used.

As in the case of deficiency, surplus is invariant under a change of reference universal machine up to an additive constant, provided that the set of admissible representations is held fixed.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:surplus_machine_invariance}
Let $U$ and $V$ be two reference universal prefix-free machines, and let $s_R^{\,U}(e,r)$ and $s_R^{\,V}(e,r)$ denote the surplus computed using the corresponding conditional Kolmogorov complexities $K_U$ and $K_V$. Assume that the representation oracle and the admissible representation set $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ remain fixed. Then, for every $r\in\mathcal R_e$ belonging to the common domain under both reference machines,
\[
s_R^{\,U}(e,r) = s_R^{\,V}(e,r) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the invariance theorem for conditional Kolmogorov complexity, there exist constants $c_{UV}$ and $c_{VU}$, depending only on the reference machines, such that for every pair of binary strings $x,y$,
\[
K_U(x\mid y)\le K_V(x\mid y)+c_{UV},
\]
and
\[
K_V(x\mid y)\le K_U(x\mid y)+c_{VU}.
\]
Applying the first inequality to every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ gives
\[
K_U(r\mid\bar r) \le K_V(r\mid\bar r) + c_{UV}.
\]
Taking minima over the fixed admissible set,
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_R^{\,U}(e,r)
& = \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} K_U(r\mid\bar r)\\
& \le \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} \bigl(K_V(r\mid\bar r)+c_{UV}\bigr)\\
& = s_R^{\,V}(e,r)+c_{UV}.
\end{aligned}
\]
By symmetry,
\[
s_R^{\,V}(e,r) \le s_R^{\,U}(e,r)+c_{VU}.
\]
Combining both inequalities yields
\[
s_R^{\,U}(e,r) = s_R^{\,V}(e,r)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

Surplus is therefore robust under the choice of universal machine in the same sense as deficiency and conditional Kolmogorov complexity. Its exact value may vary by a constant, but its interpretation as additional algorithmic information relative to admissible representations remains unchanged.

Surplus complements deficiency by measuring a different aspect of representational quality. Whereas deficiency concerns the information needed to recover an admissible representation from an extended one, surplus concerns the information needed to reconstruct the extended representation from an admissible one.

\section{Miscoding}
\label{sec:miscoding}

The concepts of deficiency and surplus capture two fundamentally different ways in which a representation may fail. Deficiency measures how much information about the entity is still missing, whereas surplus measures how much information in the representation is not genuinely attributable to the entity. Since these two quantities need not decrease simultaneously, the analysis of miscoding is naturally formulated as a multiobjective optimization problem (see Section~\ref{sec:multiobjective_optimization}) rather than as a single-objective one.

% Section: Pareto Optimality

\subsection*{Pareto optimality}

Before introducing a scalar measure of miscoding, it is useful to make explicit that representation refinement involves two competing objectives.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:miscoding_problem}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\mathcal R_e\neq\varnothing$. We define the \emph{miscoding problem}\index{Miscoding problem} for the entity $e$ as the following multiobjective optimization problem:
\[
\min_{r\in\mathcal R_e} \bigl(d_R(e,r),\,s_R(e,r)\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

This formulation emphasizes that the miscoding problem is not, at its core, a single-objective problem but a trade-off between two distinct desiderata: informational completeness and absence of extraneous content. In scientific practice, some refinements decrease deficiency at the cost of increasing surplus, while others remove surplus but also discard useful information.

The natural comparison relation for the miscoding problem is dominance (see Definition~\ref{def:multiobjective_dominance}). We now introduce this relation in the context of representations.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:dominance_miscoding}
Let $r,r'\in\mathcal R_e$ be two representations. We say that $r'$ \emph{dominates}\index{Representation dominance} $r$, denoted by $r'\prec_e r$, if
\[
d_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r), \qquad s_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r),
\]
and at least one of these inequalities is strict.
\end{definition}

A dominating representation is at least as good in both components and strictly better in one. Thus, a dominated representation is suboptimal in a precise sense: there exists another extended representation that improves one component without worsening the other.

The appropriate notion of optimality for a multiobjective problem is Pareto optimality (see Definition~\ref{def:pareto_optimal}). We are interested in those representations for which no simultaneous improvement is possible.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:pareto_miscoding}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\mathcal R_e\neq\varnothing$. A representation $r\in\mathcal R_e$ is said to be \emph{Pareto optimal}\index{Pareto optimal representation} with respect to deficiency and surplus if there does not exist any $r'\in\mathcal R_e$ such that $r'\prec_e r$.

The set of all Pareto-optimal representations of $e$ is denoted by
\[
\mathcal P_e = \bigl\{ r\in\mathcal R_e: \nexists r'\in\mathcal R_e \text{ such that }r'\prec_e r \bigr\},
\]
and is called the \emph{Pareto frontier}\index{Miscoding Pareto frontier} of the miscoding problem.
\end{definition}

The Pareto frontier collects precisely those representations for which an improvement in one component necessarily entails a deterioration in the other. In this sense, it characterizes the trade-offs among theoretically non-dominated representations of the entity.

Admissible representations have negligible deficiency and surplus, as established in the preceding sections. However, this does not necessarily imply Pareto optimality, since quantities that are negligible up to additive constants need not attain their exact minima.

The next property clarifies why dominated representations can be discarded when using strictly increasing scalar criteria.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:dominated_not_minimal_increasing_scalarization}
Let $\phi:\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^2\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}$ be strictly increasing in each argument. If $r\in\mathcal R_e$ is dominated, then $r$ cannot minimize $\phi(d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r))$ over $\mathcal R_e$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Assume that $r$ is dominated. Then there exists $r'\in\mathcal R_e$ such that $d_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r)$ and $s_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r)$, and at least one inequality is strict. Since $\phi$ is strictly increasing in each argument, it follows that
\[
\phi(d_R(e,r'),s_R(e,r')) < \phi(d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)).
\]
Therefore, $r$ cannot be a minimizer of $\phi$.
\end{proof}

This result formalizes an important elimination principle: a dominated representation is suboptimal for every strictly increasing scalarization of deficiency and surplus. Consequently, the search for an optimum of such a scalarization can be restricted to the Pareto frontier.

Before considering particular scalarizations, it is useful to establish that Pareto-optimal representations exist. Since deficiency and surplus are measured by program lengths, their values are nonnegative integers.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:pareto_nonempty_finite}
If $\mathcal R_e$ is nonempty, then $\mathcal P_e$ is nonempty.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider the set
\[
S = \bigl\{d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r): r\in\mathcal R_e \bigr\}.
\]
Since $\mathcal R_e$ is nonempty and both deficiency and surplus take values in $\mathbb N_0$, the set $S\subseteq\mathbb N_0$ is nonempty. By the well-ordering principle, $S$ has a minimum. Choose $r\in\mathcal R_e$ such that
\[
d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r)=\min S.
\]
Suppose, for contradiction, that $r$ is not Pareto optimal. Then there exists $r'\in\mathcal R_e$
such that $d_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r)$ and $s_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r)$, with at least one strict inequality. Consequently,
\[
d_R(e,r')+s_R(e,r') < d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r),
\]
contradicting the minimality of the sum. Therefore $r\in\mathcal P_e$, and hence $\mathcal P_e\neq\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

This proposition guarantees the existence of at least one Pareto-optimal representation whenever the miscoding problem has a nonempty feasible set. The result holds even when $\mathcal R_e$ is infinite, because the objectives take nonnegative integer values.

% Section: Scalar Miscoding

\subsection*{Scalar miscoding}

Although the Pareto frontier captures the full trade-off structure, in many applications one still wishes to assign a single miscoding value to each representation. A natural scalarization is to minimize the worst of the two components.

We now introduce the canonical scalar score used in this book for the concept of miscoding.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:scalar_miscoding}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ and $r\in\mathcal R_e$. The \emph{miscoding}\index{Miscoding} of $r$ with respect to $e$ is defined by
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\bigl\{d_R(e,r),\,s_R(e,r)\bigr\}.
\]
\end{definition}

This definition corresponds to the Chebyshev scalarization of a bi-objective problem (see Section~\ref{sec:pareto_optimization_methods}). It assigns to each representation the magnitude of its dominant defect, thereby avoiding the compensation of a large value of one component by a small value of the other.

The first property establishes the relationship between miscoding and admissible representations.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:miscoding_admissible}
Let $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e\cap\mathcal R_e$ be an admissible extended representation of $e$. Then
\[
\mu(e,r)=O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $r$ is admissible, the corresponding properties of deficiency and surplus give $d_R(e,r)=O(1)$ and $s_R(e,r)=O(1)$. Therefore,
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\} = O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition confirms that admissible extended representations have negligible miscoding, as expected from their theoretical role. In particular, whenever $\bar{\mathcal R}_e\cap\mathcal R_e\neq\varnothing$, the minimum achievable miscoding over $\mathcal R_e$ is $O(1)$.

An important justification for the Chebyshev scalarization is its relationship with the Pareto frontier. However, since the maximum is nondecreasing rather than strictly increasing in each argument, a minimizer of miscoding need not itself be Pareto optimal.

The following proposition establishes that a Pareto-optimal minimizer nevertheless always exists.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:chebyshev_minimizer_pareto}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity such that $\mathcal R_e\neq\varnothing$. Then there exists $r^\star\in\mathcal P_e$ satisfying
\[
\mu(e,r^\star) = \min_{r\in\mathcal R_e}\mu(e,r).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\mathcal R_e$ is nonempty and miscoding takes values in $\mathbb N_0$, the set
\[
\{\mu(e,r):r\in\mathcal R_e\}
\]
has a minimum, denoted by $\mu_{\min}$. Let
\[
\mathcal M_e = \{r\in\mathcal R_e:\mu(e,r)=\mu_{\min}\}
\]
be the nonempty set of miscoding minimizers. Choose $r^\star\in\mathcal M_e$ minimizing
\[
d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r)
\]
over $\mathcal M_e$. Such a minimizer exists because the sum takes nonnegative integer values. Suppose, for contradiction, that $r^\star$ is dominated. Then there exists $r'\in\mathcal R_e$ such that $d_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r^\star)$ and $s_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r^\star)$, with at least one strict inequality. By the monotonicity of the maximum,
\[
\mu(e,r')\le\mu(e,r^\star)=\mu_{\min}.
\]
Since $\mu_{\min}$ is the minimum achievable miscoding, we must have
\[
\mu(e,r')=\mu_{\min},
\]
and therefore $r'\in\mathcal M_e$. However, the strict dominance implies
\[
d_R(e,r')+s_R(e,r') < d_R(e,r^\star)+s_R(e,r^\star),
\]
contradicting the choice of $r^\star$. Hence $r^\star\in\mathcal P_e$, and it minimizes miscoding over $\mathcal R_e$.
\end{proof}

Thus, Chebyshev scalarization always admits a Pareto-optimal minimizer. When several representations attain the same minimum miscoding, selecting one with minimum total deficiency and surplus guarantees Pareto optimality. Not every Chebyshev minimizer need be Pareto optimal, because improving the smaller component may leave the maximum unchanged. The distinction matters when a unique or explicitly non-dominated solution must be selected.

A useful property of the Chebyshev scalarization is that it provides a uniform upper bound on both deficiency and surplus.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:chebyshev_sublevel_sets}
For every $r\in\mathcal R_e$ and every $t\ge 0$,
\[
\mu(e,r)\le t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_R(e,r)\le t \text{ and } s_R(e,r)\le t.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition,
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\}.
\]
Hence $\mu(e,r)\le t$ if and only if the maximum of the two quantities is at most $t$, which happens if and only if each quantity is at most $t$.
\end{proof}

This equivalence is one of the main reasons to prefer the maximum. Minimizing miscoding is equivalent to finding the smallest common threshold under which both deficiency and surplus can be bounded.

The maximum also identifies which of the two components is currently the main obstacle to improvement.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bottleneck_principle_section}
Let $r,r'\in\mathcal R_e$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $d_R(e,r)>s_R(e,r)$, then any $r'$ satisfying $d_R(e,r') < d_R(e,r)$ and $s_R(e,r') \le d_R(e,r')$ necessarily satisfies
    \[
    \mu(e,r')<\mu(e,r).
    \]
    \item If $s_R(e,r)>d_R(e,r)$, then any $r'$ satisfying $s_R(e,r') < s_R(e,r)$ and $d_R(e,r') \le s_R(e,r')$ necessarily satisfies
    \[
    \mu(e,r')<\mu(e,r).
    \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Assume first that $d_R(e,r)>s_R(e,r)$. Then $\mu(e,r)=d_R(e,r)$. If $r'$ satisfies $d_R(e,r') < d_R(e,r)$ and 
$s_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r')$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(e,r')
& = \max\{d_R(e,r'),s_R(e,r')\} \\
& = d_R(e,r') < d_R(e,r) = \mu(e,r).
\end{aligned}
\]
The second part is symmetric. If $s_R(e,r)>d_R(e,r)$, then $\mu(e,r)=s_R(e,r)$. If $s_R(e,r')<s_R(e,r)$ and $d_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r')$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(e,r')
& = \max\{d_R(e,r'),s_R(e,r')\} \\
& = s_R(e,r') < s_R(e,r) = \mu(e,r).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition identifies the dominant representational defect and provides sufficient conditions for decreasing miscoding. When deficiency dominates, the priority is to reduce missing information; when surplus dominates, the priority is to eliminate extraneous information.

The main advantage of the Chebyshev scalarization is that it minimizes the worst of the two representational defects. In particular, among representations with the same total deficiency and surplus, it favors those with a more balanced distribution of the two components. The next proposition formalizes this property.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:max_avoids_extremes}
Let $r_1,r_2\in\mathcal R_e$. Suppose that
\[
d_R(e,r_1)+s_R(e,r_1) = d_R(e,r_2)+s_R(e,r_2) = C
\]
for some constant $C$, and assume moreover that
\[
|d_R(e,r_1)-s_R(e,r_1)| \le |d_R(e,r_2)-s_R(e,r_2)|.
\]
Then
\[
\mu(e,r_1)\le\mu(e,r_2).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any nonnegative numbers $a,b$ with $a+b=C$, one has
\[
\max\{a,b\} = \frac{C+|a-b|}{2}.
\]
Applying this identity to the pairs $(a,b) = (d_R(e,r_1),s_R(e,r_1))$ and $(a,b) = (d_R(e,r_2),s_R(e,r_2))$ we obtain
\[
\mu(e,r_1) = \frac{ C+|d_R(e,r_1)-s_R(e,r_1)| }{2}
\]
and
\[
\mu(e,r_2) = \frac{ C+|d_R(e,r_2)-s_R(e,r_2)| }{2}.
\]
Since
\[
|d_R(e,r_1)-s_R(e,r_1)| \le |d_R(e,r_2)-s_R(e,r_2)|,
\]
it follows that
\[
\mu(e,r_1)\le\mu(e,r_2).
\]
\end{proof}

Among representations with the same total burden, the maximum prefers the more balanced one. This preference helps avoid representations in which one component is unnecessarily large relative to the other, although balance is not guaranteed when total burdens differ.

A final basic property is that the Chebyshev scalarization is the smallest uniform upper bound on the two representational defects.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:max_smallest_common_bound}
For every $r\in\mathcal R_e$, $\mu(e,r)$ is the smallest number $t\ge 0$ such that $d_R(e,r) \le t$ and $s_R(e,r)\le t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:chebyshev_sublevel_sets},
\[
\mu(e,r)\le t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_R(e,r)\le t \text{ and } s_R(e,r)\le t.
\]
Taking $t=\mu(e,r)$ shows that $\mu(e,r)$ is a common upper bound. If $t<\mu(e,r)$, then the equivalence above
implies that at least one of $d_R(e,r)$ or $s_R(e,r)$ is greater than $t$. Hence no smaller common upper bound exists.
\end{proof}

This characterization gives the maximum a clear operational meaning. It measures the least tolerance level under which both kinds of representational error can simultaneously be regarded as acceptable.

% Subsection: Alternative scalarizations

\subsection*{Alternative scalarizations}

The maximum is not the only possible way to combine deficiency and surplus into a single quantity. Different scalarizations express different preferences regarding the relative importance of the two components, and may therefore select different representations from the same feasible set.

Of particular interest are scalarizations that preserve the multiobjective structure while providing a smoother optimization criterion. Such alternatives may be advantageous in computational applications (see Chapter \ref{chap:mnplib}), where the objective is not only to evaluate representations but also to search for better ones.

A general family of scalarizations is given by the weighted power mean:
\[
\mu^{(p,\lambda)}(e,r) = \left(\lambda\,d_R(e,r)^p + (1-\lambda)\,s_R(e,r)^p \right)^{1/p},
\]
where $p\ge 1$ and $0<\lambda<1$. The parameter $p$ controls the sensitivity to the larger component, while $\lambda$ determines the relative importance assigned to deficiency and surplus. Several familiar scalarizations arise as special cases.

For $p=1$ and $\lambda=1/2$, we obtain the arithmetic mean:
\[
\mu^{(1,1/2)}(e,r) = \frac{d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r)}{2}.
\]
This criterion measures the average representational defect. It is simple, symmetric, and strictly increasing in both components. However, it permits compensation between them: a large deficiency may be offset by a small surplus, and vice versa.

For $p=2$ and $\lambda=1/2$, we obtain the root mean square:
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{RMS}}(e,r) = \sqrt{ \frac{ d_R(e,r)^2+s_R(e,r)^2}{2} }.
\]
The RMS penalizes large components more heavily than the arithmetic mean, while remaining sensitive to improvements in either component. Its squared form provides a smooth quadratic objective, which is particularly convenient for numerical optimization.

More generally, choosing $\lambda\neq 1/2$ gives the weighted RMS:
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{WRMS}}_\lambda(e,r) = \sqrt{\lambda\,d_R(e,r)^2 + (1-\lambda)\,s_R(e,r)^2 }.
\]
This allows one type of representational defect to receive greater importance than the other. Such flexibility may be useful when the consequences of missing information and extraneous information differ substantially.

The relationship between these alternatives and the maximum can be established through a general bound.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:miscoding_power_mean_bounds}
Let $a,b\ge 0$, $p\ge 1$, and $0<\lambda<1$. Define
\[
M_{p,\lambda}(a,b) = \left(\lambda a^p+(1-\lambda)b^p \right)^{1/p}.
\]
Then
\[
\min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}^{1/p} \max\{a,b\} \le M_{p,\lambda}(a,b) \le \max\{a,b\}.
\]
Moreover, for fixed $\lambda$,
\[
\lim_{p\to\infty}M_{p,\lambda}(a,b) = \max\{a,b\}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $m=\max\{a,b\}$. Since $a\le m$ and $b\le m$,
\[
\lambda a^p+(1-\lambda)b^p \le \lambda m^p+(1-\lambda)m^p = m^p.
\]
Taking the $p$-th root gives
\[
M_{p,\lambda}(a,b)\le m.
\]
For the lower bound, at least one of $a$ or $b$ equals $m$. Its corresponding weight is at least $\min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}$. Therefore,
\[
\lambda a^p+(1-\lambda)b^p \ge \min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}m^p,
\]
and hence
\[
M_{p,\lambda}(a,b) \ge \min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}^{1/p}m.
\]
Finally, since
\[
\lim_{p\to\infty} \min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}^{1/p} = 1,
\]
the result follows by the squeeze theorem.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that the maximum is the limiting case of the weighted power-mean family. As $p$ increases, the scalarization becomes progressively more sensitive to the larger component, approaching the Chebyshev criterion. In particular, for equal weights, the arithmetic mean, RMS, and maximum satisfy
\[
\frac{d_R(e,r)+s_R(e,r)}{2} \le \mu^{\mathrm{RMS}}(e,r) \le \mu(e,r).
\]
The three criteria therefore differ not in the components they evaluate, but in how strongly they penalize an unbalanced distribution of representational error.

A useful distinction concerns their behavior under Pareto dominance. Unlike the maximum, finite-order weighted power means are strictly increasing in both components.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:miscoding_power_mean_pareto}
Let $p\ge 1$ and $0<\lambda<1$. If $r\in\mathcal R_e$ minimizes $\mu^{(p,\lambda)}(e,r)$ over $\mathcal R_e$,
then $r$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $r$ minimizes $\mu^{(p,\lambda)}$ but is not Pareto optimal. Then there exists $r'\in\mathcal R_e$ such that $d_R(e,r')\le d_R(e,r)$ and $s_R(e,r')\le s_R(e,r)$, with at least one strict inequality. Since $p\ge 1$ and both weights are strictly positive, it follows that
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\lambda d_R(e,r')^p + (1-\lambda)s_R(e,r')^p \\
&\quad<\lambda d_R(e,r)^p+(1-\lambda)s_R(e,r)^p.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking the $p$-th root preserves the strict inequality:
\[
\mu^{(p,\lambda)}(e,r') < \mu^{(p,\lambda)}(e,r).
\]
This contradicts the minimality of $r$. Therefore $r$ is Pareto optimal.
\end{proof}

This result provides an advantage of finite-order power means: every minimizer is Pareto optimal. By contrast, the maximum may assign equal values to two representations even when one dominates the other, because improving the smaller component does not necessarily change the scalar score.

This difference is also relevant for numerical optimization. The maximum has flat regions with respect to changes in the non-dominant component. RMS, on the other hand, responds to improvements in either component and has a differentiable objective surface away from the origin. In particular, minimizing weighted RMS is equivalent to minimizing its square:
\[
\lambda d_R(e,r)^2 + (1-\lambda)s_R(e,r)^2.
\]
When the component measures admit differentiable approximations, this quadratic form provides a smooth objective suitable for gradient-based optimization. The exact Kolmogorov-complexity quantities remain discrete and generally non-computable, so this computational advantage concerns their practical approximations rather than the theoretical measures themselves. Nevertheless, the greater sensitivity of RMS to both components also entails a different preference regarding trade-offs.

\begin{example}
Consider the hypothetical objective pairs $(5,0)$ and $(4,4)$. The maximum prefers the second pair:
\[
\max\{5,0\}=5>4=\max\{4,4\}.
\]
By contrast, RMS prefers the first:
\[
\sqrt{\frac{25}{2}}<4.
\]
Thus, RMS may prefer a representation with a larger dominant defect when its overall quadratic burden is smaller. The maximum instead prioritizes reducing the worst component, independently of the total burden.
\end{example}

This distinction is important for miscoding. Deficiency and surplus represent two complementary failures in encoding an entity: missing information and extraneous information. A representation with a very large value of either component remains seriously defective, even when the other component is small. The maximum captures this bottleneck interpretation directly.

\section{Relative Miscoding}
\label{sec:relative_miscoding}

The quantity $\mu(e,r)$ measures the miscoding of an extended representation $r\in\mathcal R_e$ in absolute terms, that is, in bits. This absolute measure is appropriate for the theoretical framework developed in the previous sections. However, one may also wish to compare the miscoding of representations of different complexities, or even representations associated with different entities. For this purpose, it is useful to introduce a normalized, dimensionless version of miscoding. The idea is to divide absolute miscoding by a complexity scale intrinsic to both the representation and the entity, that is, the admissible representational complexity floor $\bar\kappa_e$. We use this quantity together with the complexity of the representation to define relative miscoding.

\begin{definition}
\index{Relative miscoding}
\label{def:relative_miscoding}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be an entity admitting admissible representations, and let $r\in\mathcal R_e$ be an extended representation of $e$. We define the \emph{relative miscoding} of $r$ with respect to $e$ as
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) = \frac{\mu(e,r)} {\max\{K(r),\bar\kappa_e\}}
= \frac{\max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\}}{\max\{K(r),\bar\kappa_e\}}.
\]
\end{definition}

The denominator provides a common normalization scale for deficiency and surplus. Deficiency is bounded by the admissible complexity floor of the entity, whereas surplus is bounded by the complexity of the representation itself. Taking the maximum of these two quantities therefore provides a scale appropriate for both components.

\begin{notation}
For convenience, we write
\[
C_e(r):=\max\{K(r),\bar\kappa_e\},
\]
so that
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r)=\frac{\mu(e,r)}{C_e(r)}.
\]
The denominator is positive for the nontrivial representations considered here.
\end{notation}

The first property establishes that relative miscoding
is bounded approximately between zero and one.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:range_rel_miscoding}
For every $r\in\mathcal R_e$,
\[
0 \le \mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) \le 1 + O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity follows directly from $d_R(e,r)\ge 0$ and $s_R(e,r)\ge 0$.

By the upper bounds established for deficiency and surplus,
\[
d_R(e,r)\le\bar\kappa_e+O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,r)\le K(r)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(e,r)
&=\max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\}\\
&\le\max\{\bar\kappa_e,K(r)\}+O(1)\\
&=C_e(r)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Dividing by $C_e(r)$ gives
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r)
\le
1+O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that relative miscoding behaves as a normalized measure of representational error. The additive term becomes negligible as the intrinsic complexity scale increases. In particular, along any family of representations for which $C_e(r)\to\infty$, the upper bound approaches one.

The next proposition relates relative miscoding to the theoretical ideal of admissible representations.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:perfect_rel_miscoding}
Let $r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e\cap\mathcal R_e$ be an admissible extended representation of $e$. Then
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) = O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $r$ is admissible, the corresponding properties of deficiency and surplus give $d_R(e,r)=O(1)$ and $s_R(e,r)=O(1)$. Therefore,
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\} = O(1).
\]
By the definition of relative miscoding,
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) = \frac{\mu(e,r)}{C_e(r)} = O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proof}

Thus, admissible representations have negligible relative miscoding when their intrinsic complexity scale is sufficiently large. More generally, any representation with negligible absolute miscoding has relative miscoding of order $O(1/C_e(r))$. The converse, however, does not hold in general. A representation may have substantial absolute miscoding while its relative miscoding is small because the complexity scale is much larger.

The following proposition formalizes this distinction.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:small_rel_not_small_abs}
There exist families of entities $e_n$ and extended representations $r_n\in\mathcal R_{e_n}$ such that
\[
\mu(e_n,r_n)\to\infty
\]
while
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e_n,r_n)\to 0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For each positive integer $n$, choose a binary string $u_n$ such that $K(u_n)=\Theta(n^2)$. The strings may be chosen to begin with $0$ and to have distinct lengths. Consider a fixed representation oracle that decodes $u_n$ as an entity $e_n$, and no other string as $e_n$. Consequently, $\bar{\mathcal R}_{e_n}=\{u_n\}$ and $\bar\kappa_{e_n}=K(u_n)=\Theta(n^2)$. Choose a binary string $z_n$ of length $n$ that is incompressible relative to $u_n$, so that
\[
K(z_n\mid u_n)=n+O(\log(n+2)).
\]
Construct $r_n = 1\langle u_n,z_n\rangle$, where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denotes a fixed computable, self-delimiting encoding of pairs. Since $u_n$ can be recovered from $r_n$ by a fixed computable procedure $K(u_n\mid r_n)=O(1)$. Thus $r_n$ contains nontrivial information about $e_n$ and belongs to $\mathcal R_{e_n}$
for sufficiently large $n$. Its deficiency satisfies $d_R(e_n,r_n)=O(1)$. Since $u_n$ is the only admissible representation of $e_n$, its surplus is
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_R(e_n,r_n)
&=K(r_n\mid u_n)\\
&=K(z_n\mid u_n)+O(1)\\
&=\Theta(n).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\mu(e_n,r_n)=\Theta(n).
\]

On the other hand, the recoverability of $u_n$ from $r_n$, together with the size of the construction, gives $K(r_n)=\Theta(n^2)$. Therefore,
\[
C_{e_n}(r_n) = \max\{K(r_n),\bar\kappa_{e_n}\} = \Theta(n^2).
\]
It follows that
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e_n,r_n) = \frac{\Theta(n)}{\Theta(n^2)} = \Theta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \longrightarrow 0,
\]
while
\[
\mu(e_n,r_n)=\Theta(n)\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
\end{proof}

This proposition establishes that relative and absolute miscoding measure different aspects of representational quality. A small relative value indicates that the dominant defect is small compared with the intrinsic complexity scale, but does not imply that the absolute amount of missing or extraneous information is negligible.

A further desirable property is stability under computable changes of encoding. Since representations are finite binary strings, their particular encoding convention should not substantially affect their normalized miscoding when the transformation preserves their algorithmic information.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:rel_miscoding_recoding}
Let $f:\mathcal B^\ast\to\mathcal B^\ast$ be a fixed total computable bijection with computable inverse. Suppose that $r,f(r)\in\mathcal R_e$, and that the representation oracle and admissible reference set remain fixed. Then
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,f(r)) = \mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $f$ and its inverse are computable by fixed programs, for every $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ we have
\[
K(\bar r\mid f(r)) = K(\bar r\mid r)+O(1)
\]
and
\[
K(f(r)\mid\bar r) = K(r\mid\bar r)+O(1).
\]
The additive constants are independent of the particular admissible representation $\bar r$. Taking minima over $\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ gives
\[
d_R(e,f(r))=d_R(e,r)+O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,f(r))=s_R(e,r)+O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\mu(e,f(r))=\mu(e,r)+O(1).
\]
Furthermore, computable invertibility implies
\[
K(f(r))=K(r)+O(1).
\]
Since $\bar\kappa_e$ is unchanged,
\[
C_e(f(r))=C_e(r)+O(1).
\]
Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,f(r))
& = \frac{\mu(e,r)+O(1)}{C_e(r)+O(1)}\\
& = \frac{\mu(e,r)}{C_e(r)} + O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the final equality uses
$\mu(e,r)\le C_e(r)+O(1)$. Hence,
\[
\mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,f(r)) = \mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r) + O\left(\frac{1}{C_e(r)}\right).
\]
\end{proof}

This result shows that relative miscoding is stable, up to an asymptotically negligible correction, under fixed computable and reversible changes of encoding. It therefore depends primarily on the algorithmic information carried by the representation rather than on its particular syntactic form.

An important consequence of normalization concerns the ordering of representations. If two representations $r_1,r_2\in\mathcal R_e$ have the same complexity scale $C_e(r_1)=C_e(r_2)$, then
\[
\mu(e,r_1)\le\mu(e,r_2) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r_1) \le \mu^{\mathrm{rel}}(e,r_2).
\]
Thus, relative miscoding preserves the ordering induced by absolute miscoding when the normalization scales coincide. When the scales differ, however, their rankings need not agree. In particular, normalization may favor a representation with greater absolute deficiency or surplus simply because its complexity scale is substantially larger. Consequently, minimizing relative miscoding is not, in general, equivalent to minimizing absolute miscoding or to selecting a representation from the original Pareto frontier.

Relative miscoding also preserves the bottleneck interpretation of absolute miscoding. Since both deficiency and surplus are divided by the same positive denominator, whichever component dominates the absolute miscoding also dominates the relative miscoding.

\section{Decreasing Miscoding}
\label{sec:decreasing_miscoding}

For a fixed entity $e$, miscoding measures how far an extended representation $r\in\mathcal R_e$ is from being simultaneously informationally complete and free of extraneous content. These two requirements are measured separately by deficiency and surplus. Consequently, decreasing miscoding involves reducing missing information about the entity while avoiding the introduction of unnecessary information.

Two elementary operations on representations were introduced in Chapter \ref{chap:Representations}: augmentation, which adds information, and reduction, which removes it. Augmentation is primarily intended to decrease deficiency, whereas reduction is primarily intended to decrease surplus. Neither operation, however, guarantees a decrease in miscoding, since improving one component may worsen the other.

% Subsection: Decreasing Deficiency via Augmentation

\subsection*{Decreasing Deficiency via Augmentation}

A natural way to decrease deficiency is to augment a representation with additional information. In scientific practice, this may correspond to collecting new observations, increasing measurement resolution, or introducing previously missing variables. If the additional information is relevant to the entity, it may reduce the information required to recover an admissible representation.

Recall that augmentation is defined by appending a binary string to an existing representation. The following proposition establishes an upper bound on the deficiency of the augmented representation.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decrease_deficiency_augmentation}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, let $r\in\mathcal R_e$, and let $a\in\mathcal B^\ast$ be such that $ra\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
d_R(e,ra) \le d_R(e,r) + K(r\mid ra) + O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
d_R(e,ra) \le d_R(e,r) + O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the conditional complexity bound established in Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_triangle},
\[
d_R(e,ra) \le d_R(e,r) + K(r\mid ra) + O(1).
\]
Given $ra$, the original representation $r$ can be recovered by specifying its length $\ell(r)$ and extracting the corresponding prefix. Therefore,
\[
K(r\mid ra) \le K(\ell(r))+O(1) = O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
Substituting this bound establishes the result.
\end{proof}

Thus, augmentation cannot increase deficiency beyond the information required to recover the original representation from the augmented one. In particular, if $K(r\mid ra)=O(1)$, the increase is bounded by a constant.

However, this result does not guarantee that augmentation decreases deficiency. The additional information may be redundant, irrelevant, or insufficient to improve the representation. To quantify the effect of a particular augmentation, we introduce the following measure.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:marginal_deficiency_reduction}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, let $r\in\mathcal R_e$, and let $a\in\mathcal B^\ast$ satisfy $ra\in\mathcal R_e$. We define the \emph{marginal deficiency reduction} of augmenting $r$ by $a$ as
\[
\Delta_R^d(e,r,a) = d_R(e,r) - d_R(e,ra).
\]
\end{definition}

The quantity $\Delta_R^d(e,r,a)$ measures the decrease in missing algorithmic information resulting from the augmentation. A positive value indicates an improvement in deficiency, whereas a negative value indicates that deficiency has increased. The quantity need not bepositive, although any increase is subject to the bound
established above.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the complete interaction network of a biological signaling pathway. The current representation $r$ contains a subset of the relevant molecular interactions obtained from existing experiments. Researchers may augment this representation in different ways. One possibility is to repeat measurements of well-characterized interactions at higher precision. Another is to investigate interactions involving a regulatory protein whose role in the pathway remains poorly understood. If the second experiment reveals previously unknown interactions, its results may supply information needed to recover a more complete representation of the network. In that case, its marginal deficiency reduction may be greater than that obtained by repeating measurements of already well-characterized interactions. The most informative augmentation is therefore not necessarily the one that produces the largest amount of additional data, but the one that provides the greatest reduction in missing information.
\end{example}

Marginal deficiency reduction provides a criterion for comparing alternative augmentations. However, a decrease in deficiency does not necessarily imply a decrease in miscoding, since the additional information may also increase surplus.

% Subsection: Decreasing Surplus via Reduction

\subsection*{Decreasing Surplus via Reduction}

A complementary way to improve a representation is to remove information that is not genuinely attributable to the entity. In scientific practice, this may involve discarding irrelevant variables, eliminating extraneous metadata, or removing information that introduces spurious regularities.

Recall that reduction, as defined in Chapter \ref{chap:Representations}, removes a suffix from a representation. The resulting string need not remain an extended representation of the original entity. We therefore restrict the following analysis to reductions that preserve membership in $\mathcal R_e$.

The next proposition establishes an upper bound on the surplus of a reduced representation.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decrease_surplus_reduction}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $r,a\in\mathcal B^\ast$ be such that $r,ra\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,ra) + K(r\mid ra) + O(1).
\]
Consequently,
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,ra) + O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the conditional complexity bound for surplus, established in Proposition~\ref{prop:surplus_triangle},
\[
s_R(e,r) \le s_R(e,ra) + K(r\mid ra) + O(1).
\]
As in the case of augmentation, $r$ can be recovered from $ra$ by specifying its length and extracting the corresponding prefix. Hence,
\[
K(r\mid ra) = O(\log(\ell(r)+2)).
\]
Substituting this bound establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that reduction cannot increase surplus by more than the information required to identify the retained representation. When the reduced string can be recovered by a fixed computable procedure, the increase is bounded by $O(1)$. Nevertheless, reduction may discard information that is essential for representing the entity. In particular, the corresponding deficiency satisfies
\[
d_R(e,r)
\le
d_R(e,ra)+K(a\mid r)+O(1).
\]
Thus, removing a suffix may decrease surplus while increasing deficiency.

We now quantify the reduction in surplus obtained by removing a particular component.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:marginal_surplus_reduction}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $r,a\in\mathcal B^\ast$ satisfy $r,ra\in\mathcal R_e$. We define the \emph{marginal surplus reduction} obtained by removing $a$ from $ra$ as
\[
\Delta_R^s(e,r,a) = s_R(e,ra) - s_R(e,r).
\]
\end{definition}

A positive value of $\Delta_R^s(e,r,a)$ indicates that removing the suffix decreases surplus. A negative value indicates that the reduced representation has greater surplus than the original one. The measure therefore quantifies the effect of reduction without assuming that the operation is necessarily beneficial.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the relationship between the morphological characteristics of plants and their species identity. An extended representation may consist of a dataset containing measurements such as petal length, petal width, and leaf shape, together with additional administrative information. Suppose the dataset contains a field identifying the technician responsible for recording each observation, and that this information is independent of the biological characteristics being represented. Removing this field may decrease surplus without substantially increasing deficiency. By contrast, removing petal length may eliminate information relevant to the entity, even if the resulting dataset is shorter. The preferable reduction is therefore not necessarily the one that removes the greatest amount of information, but the one that eliminates extraneous content while preserving information relevant to the entity.
\end{example}

Marginal surplus reduction provides a criterion for evaluating alternative simplifications. However, a decrease in surplus alone does not guarantee a decrease in miscoding, since the removed information may also have contributed to reducing deficiency.

% Subsection: Strict Decrease of Miscoding

\subsection*{Strict Decrease of Miscoding}

The preceding results establish how augmentation and reduction may affect the individual components of miscoding. However, the ultimate objective is to decrease their maximum:
\[
\mu(e,r) = \max\{d_R(e,r),s_R(e,r)\}.
\]
To measure representational improvement independently of the transformation employed, we introduce a general measure of miscoding reduction.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:miscoding_reduction}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $r,r'\in\mathcal R_e$ be two extended representations of $e$. We define the \emph{miscoding reduction} from $r$ to $r'$ as
\[
\Delta_\mu(e;r,r') = \mu(e,r) - \mu(e,r').
\]
\end{definition}

A positive value of $\Delta_\mu(e;r,r')$ indicates that the new representation has lower miscoding. A negative value indicates a deterioration, while zero indicates that miscoding remains unchanged. The definition applies to any representational transformation, including augmentation, reduction, and joint representation. As established by the properties of the Chebyshev scalarization, a strict decrease in miscoding occurs precisely when both components of the new representation fall below the previous miscoding level:
\[
\mu(e,r') < \mu(e,r)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
d_R(e,r')<\mu(e,r),\\
s_R(e,r')<\mu(e,r).
\end{cases}
\]
This condition explains why decreasing only one component is not always sufficient. If deficiency is smaller than surplus, a further reduction in deficiency may leave miscoding unchanged because surplus remains the dominant component. The symmetric situation occurs when surplus is smaller than deficiency.

Consequently, the component that currently determines miscoding is generally the natural target for improvement. When deficiency dominates, augmentation may be appropriate, provided that surplus remains below the previous miscoding level. When surplus dominates, reduction may be preferable, provided that deficiency remains sufficiently small. When deficiency and surplus are equal, both components must decrease for miscoding to decrease strictly. This may require combining augmentation and reduction rather than applying either operation independently.

A strict decrease in miscoding does not necessarily require Pareto dominance. One component may increase while the other decreases sufficiently to reduce the maximum. Thus, the Chebyshev criterion permits trade-offs between deficiency and surplus while prioritizing the reduction of the dominant defect.

% Subsection: Decreasing Miscoding via Joint Representations 

\subsection*{Decreasing Miscoding via Joint Representations}

An additional approach to decreasing miscoding consists of combining complementary representations of the same entity. Such combinations are common in scientific practice, for example when measurements from different instruments, experiments, or observational methods are brought together.

Recall from Chapter \ref{chap:Representations} that the joint representation of two extended representations $s$ and $t$ is their concatenation $st$. Joint representation is therefore a particular form of augmentation, distinguished by the fact that both components are themselves extended representations. When the components contain complementary information, their combination may reduce deficiency more than either component alone. However, the combination may also introduce additional surplus.

The first proposition bounds the deficiency of a joint representation in terms of its components.

\begin{proposition}[Deficiency of joint representations]
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $s,t\in\mathcal R_e$ be extended representations such that $st\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
d_R(e,st) \le \min\{d_R(e,s),d_R(e,t)\} + O(\log(\ell(st)+2)).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:deficiency_triangle},
\[
d_R(e,st) \le d_R(e,s) + K(s\mid st)+O(1).
\]
The component $s$ can be recovered from $st$ by specifying its length and extracting the corresponding prefix. Therefore,
\[
K(s\mid st) = O(\log(\ell(st)+2)),
\]
and hence
\[
d_R(e,st) \le d_R(e,s) + O(\log(\ell(st)+2)).
\]
Similarly, the component $t$ can be recovered from $st$ by specifying its length and extracting the corresponding suffix. Therefore,
\[
d_R(e,st) \le d_R(e,t) + O(\log(\ell(st)+2)).
\]
Taking the smaller of the two upper bounds establishes the result.
\end{proof}

This proposition shows that a joint representation preserves the informational contribution of each component up to the overhead required to recover it. When the components can be recovered by fixed computable procedures, the logarithmic overhead can be replaced by $O(1)$. In particular, combining complementary representations may substantially reduce deficiency, although the result does not guarantee a strict improvement.

The corresponding effect on surplus is different. The joint representation may contain information that is not recoverable from an admissible reference, even when both components individually contain genuine information about the entity. The following proposition provides an upper bound on the additional surplus introduced by joining two representations.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:decrease_joint_surplus}
Let $e\in\mathcal E$ be a knowable entity, and let $s,t\in\mathcal R_e$ satisfy $st\in\mathcal R_e$. Then
\[
s_R(e,st) \le s_R(e,s) + K(t\mid s) + O(1).
\]
Symmetrically,
\[
s_R(e,st) \le s_R(e,t) + K(s\mid t) + O(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e$ be arbitrary. Given $\bar r$, one may first reconstruct $s$ using a shortest conditional program, then reconstruct $t$ from $s$, and finally concatenate the two strings. By the composition property of conditional Kolmogorov complexity,
\[
K(st\mid\bar r) \le K(s\mid\bar r) + K(t\mid s) + O(1).
\]
Taking the minimum over all admissible representations gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_R(e,st)
& = \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} K(st\mid\bar r)\\
& \le \min_{\bar r\in\bar{\mathcal R}_e} \bigl(K(s\mid\bar r)+K(t\mid s)+O(1) \bigr)\\
& = s_R(e,s)+K(t\mid s)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
The symmetric inequality follows by first reconstructing $t$ from the admissible representation and then reconstructing $s$ from $t$.
\end{proof}

This proposition bounds the surplus of a joint representation by the surplus of one component and the additional information required to reconstruct the other component. The term $K(t\mid s)$ measures additional
algorithmic information, not necessarily extraneous information. Some of this information may be relevant to the entity and contribute to decreasing deficiency.

A particularly important case occurs when one component is already recoverable from the other at constant algorithmic cost.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:decrease_joint_redundancy}
Let $s,t,st\in\mathcal R_e$ satisfy $K(t\mid s)=O(1)$ and $K(s\mid st)=O(1)$. Then
\[
\mu(e,st)=\mu(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Since $K(t\mid s)=O(1)$, the concatenated string $st$ can be reconstructed from $s$ at constant algorithmic cost $K(st\mid s)=O(1)$. Together with $K(s\mid st)=O(1)$, this shows that $s$ and $st$ are algorithmically
equivalent. By the invariance of deficiency and surplus under algorithmic equivalence,
\[
d_R(e,st)=d_R(e,s)+O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,st)=s_R(e,s)+O(1).
\]
Taking the maximum gives
\[
\mu(e,st)=\mu(e,s)+O(1).
\]
\end{proof}

Thus, joining a component whose information is already recoverable from the original representation cannot produce an unbounded improvement in miscoding under the stated recoverability conditions. Substantial improvements must instead arise from information that was not previously available and that contributes to reducing the dominant representational defect without introducing excessive surplus.

\begin{example}
Consider an entity $e$ corresponding to the spatial distribution of vegetation and wetlands in a coastal region. Two different observational methods provide partial representations of this entity. The first representation $s$ contains optical satellite measurements, which provide information about surface reflectance and vegetation characteristics. However, these observations may be incomplete in areas affected by clouds. The second representation $t$ contains radar measurements, which provide complementary information about surface structure and moisture under conditions in which optical measurements are unavailable. Both representations may contain genuine information about the same entity while leaving different aspects insufficiently characterized. Their joint representation $st$ may reduce deficiency by combining the complementary information provided by the two instruments. However, this improvement does not depend merely on the quantity of additional data. If the radar representation includes substantial information unrelated to the target entity, the resulting surplus may increase. The joint representation decreases miscoding only when the reduction in the dominant defect is
sufficient to compensate for any deterioration in the other component.
\end{example}

The analysis extends to finite collections of extended representations. Given $r_1,\ldots,r_n\in\mathcal R_e$, one may construct the joint string $r_1r_2\cdots r_n$, provided that the resulting string is also an extended representation of $e$. The preceding bounds can be applied repeatedly to study the combined information. Recovering individual components may require additional boundary information unless a fixed self-delimiting
encoding convention is used.

\section{Miscoding of Effective Representations}
\label{sec:effective_miscoding}

The theory of miscoding provides a mathematical framework for evaluating the quality of representations of an entity. However, its direct application requires knowledge of the entity being represented, or at least access to its admissible representations. In scientific practice, neither may be available. Researchers frequently construct and refine representations of phenomena whose underlying nature remains unknown.

Consider a fixed but unidentified entity $e\in\mathcal E$ and a time-ordered sequence of effective representations
\[
r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n,\ldots
\]
that researchers consider to represent $e$. The identity of $e$ is unknown, but we assume that all representations are intended to describe the same entity. As discussed in Chapter \ref{chap:Representations}, effective representations are those considered in scientific practice. They need not necessarily be extended representations in the theoretical sense. Consequently, although $r_i\in\hat{\mathcal R}_e$, we cannot generally assume that $r_i\in\mathcal R_e$. If the effective representations are also extended representations, their theoretical miscoding is well-defined:
\[
\mu(e,r_i)
=
\max\{d_R(e,r_i),s_R(e,r_i)\}.
\]
The fact that $e$ is unidentified does not alter this definition. Its identity need not be known to researchers for miscoding to exist as a mathematical quantity. However, without knowledge of the admissible representations of $e$, the quantities defining miscoding are generally inaccessible.

The problem is therefore not necessarily to determine the absolute miscoding of each representation. A more modest and potentially useful objective is to establish whether successive representations improve:
\[
\mu(e,r_{i+1})<\mu(e,r_i).
\]
Such a comparison would provide evidence of decreasing miscoding without requiring the entity to be identified or either miscoding value to be calculated.

% Subsection: Comparison of successive representations

\subsection*{Comparison of Successive Representations}

Some properties of representational refinement can be established without knowing the underlying entity. As shown in the preceding sections, for any two extended representations $r_i,r_j\in\mathcal R_e$,
\[
d_R(e,r_j) \le d_R(e,r_i) + K(r_i\mid r_j) + O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,r_j) \le s_R(e,r_i) + K(r_j\mid r_i) + O(1).
\]
These inequalities depend on the algorithmic relationship between the representations, rather than on the identity of the entity. They therefore remain valid when $e$ is unknown. In particular, if $K(r_i\mid r_j)=O(1)$, then
\[
d_R(e,r_j)\le d_R(e,r_i)+O(1).
\]
Thus, a new representation from which the previous one can be recovered at constant algorithmic cost cannot substantially increase deficiency. Similarly, if $K(r_j\mid r_i)=O(1)$, then
\[
s_R(e,r_j)\le s_R(e,r_i)+O(1).
\]
If both conditional complexities are $O(1)$, the two representations are algorithmically equivalent, and their miscoding differs by at most an additive constant:
\[
\mu(e,r_j)=\mu(e,r_i)+O(1).
\]
These results constrain changes in miscoding without requiring identification of the underlying entity. However, they do not generally establish whether miscoding has decreased. Additional information may reduce deficiency, increase surplus, or affect both components simultaneously.

% Subsection: Cumulative Represenations

\subsection*{Cumulative Representations}

An alternative is to consider the cumulative information provided by all effective representations available
up to a given time. Define
\[
R_n=\langle r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n\rangle,
\]
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes a fixed computable encoding from which the individual components can be recovered. Assume that $R_n,R_{n+1}\in\mathcal R_e$. Since $R_n$ can be recovered from $R_{n+1}$ by a fixed computable procedure, the preceding inequalities yield
\[
d_R(e,R_{n+1}) \le d_R(e,R_n) + O(1)
\]
and
\[
s_R(e,R_n) \le s_R(e,R_{n+1}) + O(1).
\]

Consequently, accumulating representations cannot substantially increase deficiency, but neither can it substantially decrease surplus. Furthermore, the possible changes are constrained by the algorithmic information introduced by the new representation. In particular,
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_R(e,R_n)-d_R(e,R_{n+1})
&\le K(r_{n+1}\mid R_n)+O(1),\\
s_R(e,R_{n+1})-s_R(e,R_n)
&\le K(r_{n+1}\mid R_n)+O(1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, a new representation cannot provide an unbounded improvement in deficiency without introducing a corresponding amount of algorithmic information. However, the presence of new information does not establish that this information is relevant to the entity. Moreover, cumulative representations retain information from earlier representations even when that information has subsequently been identified as incorrect or extraneous. The cumulative representation therefore characterizes the history of the information acquired, rather than necessarily the best representation available at a particular time.

These results illustrate the complementary roles of augmentation and reduction. Accumulation may address missing information, but improving miscoding may also require discarding information that was previously considered relevant.

\begin{remark}\emph{The problem of comparative miscoding}

The preceding properties do not provide a general procedure for determining whether one effective representation has less miscoding than another. The fundamental difficulty is that the relevance of information depends on the entity being represented. An additional component may contain previously missing information about the entity, or it may contain extraneous information unrelated to it. Likewise, removing information may eliminate surplus or increase deficiency. Even if two representations are known to concern the same entity, their algorithmic relationship alone does not generally determine which has lower miscoding. Different possible underlying entities may justify different evaluations of the same representations.

Consequently, the assumption of a common but unidentified entity is insufficient, by itself, to establish a practical ordering that is guaranteed to agree with theoretical miscoding. Nevertheless, scientific practice may provide additional evidence. Independent observations, reproducible experimental results, and constraints on admissible interpretations may help distinguish information relevant to the entity from information introduced by measurement errors or other sources.

For example, successive observations of an unidentified astronomical phenomenon may reveal increasingly reproducible patterns while eliminating previously unidentified instrumental artifacts. These changes may provide evidence that the representations are improving, even before the physical nature of the phenomenon is understood.

The challenge is to establish under which assumptions such evidence is sufficient to guarantee a decrease
in theoretical miscoding. This leads to the following open problem:

\begin{quote}
Given a time-ordered sequence of effective representations of a fixed but unidentified entity, under what conditions can we establish that a new representation has less theoretical miscoding than a previous one, without identifying the entity or computing their absolute miscoding values?
\end{quote}

A solution would require a comparison criterion based on information available in scientific practice, together with conditions establishing its relationship to theoretical deficiency and surplus. Such a criterion could depend on independent observations or structural properties of the representation process, but its validity would need to be justified mathematically. The theory developed in this chapter provides constraints on representational improvement, but does not yet establish a general solution to this problem. Determining whether scientific knowledge is improving before the entity under investigation has been identified therefore remains an open research direction.
\end{remark}

\section{Miscoding of Areas}
\label{sec:miscoding_areas}

The concept of miscoding can be extended from individual entities to research areas. Recall that an area $A\subseteq\mathcal E$ is a subset of entities, and that an $n$-fold representation encodes a finite collection of entities while preserving the individual representations.

Let $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\subseteq A$ be a nonempty finite subarea whose entities admit admissible representations. For each $e_i$, let $r_i\in\mathcal R_{e_i}$ be an extended representation. The corresponding $n$-fold representation is
\[
R=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle,
\]
where the brackets denote a fixed computable encoding from which each component can be recovered.

To extend miscoding to $F$, we consider all $n$-fold representations whose components are admissible representations of their respective entities.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:miscoding_areas}
Let $F=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\subseteq A$ be a nonempty finite subarea, and let $R=\langle r_1,\ldots,r_n\rangle$ be an $n$-fold representation with $r_i\in\mathcal R_{e_i}$.

Define the set of \emph{admissible reference representations} of $F$ as
\[
\bar{\mathcal R}_F = \left\{ \langle\bar r_1,\ldots,\bar r_n\rangle : 
\bar r_i\in\bar{\mathcal R}_{e_i}, \quad i=1,\ldots,n \right\}.
\]
The \emph{deficiency}\index{Deficiency of an area} and \emph{surplus}\index{Surplus of an area} of $R$ with respect to $F$ are defined as
\[
d_A(F,R) = \min_{\bar R\in\bar{\mathcal R}_F} K(\bar R\mid R)
\]
and
\[
s_A(F,R) = \min_{\bar R\in\bar{\mathcal R}_F} K(R\mid\bar R),
\]
respectively.

The \emph{miscoding}\index{Miscoding of an area} of $R$ with respect to $F$ is
\[
\mu_A(F,R) = \max\{d_A(F,R),s_A(F,R)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

These definitions preserve the interpretation of deficiency and surplus established for individual entities. Deficiency measures the information required to recover an admissible representation of the subarea, whereas surplus measures the information required to reconstruct the given representation from an admissible reference.

An important distinction is that area miscoding is not necessarily the sum of the miscoding of its constituent representations:
\[
\mu_A(F,R) \neq \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(e_i,r_i)
\]
in general. The conditional complexities are evaluated over the complete $n$-fold representation, allowing algorithmic dependencies between different entities to be taken into account. For example, representations of related biological species may share substantial genetic information, reducing the information required to reconstruct their representations jointly.

Finally, miscoding of an area representation must be distinguished from the completeness of the area itself. The quantity $\mu_A(F,R)$ evaluates the quality of the representation of the entities in $F$, but does not measure how many entities of $A$ remain unrepresented. Consequently, an $n$-fold representation may have negligible miscoding even when it covers only a small part of the research area.

\section*{References}

Misrepresentation or inaccuracies in scientific representation have significant implications for scientific discovery, technological progress, policymaking, and other domains. However, no book or paper explicitly addresses the topic of "incorrect representations in science" from the perspective adopted in this work. Here, we decompose the problem of scientific representation into two complementary subproblems: the representation of entities and the description of those representations.

\cite{suppes2002representation} provides a formal foundation for scientific representation and emphasizes the role of structure-preserving mappings; his analysis is crucial for understanding when and how representations fail to capture relevant features of phenomena. \cite{van1980scientific} introduces the concept of "constructive empiricism" and highlights the model-dependent nature of scientific representation. \cite{latour2013laboratory} offers an ethnographic study of how scientific representations are socially constructed—and, at times, distorted—in scientific practice.

Although the specific topic of incorrect representations has not been directly examined in this way, various researchers have addressed related concerns indirectly. Their discussions often focus on issues such as scientific fraud, the replication crisis, and the use of incorrect or misleading models. For example, \cite{ioannidis2005most} presents a widely cited empirical and philosophical analysis of how methodological biases, poor model design, and selective reporting contribute to unreliable or misleading scientific results.
